Two Philosophical Approaches to Physics

N. Vivian Pope

There are two absolutely distinct philosophical approaches to what is currently called 'physics', which is a peculiar modern distillate of what was formerly known as Natural Philosophy.

One of these distinct approaches can be described (although not quite correctly) as 'anthropocentric'. This stems originally from the phenomenalism of Berkeley, Hume and Mach. The other approach, the one that can be described as 'non-anthropocentric' is the one tacitly taken by the likes of Galileo and Newton. This approach presumes an observational standpoint of cosmic ubiquity, as of some all-pervading, universal being, or deity.

Einstein, to the chagrin of his relativistic tutor, Mach, conflated these two absolutely incommensurable approaches to physics by making the light by which we see things (i.e., 'anthropocentrically') travel in an absolute way at a constant speed relatively to a vacuum (witness his notorious Second Postulate). This oil-and-water mixture of anthropocentricity and cosmic ubiquity, so despised by Mach, is what has come to be known – or, rather, misknown – as 'Relativity'.

What POAMS does is to sort out or distinguish, philosophically, these two radically conflicting ways of thinking about light, the mixture of which, so far as POAMS is concerned, is the cause of all the current conceptual confusion. The winnowing-out process is described in full in The Eye of the Beholder: The Role of the Observer in Modern Physics.

With these philosophical delineations firmly in mind, we can now examine light. In the truly relativistic way of thinking, which can be called 'anthropocentric', there can be no question of the light which, in its full range of spectral manifestations is the whole world of information from which all empirical science necessarily starts, as having a 'velocity' separating objects as they are 'in themselves' from our observations of them. Nor can there be any objectively existing 'void', or 'space-in-itself', where there are no objects to be observed (pace Mach), far less as a vacuous conveyor of light in the form of either 'waves' or 'particles' – or both.

The only interpretation of c that can make any logical sense in this phenomenalist context is that on which Bondi and I concurred at about the same time (in the early 1950s); that is, purely as a dimensional constant, or 'conversion factor', relating conventional measures of distance and time in the way that the constant 39.37 inches to the metre interrelates those two different conventions of measurement without that number having any objective, far less cosmical, significance.

Now, it must be borne in mind that true relativity (à la Mach) and what one may call 'anthropocentricity' are by no means the same thing. There was a whole era of academic philosophy during which it was assumed that observer-centredness (as proposed by Berkeley) automatically implies 'subjective idealism' – or, at worst, solipsism – the view that nothing exists but oneself and that everything else is a dream. This nonsensical view was famously disposed of in the 1920s by the philosophers, Moore, Ryle, Wittgenstein et al. However, due to the artificial bifurcation in Education between Arts and Science that was bemoaned by the likes of C.P. Snow, scientists are not taught these things. This is why, innocently and unwittingly, many of today's scientists retain a vestigial dread of what they see as 'subjective idealism' which, in fact, is no such thing. And, even worse, there are many scientists, taught in the old way, who conflate this 'subjective idealism' with whatever it is that they imagine is 'Philosophy'.

For this discussion to remain on logical rails, a clear distinction has to be retained throughout between the two logically incommensurable theses described above, in the one of which (let's call it X) light is simply what you see, and in the other (call it Y) light is something that travels inscrutably in vacuo in the way Einstein's Second Postulate presumes. In other words, the two divergent approaches to science are what might be appropriately described as, respectively: X, which is to see the world from the actual viewpoint of a human eye (or camera or other optical instrument or set of such instruments); and Y, which is to adopt, in imagination, a standpoint of cosmic ubiquity or universal non-locality – what POAMS identifies as an incipient 'God's-Eye-View'.

Now a classic fallacy is to judge or try to refute a thesis X from the standpoint of an alternative thesis Y, Z or whatever. Such is the fallacy involved in judging or seeking to refute the POAMS non-velocity interpretation of c in terms of the classical 'velocity-of-light' interpretation of that constant. It is a fact that velocities, truly so called, obey the classical rule of vectorial composition, whereas this rule cannot be applied to a purely dimensional constant (which is to commit what, logically, is called a category mistake).

Questions, then, as to whether c is a 'proper speed' or a 'relative speed', whether it is a speed of expansion or contraction, whether it is the speed of 'waves' or of 'particles', whether its vectors do or do not linearly add, or the question of with respect to what reference the characteristic speed is specified … etc., make no more sense in the POAMS context than does the question 'Why is a mouse when it spins?' Such questions may well have relevance in the context of the Galilean, or God's-eye-view thesis Y, but not in that of the relativistic or Machian thesis X, in which c can in no sense whatsoever be regarded as a 'speed'.

But, of course, for the same reason that a critique of POAMS based on a thesis-Y interpretation of c cannot refute our X-based interpretation of that factor, neither can our POAMS, X-based interpretation of c refute a Y-based, two-step interpretation, which is certainly interesting in its own philosophical domain – as the POAMS thesis is in its own entirely different one. Only a final appeal to the criteria of logical consistency, correspondence with observation and conceptual economy (pace Ockham) can determine which of those two logical alternatives 'fits the bill'. Meanwhile, in order to make that eventual judgement a good one, the correct philosophical as well as theoretical distinctions must be observed.

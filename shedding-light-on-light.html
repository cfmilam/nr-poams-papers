Shedding Light on Light

by

Viv Pope

Relativity and Quantum Physics, notoriously, do not gel. But we can scarcely blame Nature for that. The fault must lie with ourselves.

Somehow, we have misinterpreted the natural facts from which the two theoretical divisions have arisen. Further experimentation, especially of the 'Gedanken' kind seems only to deepen the divide. The situation becomes more and more suggestive of what the philosopher Wittgenstein called a Scheinproblem, or problem of language.

What, then, are the facts in this case? They are that Relativity says there is a finite upper limit c on the speed of all distant interaction, namely, the speed of light, while Quantum Theory says there is no such limit, that in some cases the speed of distant interaction is infinite.

So, what does analytic philosophy have to say about that? It says that the contradiction between the two theories is no more than imaginary, that in distant interaction the finite and the infinite can go logically together.

The model it presents for understanding this is called the Cinematic Model. In this model, distant interaction is like that in a movie, where the objects in the film coexist and are connected in the instantaneous dimensions of each successive frame, or 'still', while all interactions between those objects take place in the time of the film.

A Physics parallel of this is to think of a balanced set of masses in an angular momentum relation at any instant as a quantum still, or frame – the sort of instantaneous connection that Quantum Theory deals with – and to think of the time-successions of these frames as the sort of interaction that Relativity deals with, all within the same natural scenario.

In any case, since speed is the quotient of distance by time, if that speed is expressed as the distance of a light-interaction, divided by the time of that interaction as measured by an ordinary observer, then its speed is the finite speed c. On the other hand, if the speed is expressed as that same distance divided by the intrinsic time (proper time) of the interaction itself, then the speed of that same interaction is instantaneous.

What nature seems to be telling us, then, according to this analysis, is that the limiting speed of relativistic interaction and the infinite speed of quantum interaction are no more than different-dimensional aspects of the same action, and that our conventional jargon which differentiates between 'photons' and 'superluminality' is just noise. The details of the Cinematic solution are explained in the paper.

The Cinematic Model of Action-at-a-Distance

N.V. POPE

Keele University, England and University of Wales, Swansea.

'Llys Alaw', 10 West End, Penclawdd, Swansea, U.K. SA4 3YX

Abstract

Instantaneous action-at-a-distance and the finite-speed of light are generally considered antithetical. However, it is well known that in relativistic physics light has both a finite and an infinite speed. Logically, then, we may think of the non-local quantum components of light-interaction as occurring in terms of the proper time of that interaction, in which the speed is instantaneous, and of the light signals, or waves, as a local process consisting of cinematic sequences of these 'quantum stills' in the relative or observer time of the action, in which the speed is finite.

This logical synthesis of locality and non-locality requires replacing the propagational field-electrodynamics of Faraday and Maxwell by the direct, non-field type of interaction of Weber and Helmholtz, as preferred by Feynman and Wheeler, Chubykalo and Smirnov-Rueda. It also requires replacing the traditional concepts of gravity and electric force by that of instantaneous balancing-moments between masses and spinning masses, respectively, in overall-conserved angular momentum relations.

1. Introduction

In his response to my last talk at the first Vigier Symposium at Toronto in 1995,¹ Professor Vigier declared that the theory I had described, the Pope-Osborne theory of relativistic action-at-a-distance² exactly resembled the subjective-idealist theory of Bishop Berkeley. And he added:

The thing has to be settled by the experimental physicist, and no philosophical a priori theory drawn from some philosophical heaven is going to tell us any hard facts about photons.

In my rejoinder I said that that was an a priori statement if ever there was one, and I contrasted his negative view of the relevance of philosophy to experimental physics by quoting what John Bell had said to me at CERN in 1975. 'Why,' Bell had asked, 'aren't you philosophers here in droves helping us to interpret the results of our experiments?' This, as I explained, was the reason I was here, a philosopher at a physics conference,

In this second Symposium two years later, what has become evident is the extent to which physics, as understood by the veterans of the subject, is now, as someone remarked, 'in a state of terminal decline.' Some of the younger members see this as a cause for excitement because it seems to them to portend an imminent conceptual breakthrough. Older members (including Vigier) reported how awesome it had been to associate with the physics giants of the past, and it was suggested that the great minds of the Solvay Conference era had, albeit inadvertently, done a great disservice to physics by making it seem to their followers that they were to be regarded as the only mediators and advocates of our knowledge of nature. It had got so, in physics, someone remarked, that one couldn't report that it was raining without genuflecting to the Officials of the Weather Bureau and quoting the works of its founding fathers.

However, as Vigier advised us in his speech at the Conference banquet: 'We no longer need to be frightened by those great figures', so that now is perhaps the time to do some more truly adventurous thinking, of the sort these giants had done but had failed to consummate. Accordingly, as a non-physicist –especially in that contemporary sense of 'physics' which has become little more than a desperate defence of the failing 'wave-particle' model– here, in response to Bell's plea, is my suggestion as to how the observational and experimental evidence gathered over the last three centuries might be re-interpreted so as to resolve the present difficulties. To those who keep saying to me 'but this is not physics', and that 'only more and more experimental evidence can solve the present problems,' I can only doff my cap and pass on. Otherwise, for anyone prepared to see physics for what it truly is, an offshoot of natural philosophy which, following an unprecedented measure of success, can now be seen to have failed, here, for what it is worth, is a suggestion as to what might have been an alternative and more promising philosophical route for natural history to have taken, had that been possible before all the present fuss about light and relativity.

2. A more logical, ahistorical route to relativity

Let us return, in imagination, to that point in history prior to all the present conceptual confusion over 'EPR' and so on, when as a result of his well-known observations of the transits of Jupiter's satellite Io, Olaus Römer reported his discovery of what we currently call the 'speed of light'. Here is how, logically, physics might have developed from there. Römer might have reported simply having discovered that astronomical measures of distance were also measures of time in the constant ratio of units c. This constant is what Herman Bondi has since described as a dimensional conversion-factor of the sort that is implicit in the decision of the Standards Institute in Paris to define the metre as equivalent to 3.3. nanoseconds.³

On the basis of this more parsimonious interpretation of c, the natural philosophers of the time might have reasoned as follows. While observations of stellar aberration, experiments with toothed wheels, revolving mirrors and so on, corroborate Römer's discovery and refine his value for c, subsequent evidence shows that although c has the dimensions of a velocity of motion it cannot really be a velocity of anything moving

because it is constant for all observers in relative motion, which they see as logically impossible for anything truly travelling. They therefore conclude that c is simply a space-time constant, manifesting itself in all situations wherever large-scale lengths and times are involved –as in collecting astronomical data, experimenting with light-signals in space, reflecting light-rays to and fro between mirrors and so on, in all those ways that are familiar to present students of optics.

Together with that, of course, goes all the experimental evidence of the periodical nature of those light signals. These our alternative physicists cannot regard as 'waves', because waves travel; and, as they have already decided, that is impossible for c, especially in a pure vacuum. Against that pre-modern conceptual background, let us consider how a natural philosopher of the time might have reasoned.

Let A and B be physical objects (masses) separated at some (Newtonian) instant in time by a distance s. Let that axis s be called the dimension of co-occurrence of A and B (because in that dimension, A and B exist together in that same instant) and let the time in which those co-occurrences succeed one another be called the dimension of co-exclusion (because in that dimension the co-occurrences take place not together but only one at a time). Now s is a measure in metres, and the metre is defined (presaging the modern Standards Institute definition) as equivalent to a time-measure of metre/c (= 3.3 nanoseconds). Accordingly, by writing s as s/c we express that distance measure in units of seconds. Let that time-length s/c in the co-occurrence dimension, when the time-length t in the co-exclusion dimension is zero, be denoted by t_R. Since these dimensions exclude each other they are orthogonal; and since they are both measured in the same units (i.e., seconds) those orthogonal axes are geometrical, analogous in temporal terms to ordinary length and breadth, as shown in Fig. 1. This means that in the motion of an object from A to B, shown in the figure, t_R is a geometrical (or, rather, geometro-temporal) resultant of the two orthogonal vector components of the motion, s/c and t which is, by Pythagoras:

$$t _ { \mathrm { R } } = [ ( s / c ) ^ { 2 } + t ^ { 2 } ] ^ { 1 / 2 }$$

Now this in itself is a sufficient expression of relativistic time-dilation. However, in this Pythagorean equation, s is equivalent to vtR, where v is the velocity of the body relative to an observer in Einsteinian Special Relativity. Substituting accordingly, we have $$t_{\mathrm{R}} = [(vt_{\mathrm{R}}/c)^2 + t^2]^{1/2} \tag{2}$$

This simplifies to

$$t_{\mathrm{R}} = [1 - (v^2/c^2)]^{-1/2} \tag{3}$$

which is immediately recognisable as Einstein's formula for the time-dilation of a relatively moving object. Significantly, however, the only difference between this Pythagorean formula and the modern, Einsteinian equivalent lies in its interpretation of the constant c. This, we are reminded, has the dimensions of a velocity, as in the standard formula, but in this ahistorical interpretation it is not the velocity of any travelling wave-particle or 'photon'. The quanta of which a light-signal consists are not 'photons' as in the customary conception of light-interaction (in Vigier's view, for instance, see above) but are auto-extended, non-local quantum extensions or co-occurrences analogous to 'stills' in cinematography. In other words, the 'velocity' -if such it can be called- of the quantum contact between A and B is the proper-time-instantaneous rate s/t, where t = 0; and the finite speed c, measured by Michelson et al, is the rate of cinematic succession of these non-local quantum connections (quantum 'stills') in the time tR of the light signal (or 'wave'), which is a statistical ensemble of these instantaneous quantum connections. That is to say, when the proper-time t of the quantum contact is zero, the cinematic speed of the signal or 'wave' is, from (3),

$$v = c [1 - (t/t_{\mathrm{R}})^2] = c \tag{4}$$

Logically, then, the solution to those conceptual difficulties which have all but halted progress in modern theoretical physics might have been a 'Copernican' switch. This is from thinking of light as either waves or particles (or as wave-particles) moving continuously in an underlying vacuum, to thinking of that whole space-time scenario as essentially 'cinematic'. The suggested switch is, in other words, from what might be called an 'engineering' or analog paradigm of fundamental physics to a digital or information-theoretical one. This, as we have seen, was dismissed by Vigier as 'a return to Berkeley'. However, as I pointed out, that does not rule out the information-theoretical approach a priori. If this philosophical theory has no relevance to physics, then how is it possible to derive Einstein's formula from it in such a simple and natural way? Such a result can scarcely be dismissed as 'idealistic', or trivial.

The trouble is, of course, that it is difficult to conceive of objects moving and influencing one another in a purely cinematic way, without definite causal connections of the sort we observe -or think we observe- between objects in 'the real world'. In ordinary experience, when one body influences another it is either by mechanical contact or by means of some continuous material agency. So we tend to extrapolate the ideas we have evolved on those ordinary mechanical-engineering levels of experience into the fundamental levels of quantum physics -which is a category mistake, like failing to distinguish a cinematic process from a cinematic scenario. This prompts us to imagine quantum light-interactions being mediated, in a manner analogous to that of sound, by 'waves' in some elastic intermediary like air or water, which then have to SHEDDING LIGHT ON LIGHT

be envisaged as some sort of underlying quasi-continuum called 'ether' or 'field'. Otherwise the sheer acausality of events at the quantum level seems too abstract to consider in contexts of experimental physics, which, of course, is what generates the reactionary view of 'Realism'.

Our pre-modern physicist, however, has not gone down that historical road, so he has avoided both 'subjective idealism' and its 'realistic' reaction.⁵ Contemplating formula (3) he might realise that the term $v^2$ in that formula is dimensionally equivalent to $e/m$, where $e$ is potential energy (twice the kinetic energy) and $m$ is mass. Substituting accordingly in (3) and expressing the equation in terms of $e$ he derives

$$e = mc^2[1 - (t/t_R)^2]$$

Now, he says, let $t$ in this formula be some fundamental non-zero time-measure, of which $t_R$ is some whole multiple $n$. The formula thus becomes:

$$e = mc^2[1 - (1/n^2)] \tag{5}$$

We may imagine, then, a Johannes Balmer of the time, while seeking to rationalise the distributions of the discrete lines of the hydrogen spectrum, chancing upon this formula and recognising it immediately as the solution to his problem. It would not take long for him to appreciate what the formula signifies, that the energies displayed on the spectrograph are obtained directly at the expense of some mass-coefficient of the light-source, in fractions depending on the value of the controlling integer $n$. For $n = 1$ the fraction $e/mc^2 = 0$, and for interim values, with $n_1$ as the initial (fixed) term and $n_2$ as the final (or running) term the formula becomes

$$e = mc^2[(1/n_1)^2 - (1/n_2)^2] \tag{6}$$

which is exactly the relation Balmer required.⁶ For $n_1 \to 1$ and $n_2 \to \infty$ in this formula we have, virtually, $e = mc^2$, which seems to signify that on macrophysical levels, mass and energy are interconvertible up to a limit where the ratio of units is $c^2$. And if energy and mass are interconvertible in that way (so reasons our pre-modern physicist), then the more energy we impart to a body in order to increase its relative velocity the more we increase its mass, so that in the limit $c$ of relative velocity the mass and the energy of a body are the same and no further acceleration of the mass is possible. By the same token, of course, macroscopic masses in kilograms, divided by $c^2$ should, under the right circumstances, revert to pure energy in the prodigious ratio of $9 \times 10^{16}$ joules for every kilogram –which might have excited military minds of the time.

What we have here, then, is no Berkeleyan idealism, since the objects concerned are masses, not minds, and the transactions between them, as expressed in the formulae, are physical energies, not 'ideas' or 'sense-data'. All that is missing, so far as the local realists are concerned, is their historically imagined 'travelling wave-particle' ('photon') to provide the hard and determinate causal connections they consider necessary between events at the quantum level and with which they would want to experiment. Otherwise, to think of cause as a matter of pure statistics, as in the second law of thermodynamics –especially as this applies in the science of information-technology– proves perfectly sufficient.⁷

But, it may sensibly be asked, if nothing travels between A and B in a light-contact, then in what sense are A and B connected? What, in other words, is the physical matrix of our 'quantum still'? The answer is that it is what is conventionally called 'gravitation' but which, in the Pope-Osborne interpretation is a redundant conception replaced by an expression of pure angular momentum.⁸ This angular momentum nexus is the still, in units of h/2π, and the units of the sequences of these stills are actions in units of h. This distinction between the instantaneity of the still and the time-retardedness of the stills' sequence is corroborated (notwithstanding that it is couched in the conventional terminology of 'gravitation' and 'electromagnetism'), by Van Flandern, in his 'instantaneous present position' argument. This is based upon evidence from aberration:

Gravitation and electromagnetism do not behave similarly in one critical way. In E-M, the field of a charge ... is not felt until after the light-time delay between the charges. By contrast, in the case of gravity, the position, velocity and acceleration of a source of gravity are felt by the target body in much less than the light-time between them.⁹

Light is thus conceived, in the Pope-Osborne theory, as a statistical, movie-type wave-like succession in cinematic space, of quantum shifts nh in a non-local or co-occurrent angular momentum matrix nh/2π. This is in some sense a field like that of historical electrodynamics, except that there is no underlying continuum and there are no 'electric charges' as sources and sinks of the propagated energy. In the P-O theory, therefore, there is no need of measures in coulombs. The 'charges' are re-interpreted as energies in joules, which are energies either of motion or potential for motion, thus dispensing with any need to think of the particles possessing a kind of 'static' metaphysically 'frizzing away' in the void. The different strengths of the various forces, such as 'gravitational force' and 'coulomb force' are thus explained as due to different convolutions ('wheels within wheels') of angular momentum, such as 'spins' or other forms of mechanical oscillation whose various non-local vector-orientations take the place of the conventional electromagnetic 'field-vectors' of Faraday and Maxwell. This use of 'Occam's razor' in shaving away so many historical conceptions should give no grounds for regret. For no matter how many unobservable entities we postulate as causal agents in between the movement of a particle at point A and another at point B (whether that intermediary agency be measured in volts, coulombs, farads, henrys, gauss or whatever), in the end it is a case simply of 'joules in, joules out'. And if that connection can be described directly, in angular momentum terms, without all the usual theoretical intermediaries, then, surely, so much the better.

The Pope-Osborne quantum angular momentum nexus is therefore, in effect, that of the direct-interaction (non-local) electrodynamics of Weber and Helmholtz, as encapsulated in the work of Chubykalo and Smirnov-Rueda.¹⁰ Just like the 'gravitational' and 'electrodynamical' connections of Van Flandern, their 'instantaneous Newton long-range and Faraday-Maxwell short-range interactions which cannot be reduced to each other'¹¹ correspond to the stills and stills-sequences, respectively, of our cinematic model. We should also mention that in the P-O theory, the problems regarding self-interaction, infinite contribution of self-energy and so on that typically afflict the usual 'electrodynamical' interpretations do not arise. In our theory the interactions are discrete and intransitive, with no underlying continuum. The only continuity is Heraclitan, with no 'Eleatic stasis' implications of the kind that caused Feynman, for example, to abandon the direct-action solution.¹²

3. Summary

It is well known that in relativistic physics there is a finite upper limit c to the speed that a body or any physical influence may travel in the time measured by an observer of the motion (i.e., the relative time), whereas in the time of the moving entity itself (the proper time) there is no such limit. In other words, in the proper time of a physical entity its speed between A and B is unlimited, whilst in relative time the speed of that same motion is the finite speed c.

Meanwhile, in modern quantum physics there are physical influences between bodies that have to take place immediately –that is to say, instantly, or at an infinite speed. In the words of Grunhaus, et al, 'while experiments are still open to criticism, it is generally accepted that local realism is untenable.' These researchers therefore assume that 'in nature there exist non-local correlations as predicted by quantum mechanics'.¹⁴ Others, such as Mandel, et al support this view of quantum non-locality on the basis that from experimental considerations, despite its usefulness in some cases, in others 'it is apparent that the concept of the photon as a localised particle travelling with the velocity c can be quite inappropriate and misleading.'¹⁵

The problem of the age, then, so far as physics is concerned is that relativity requires that no physical influence can travel faster than c, while quantum physics requires that there are influences that travel at an infinite or instantaneous speed. But how can this be a problem when in relativistic physics light interaction has both a finite and an infinite speed? The situation is very curious, since the obvious solution would seem to be to couch quantum physics in terms of the proper time of relativistic physics, in which light-speed is instantaneous, and couch ordinary physics in terms of the relative time in which that speed is finite. That should, logically, synthesise the two theories and dispose, at a stroke, of the apparent 'spookiness' and paradoxicality of action-at-a-distance.

This paper is an attempt to synthesise the two theories in what is felt to be that obviously logical and natural way. By disregarding the precedent of assuming that it is contradictory to think of light as having both a finite and an infinite speed, it is suggested that with a paradigm change of conception we may think of light as incorporating both of these speeds –that is, as finite-rate, movie-like sequences of instantaneous quantum connections analogous to the sequences of 'stills' in

cinematography. The fact that this greatly simplifies relativity by deriving Einstein from pure Pythagoras may be considered a bonus.

Predictably, then, according to the Pope-Osborne theory, any experiment designed to measure quantum interactions will measure 'speeds in excess of c'. However, the term 'superluminal' used to describe this phenomenon is, to say the least, confusing, since no quantum effect can travel 'faster than c' any more than the instantaneous connections between objects in a photographic still or frame can sensibly be said to 'travel faster' than the finite speed of the film. This applies to that whole genre of experiments that claim to have measured 'superluminal' action-speeds.¹⁶ In other words, according to our analysis, there is no real problem of action-at-a-distance. The 'problem' is no more than what the philosopher Wittgenstein calls a Scheinproblem, an imaginary problem created by carelessness in the use and creation of language –as, for instance, in the over-readiness of entrepreneurs of science to incorporate into scientific language such nonsensical terms as 'wave-particle' and 'superluminality'.

The Refutation of Solipsism

N. Vivian Pope

Or: The Death of Idealism. Response to Penny Gower, for circulation within NRIG.

Dear Penny,

You say that my piece 'Existence, Science and Language' is written very clearly but that the subject-matter is difficult to cope with. You say you'd appreciate it if I could write down for you the refutation of solipsism, as you suspect you need to 'immunise' yourself from it by getting your mind round the 'language business'. Well, let's see what we can do.

When I first began to understand Relativity it caused me to sail very close to solipsism. For those who may not know it, solipsism is the view that oneself is the sole observer, or perceiver, and everything else is mere illusion. In that catastrophic state of mind there is no assured distinction between dream and reality. Insanity becomes a real danger.

Historically, this was seen to be the logical consequence of Berkeley's dictum esse is percipi (to be is to be perceived), which makes the observer, or perceiver, the source and arbiter of everything. In my earlier studies of Einstein's Theory of Relativity, this Berkeleyan dictum seemed to be a logical consequence of that theory. The relativistic idea that all physical measures are centred on the observer, hence dependent on perspective, confounded me. I had always believed that things were just things, existing out there, absolutely, in themselves, regardless of whether I — or anyone else — perceived them or not. For me, matter had mass and duration in and of itself, regardless of how one 'looked at it'. Space had length-dimensions in-and-of-itself and a duration in-and-of-itself.

So it wouldn't be too much to say that I was offended by the idea that all time and space are relative to the observer, that bodies don't all endure everywhere at the same rate, that they don't have any length, mass or time except in relation to other objects in something like an anthropocentric reference-frame. As with you, thinking of this made me feel 'a bit funny' — to say the very least. Sometimes, I confess, I felt that I might become 'unhinged' by it. But, despite what my detractors might say, sanity eventually prevailed.

To cut a long story short, this was when I started my degree course in Philosophy at Bangor. One of the things I had to study in depth was the Empiricist, or phenomenalist tradition from Locke, via Berkeley, Hume and Kant, right down to their more modern successors, Ryle, Austin, Mach and the later Wittgenstein. This, I learned, was the tradition in which Einstein's Theory of Relativity had been conceived. In this tradition, the standard 'deistic' 'God's-eye-view' of material objects is replaced by a view of those physical things and events being, basically, phenomena, as interpreted in human perception and described in human language.

Throughout this period there persisted the niggling fear of solipsism as a consequence of these studies. However, in a 'road to Damascus' experience, similar to the one you describe in relation to light, I was suddenly disabused of this danger of solipsism. This came from the reading of a short essay by the Cambridge 'Commonsense Philosopher', G.E. Moore. Having to study this essay for my exams, I became intrigued with it. My fellow students were worried that I was spending an inordinate amount of time trying to come to grips with what Moore was saying, as opposed to just chanting it for the exams as they were content to do. And then, during a walk in the North Wales mountains, it suddenly 'hit me'.

The title of the essay was 'A Refutation of Idealism', and what it did was to analyse, logically, Berkeley's subjectivist (idealist) dictum 'To be is to be perceived'. Everyone up till then, and even subsequently, had taken this dictum as some kind of truism. Indeed, so had I up till that point. What Moore did (launching what later became known as the method of Logical, or Linguistic, Analysis) was to subject that statement of Berkeley's to logical scrutiny. 'What warrant,' Moore asked, 'does that statement have for being taken as true?'

The results of this analysis were, to say the least, surprising. Having eventually understood what Moore was saying, it sent me in a whole new philosophical direction, the one that has since become known as Normal Realism.

In what sense, then, Moore asks, can Berkeley's dictum be taken as true? Is 'to be' synonymous with 'to be perceived'? Obviously not, since in normal experience and language there are many things that obviously exist without being perceived. There are, for instance, deposits of coal beneath the earth's surface that we know are there, though they may never be unearthed. The same goes for the insides of such things as rocks, nuts and planets. So Berkeley's statement can't be regarded as true in that sense.

Is it true, then, Moore asks, in the sense of being true by definition — like 'A bachelor is an unmarried man'? Again, obviously not, since 'to be' and 'to be perceived' are different by definition.

Is it true, then, as a matter of observational/experimental fact? But how can you possibly observe, or determine by experiment, that nothing exists but what you observe? I can't actually observe that the road I'm driving along extends around the next bend, but if I didn't trust and believe that it does, then I wouldn't be able to drive. Indeed, I would probably be locked up somewhere, suffering from terminal solipsism!

In what sense, then, can Berkeley's dictum be taken as true? The answer Moore comes up with is: in no sense whatsoever. Berkeley's dictum is just plain false. Solipsism has no case to answer. It is no more than an intellectual bogey. For me, that bogey was exorcised by Moore and, as I say, it pointed me in the direction of Linguistic Analysis as a tool in disposing of many other bogeys and chimeras of modern physics.

Perhaps this will help you, Penny, to dispel the bogey of 'solipsism' which, surprisingly, still inhibits progress in the phenomenalist approach to relativistic and quantum physics. But physicists just don't study Arts subjects like Philosophy. If they did, then things might come together in the humanitarian way that is projected in my book. But I am sometimes made to feel that hell will freeze over before that happens to any significant social extent!

Best to all,
Viv.

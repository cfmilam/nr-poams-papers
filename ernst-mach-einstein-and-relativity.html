Ernst Mach, Einstein and Relativity

N. Vivian Pope

Ernst Mach, Einstein and Relativity In physics nowadays, we hear a lot about ‘relativity’. Motion is relative. Time is relative, length is relative, mass is relative and so on. What we don’t hear is: relative to what? The answer is: to the observer, what else. That takes us right back to Ernst Mach, the relativistic precursor and virtual mentor of Einstein, For Mach, relativity plainly meant ‘relative to the observer’. And that, in a word, is phenomenalism. For Mach, relativism and phenomenalism were practically synonymous. Mach thoroughly rejected the popular notion, of his era, that he was the forerunner of the Theory of Relativity. Actually, he repudiated Einsteinian Relativity. For him, all the fuss over ‘The Theory of Relativity’ was no more than an historic ’flash in the pan’. He thought that Einstein was confused. For Einstein, everything was relative except for light. Light was absolute; it was something travelling with a speed that was constant relative to the vacuum (or space) itself, the same for all observers, regardless of whether, in that space, they are stationary of moving with respect to one another. This could have made no sense to Mach. In a vacuum, to what can the ‘speed of light’ be measured, constant or otherwise? And what sort of ‘velocity’ is it that can be the same for all observers as well as to space itself? And as for the idea of light being ‘waves in vacuo’, what can possibly ‘wave’ in a vacuum? No wonder that, from its start, in 1905, the ‘Theory of Relativity’, hailed by the experts as a scientific masterpiece, was synonymous with obscurity in the minds of just about everyone at that time – and, indeed, still is with most people! For Mach the meaning of ‘relativity’ was perfectly straightforward. It meant ordinary everyday perspective, in which the World is as it actually appears to us individually, in terms of our sensations of sight, sound, scent, touch, ,,, etc., in all sectors of human experience, from ordinary commonsense to the levels of physics and astronomy. The presumption of some scientists that what they were dealing with was some overall sub-reality which is independent of all human perception and conception seemed to Mach the height of absurdity and human vanity, Once that presumptuous ‘God’s-eye-view’ form of ‘realism’ is dispensed with, the logical fall-back position is that of phenomenalism. Now phenomenalism is all too often confused with solipsism, the catastrophic idea that only oneself exists and that everything else is a figment of one’s own imagination, a kind of dream. That inference, however, is a fallacy. There is simply no way whatsoever of forming any concept of oneself other than in relation to other things and other people. That immense manifold of other things, in all the logical categories of animal, mineral and vegetable, is the World, or universe, so far as the phenomenalist is concerned. Apart from our natural physical limitations, we can, by our own efforts place either ourselves or our instruments in any part of that manifold and view it from there. (one thinks, for instance, of the close-up views of the surfaces of other planets, comets and so on that are obtained by such instruments as the Galileo space-telescope and NASA’s Pioneer and Voyager space-probes.) And what we do not experience ourselves we can experience at second hand, in conversation with others or in news reports and in books that these people may write of how things appear to them in other places and at other times. And that, no more nor less, is what is generally known as objectivity or objective reality. In that way, the whole nexus of informationally interconnected individual perspectives

becomes a whole community of perception, a framework, or grid-space of objectivised Cartesian dimensions, spread all over all geography and history. For whatever can we possibly know about or speak about, in any sector of knowledge or understanding other than in those terms? In Phenomenalism, then, nothing is omitted that is not already there in normal, everyday experience. In fact, that normal realism and phenomenalism are, for all ordinary and scientific purposes, one and the same. All that is missing from phenomenalism, as from normal realism, is the purely abstract, purely theoretical nether world of the classical ‘atomists’, the followers of Democritus, for whom there is no true reality in that normal, everyday world of colours, scents, sounds, scenes and so on, For these ironically self-styled ‘realists’ the only true reality lies in what they regard as the underlying material ‘sources’, or ‘causes’, of our perceptions, entities which we can never actually see or sense but only infer from the fact that we perceive. But the fallacy in that way of thinking, as it has always been, is that if these things are there by nothing more than inference, then since we have no way of comparing those allegedly hidden things with our perceptions of them, how can we possibly know that this inference is true? In other words, how can we ever be sure that there is anything there at all? There are atoms, of course, along with all sorts of other things with which empirical science deals. But, for the normal realist, these things are always the subjects of real sensory and instrumental predications of the sort that, for scientists and others in all the various areas of research, are just part and parcel of ordinary workaday reality. The problem always remains, however, of scientists finding the right sort of language in which to describe scientific discoveries, a language which gels logically, not only with their own findings on other occasions but also with the findings of other scientists in other areas of research, as recorded in libraries and in common language generally. This defines the branch of phenomenalism called Linguistic Analysis, which is the occupation of those philosophers who seek to keep the common language under continual scrutiny against logical incoherences and contradictions of the sort that create general corruption and confusion of common understanding. These analytic philosophers are sometimes disparagingly referred to by busy, jargon-mongering physicists in particular, as the ‘Language police’. This is insofar as with their insistence on logical rules and regulations they seek to prevent the specialists of science, with concern only for their own specialisms, from doing ‘what the hell they like’ with common language and understanding. There are two main modern traditions of phenomenalism or empiricism. On the one hand there is ‘English empiricism’, from Locke, Berkeley and Hume in that chronological order, and on the other hand is ‘Continental Phenomenalism’, after Hegel, Husserl and Heidegger. This latter takes the form called ‘phenomenologie, the emphasis of which is on psychologism and mind theories generally. By contrast, in English empiricism, which began in that same psychologistic mode, with John Locke George Berkeley and David Hume, the emphasis, after the physics-philosopher, Mach, has been on objective physics in general and on relativity and quantum theory in particular. The latest moves in this neo-Machian direction is a new paradigm of physics which has its roots in a correspondence connection between Viv Pope and Albert Einstein, in 1954. This led, in the 1960s to Pope’s introduction, at the University of South Wales, Swansea, to the Linguistic philosophy of Ludwig Wittgenstein, at the high point of that ‘Linguistic’ movement. Eventually, however, disenchanted with the anti-science emphasis of this particular form of neo-Machian philosophy, Pope left

and repaired to the University of North Wales, Bangor, where he conceived a new branch of neo-Machian, commonsense philosophy which he named Normal Realism. From there, Pope went on to develop, with his present colleague, Anthony Osborne, in an ongoing project at Keele University UK, the phenomenalist paradigm of NeoMachian Physics known as POAMS, the Pope-Osborne Angular momentum Synthesis. Although this retained some central elements of Linguisticism, it was emphatically pro-science as opposed to the anti-science of the Swansea group. The seminal idea, on which Normal Realism is based occurred to Pope at around the time of his contact with Einstein. His discovery, later corroborated by Sir Herman Bondi, was that Einstein’s notoriously abstruse Theory of Relativity could be deduced in a much simpler and no less effective way,. This was by taking the constant, c, which Einstein had conceived as ‘the velocity of light in vacuo’ – Einstein’s well-known Second Postulate – and reinterpreting it as just that, a constant, no more and no less, relating the two observer-projected dimensions of distance and time in the ratio of conventional units, three-hundred thousand kilometres to the second. This places objects that are observationally projected at kilometres of distance, a proportionate number of seconds, minutes, hours, years or whatever, earlier in time. This is without having to think of light winging its way through space, for centuries, perhaps, all alone in space, to meet up eventually with our eyes or other observing instruments. For instance, instead of having to think of the light leaving the sun in some inscrutable emission event that we can never know about until ‘the light reaches us’, we start with the information we perceive immediately and extrapolate backwards from that, via the constant c, the time difference between ourselves and the sun at its projected distance of 149,700.000,000,000 metres.This turns out to be 499 seconds, or 8.316 minutes, all results of which are exactly the same as are obtained in terms of the classical and extant interpretation of c as a ‘velocity’. . In this essentially phenomenalist re-interpretation, then, of c, nothing falls out of practical account except for the purported. purely theoretical intermediaries of classical 18th-20th century physics, such as the speculative ‘luminiferous ether’, ‘electromagnetic waves’, ‘photons’ and other entities of that metaphysical conception. So instead of the enigmatic ‘wave-particles’ called ‘photons’ and other in vacuo mediating entities separating physical objects from ourselves (in what is known as ‘Einstein separation’), we have direct and immediate informational bits – termed ‘phota’ – which have the physical dimensions of Planck’s action quantum h. In Normal Realism, these are the ultimate ‘pixels’, as it were, out of informational patterns and sequences of which, the phenomenal scenario is projected – somewhat in the way a viewer projects a TV drama out of the patterns and sequences of dots on a screen. In Normal Realism, these informational dots, or ‘pixels’, in the complete range of frequencies from deepest infra-red to utmost ultra-violet, are the successors of Mach’s original ‘sense-data’ out of which, for Mach, the World was observationally projected. This New Approach, or New Paradigm of phenomenalist physics is, almost certainly, the physics which, logically, Mach would have pursued if he had lived long enough. The specific mathematical-physics spin-off from this neo-Machian physicsphilosophy called Normal Realism, is the angular momentum synthesis, POAMS, which replaces Newtonian ‘gravitation’ with angular momentum as the cohesive principle of the phenomenal universe.

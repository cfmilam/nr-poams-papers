The Quest for Quantum Relativity

N. Vivian Pope — c. 2000

A Continuing Problem for 21st Century Science

Scientists of the nineteenth century were happy to accept that the basis of all physical quantities and qualities are the dimensions mass, length and time. In the twentieth century, scientists were surprised and disconcerted to discover that these dimensions are (a) relative and (b) quantised. That is to say, they are (a) the measures of things as they appear in observation, not in themselves; and (b) they form, at the most fundamental level of reduction, a dimensional package which cannot be reduced any further. This stopping-point is the same for all observers regardless of their relative differences and it defines the quantum of action known as Planck's constant, $h$. Once this level is reached, any further reduction in any one of the measure-components affects the others in such a way that their overall product remains the same, no matter what.

The Problem

The trouble is that the discovery of the relativity of physical dimensions and the discovery of their quantisation has split physical science in two, and no satisfactory way has yet been found of getting these two bits of science to make overall sense. The two factions are known as Relativity and Quantum Theory. The reason for the split is that Relativity demands that nothing can travel faster than light, whereas Quantum Theory demands that there are physical influences which are instantaneous — such as the immediacy of quantum action-jumps between distant places and the balance between bodies in free-orbital angular momentum relations. This presents the problem popularly known as 'EPR', from a paper presented in 1935, by Albert Einstein, Boris Podolski and Nathan Rosen, according to which the faster-than-light interactions required by the quantum theorists are forbidden by the Relativistic light-speed limitation.

The Underlying Fallacy

The root of the problem is taken to be that the experimental evidence of instantaneous action-at-a-distance, in gravitational and quantum interactions, for instance, and of relativistic time-separation in the case of light, are contradictory. This is a fallacy, because there is a model supplied by ordinary cinematography, which shows how instantaneity and time-delay can be conceived as complementary aspects of the same action. The proposed solution is therefore, with this cinematic model in mind, to re-examine the theoretical justification for Einstein's assumption that relativity demands that nothing can travel faster than light.

A Proposed 'Cinematic' Shift in Thinking About Light

In the classical way of thinking that was followed by Einstein, light is a single, self-contained action, separate entirely from other sorts of interactions such as, for instance, those we call 'gravitational'. It is a one-way, time-delayed process in which an atom sends luminous energy to travel in space until (if ever) it encounters some other atom which happens to be at the right distance and possesses the right resonance characteristics to be receptive.

By contrast, in the proposed cinematic conception, atomic interaction has two components. It is like the action in a movie, where one component of the action is the simultaneous presentation of a whole set of objects in a single photographic frame, or 'still'; the other component is a delayed-action connection between those objects in the time-sequences of the stills. In what the movie-maker understands by action, those two components, the instantaneous and the time-delayed, are combined, with no question of contradiction.

As applied to atomic interaction, a 'still', according to this analogy, is an electron and a proton which together form a single, reciprocally balanced, complete and irreducible cycle of angular momentum $h/2\pi$ (where $h$ is Planck's action-constant divided by $2\pi$). That is the static component. The action component is then an irreducible sequence-jump, in quantum units of $h$ (as opposed to $h/2\pi$) from one such static configuration (quantum still) to another, in which, in accordance with the law of overall conservation, the angular momentum lost by the one atom has to appear immediately in some other atom, somewhere else.

Without that other atom being present, the action-jump cannot take place. In other words, the interaction has to be with the other atom, not with 'space' as such, nor with any intervening 'field' or 'ether'. And since the quantum is irreducible, there can be no question of what happens to it en route from the one atom to the other, which may be anywhere in the universe, regardless of distance. Questions of the quantum's 'behaviour', its manner of conduction or even its 'existence' between the two atoms are therefore meaningless. It cannot be conceived as a 'wave', a 'particle', 'wave-particle', 'field-force', 'ether-disturbance' or whatever. It is veritably a quantum jump of pure instantaneous and unmediated action-at-a-distance, like the jumps which objects are seen to make between one place and another at the 'quantum' level of a cartoon film, when the film is slowed and shown frame-by-frame.

The time-delay in this composite cinematic process is therefore not that of individual stills (quanta) travelling from one place to another but is the delay in the movie-like time-sequences of these individually instantaneous quantum events. Thus, what we traditionally call 'light' is neither something that travels nor is it the instantaneous quantum events in themselves. It is the time-sequences of those instantaneous events.

The constant $c$, of 3.3 nanoseconds per metre, is then not the 'speed of light in space' but is simply the distance-time rate of those sequential events in the observer's frame. In this way, the 'speed-limit $c$' is no bar to instantaneous individual quantum events. Hence, Quantum Theory and Relativity are reconciled. This is the essence of the 'Quantum Relativity' towards which physics has been struggling. ■

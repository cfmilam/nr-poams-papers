A Philosophy for Science (Synopsis of Thesis)

Book: A Philosophy for Science by N. V. Pope (120,000 words, approx.) 1979

Synopsis of Thesis

Our customary ideas of space, time and matter have a long and illustrious history, culminating in that synthesis by Isaac Newton that is now referred to as 'classical' or 'commonsense' science. The trouble is that now, from the point of view of modern science, this classical or commonsense world-view has to be thought of as radically misconceived, whereas nothing of comparable appeal to commonsense has been provided by modern science to take its place. So commonsense remains firmly in that 'classical' groove, with a view of the world that modern science judges to be unreal or illusory, whereas from that same commonsense point of view the affairs of science are no more than a high-flown and esoteric kind of 'nonsense'. The polarisation of the two outlooks, therefore, is now more or less complete; and who is to say where, between them, lies true, objective reality?

But what, exactly, are 'space', 'time' and 'matter'? The answer is that they are primitive scientific concepts fundamental to the structure of human experience. In science, generally, these concepts form a triad of inter-definables. Matter, for instance, is that whose positions and changes in position with time define space, or distance (e.g., my arm which, by its movements can 'feel out' the space around me or which, by resisting the motion of your arm, signals the distance between you and me). Space is then what is defined by such operations (e.g., the distance between your arm and mine), while time is what is defined by the movements of matter in tracing out space (e.g., the movements of a hand across a clock-face or a planet across the sky). In science, of course, these commonplace operations have been refined by the introduction of fixed measuring-standards, like the mile and the metre for space; hours and seconds for time; and pounds, tons, kilogrammes, etc., for mass. However, in this gradual shift from ordinary commonplace 'rule of thumb' measuring towards complete instrumentalisation, with its consequent need for rationalising physical dimensions, nothing of the essentially humane character of science is lost.

What went wrong, in classical science, it seems, was not the increasing technicality of our methods and standards of measuring, but something of a deeper, more philosophical, nature connected with the very fundamentals of our physical understanding. From early in its history, classical science had somehow taken the world that commonsense had defined in terms of matter, space and time, removed it from the realm of reason and imagination where it had been conceived, and placed it instead (by an operation of that same imagination) in realms apart, where it became inaccessible to direct human experience. And there, forever, it remains, so far as science was concerned, absolute and autonomous. The only reason we are aware of its existence is because it sends out all sorts of energies, carried by various kinds of 'ether', to signal to us, belatedly, events that have already happened and existences that, in many cases, will already have ceased.

This absolutistic projection of human reason and imagination has always been a headache to philosophers. This, of course, is because as soon as we assume that we are cut off in this way from direct contact with physical reality and made dependant on secondary media like 'electric fields' and 'magnetic fields' —and the processes in these which we call 'light-waves,'radio-waves', etc.— then we forego all logical justification for making any confident assertions as to the 'nature' of this remote, extra-sensory, 'reality'. People like Descartes, Locke and Hume were thus able to create consternation by raising serious doubts as to whether this 'external physical reality' really existed.

None of these difficulties, however, was allowed to trouble the more practically minded natural philosophers who, under their newly-adopted name of 'scientist' carried on regardless. And so successful did they become, in a purely practical kind of way, that they gradually won over commonsense into making this same tacit, metaphysical, projection. That is why it is now more or less commonplace to assume that the real physical world is something essentially 'out there', a great cosmical juggernaut with its own absolute extension and duration, consisting of fast-moving atoms, each with its own characteristic mass and other physical properties which are what they are irrespective of how we perceive them, and exerting on one another, autonomously, all sorts of attractive and repulsive

'forces' like 'gravitation', 'electric force', 'magnetic force' and 'nuclear force'. Indeed, many modern scientists display their adherence to this same metaphysical mode of thinking when they conceive it their business, by means of bigger and ever more expensive bits of machinery, simply to 'puncture' these 'fields' and generally 'smash away' at the barrier which, in their imaginations, their predecessors erected between ourselves and these 'hidden depths' of nature.

In modern philosophy, however, we find the gradual casting-off of this ancient and mystical mode of thought and the institution, in its place, of a much more direct and sensible kind of realism. The real world, in these modern physical terms, is not some remote and metaphysical 'underlying reality' to be 'hacked away' in any refined or philistine fashion. It is what men actually perceive it to be within the framework supplied by common language. To 'understand nature' is then simply to be able to use and develop ordinary physical language (i.e., describe physical operations, etc.) in the most positive and economical (least metaphysical) kind of way. What is least desirable, from this point of view, is any useless proliferation of language, especially the kind that creates unnecessary problems and generates intellectual confusion.

However, it is not only in philosophy that such developments have occurred. The same trend is reflected in the rise of scientific positivism and so-called 'operationalism', after Ernst Mach and P. W. Bridgman. Allied to these is that development in which the same observer-involved realism appears under the name of 'relativity'. In relativity, the old absolute and autonomous world of space, time and matter disappears and is replaced by a community of observers, each defining the reality of the commonly observed situation in his own peculiar way —indeed, with the only truly 'common' characteristics of the situation emerging as a function of their common mathematical language.

The relativistic rejection of the old and deterministic 'block universe' of absolute space and time is also reflected in what is known as 'indeterminacy' and 'quantum theory'. These conceive the world, not as a single, monolithic, space extending to infinity and enduring in a single and inexorable time, but simply as a collection of relativistic and elemental (i.e., finite) events, of very

small mass, length and duration. The interrelations between these obey no cosmic- al, mechanical, laws but only those laws provided by the language of probability- statistics. Such events, far from being conceived as the secondary effects of things that have already happened in some extended physical (metaphysical) sub- stratum, are the actual events of which the world consists, being manifest both in reality and in (either direct or instrumental) observation all in one and the same instance.

The problem with which we are faced nowadays, however, is that although there has undoubtedly occurred this general shift in philosophy, away from classical absolutism towards a new relative realism, the results, philosophically speaking, are confused. In fact, nothing is more evident than that the efforts of the phil- osophers and the scientists who have participated in this advance have not, so far, been properly coordinated. There are, for instance, still scientists who insist (as Einstein did, in fact) that relativity, indeterminacy and probability are no more than illusory aspects of an underlying absolute continuum known in its entirety only to God. What the properties of such a 'universe' would be, however, relative to all possible points of observation and states of motion, at all instants and at all times, it is pointless to speculate upon. Certainly, these could not be what we customarily conceive as physical properties. If any- thing, they are metaphysical in the manner which modern philosophy rejects.

Many of our so-called 'physicists' are therefore, in a definite sense, meta- physicians: high priests, in effect, of a modern and implicit kind of deism. Nor does speaking of 'modern philosophy' indicate any clear consensus of ideas as to what constitutes physical reality. We are therefore, it seems, a long way yet from that evolutionary stage in commonsense understanding where a re- combining of philosophy and science becomes inevitable. Meanwhile, what typifies this present era is the variety of attitudes that can be adopted, in all sectors of human experience, towards 'truth' and 'reality' which, by and large, have be- come matters of arbitrary choice or conventional doctrine. And what makes any progress difficult is the pressure exerted by those who declare that they are happy with this 'glorious intellectual freedom' and would not have it otherwise.

The trouble with such 'freedom', however, is that in the end it tends to become a regime, a monopoly imposed by whoever, and for whatever reason, can command the greatest 'pull', who can say what he likes and somehow get away with it. In a more tightly ordered, more rational, system such sophistry is reduced, increasing the balance of freedom in favour of the more conscientious and non-political reasoner. We should not be surprised, therefore, if we find that any philosophical attempt to advance reason and rationality meets with political opposition. But in suggesting a rational coordination of philosophy and science one is far from posing any real 'threat to intellectual freedom', and if there are any who conceive it so then they must be, surely, sophists of the kind indicated above, for no-one else has anything to fear from the prospect of a systematic approach to truth and reality.

And the truth is that from a philosophical as from the commonsense point of view there are aspects of modern physics which are, quite plainly, nonsense. One of the major troubles with relativity, for instance, is that having disposed, ostensibly, of the classical myth of absolutism, that concept nevertheless remains implicit in the formulation of the theory. The events of the physical world are still conceived, in other words, as occurring essentially 'out there', in a kind of cosmical and continuous 'space-time', so that by the time we see them they have already happened, giving us no part whatever to play in their occurrence. This notion of events occurring autonomously and independently, and relayed to us by accident in the form of 'incident light' (radio-waves or whatever) does not fit together logically with concepts like fundamental relativity and indeterminacy. Something is therefore seriously wrong with relativity theory insofar as it bases itself on 'light-rays', conceived after the fashion of classical science as propagated across an absolute and pre-existing space by the agency of interacting 'fields' or 'ethers' between one absolute assignment and another. This excruciating cacophony of old and new ideas is no doubt responsible, in one way or another, for the fact that even now, after nearly three quarters of a century, there remains no sign of the theory being acceptable to commonsense. On the issue of relativity, commonsense and science live completely apart.

It says something, however, for the persuasiveness of its exponents that to criticise this most obvious kind of nonsense has become the modern heresy. Some exponents of the theory counter criticism by parading its practical results, like the fact of time-dilation in accelerated particles or the conversion from mass to energy that takes place in the hydrogen bomb. This, of course, is a fallacy, because it may well be possible that these same practical consequences (and more) follow from other theories which we have so far not been allowed to try, ones which, notwithstanding their initial novelty, might be potentially more acceptable to commonsense. Perhaps it is time, therefore, to assert that from a democratic point of view present relativity theory is unacceptable, and that some new and more sensible way of interpreting the same results should now be explored.

Anyway, whether or not it might be politic to attempt such a thing is an academic question so far as this thesis is concerned, for that is precisely what the thesis has attempted. What it does is to undertake a wholesale and systematic revision of the concepts 'length', 'time' and 'mass', from the standpoint of modern philosophical realism. This, it is hoped, brings into prominence that systematic shift in general philosophy which the untidy and eclectic language of modern science tends to conceal.

The thesis begins by taking a certain physical concept, namely, that of 'light', and purging it of all the old absolutistic associations. On this new basis is then prepared the ground for a thoroughly realistic re-interpretation of modern relativity. This new theory has much the same mathematical consequences as the old, except that it dispenses with the vastly troublesome concept of 'light-velocity' which is so central to the conventional theory. It also dispenses in the same way with all sorts of 'etherial' conceptions, such as those of 'electrical', 'gravitational' and other 'field-forces' dividing the observer from entities absolutely possessed of things like 'charge' and 'intrinsic spin'.

Instead, the event, the distance and the time become relative extrapolations, or projections, by different observers from experiences coordinated at these points and couched essentially in human language. From this pluriverse, or polyverse, of reciprocating observations, via the medium of the common language, is then constructed a single, objective, yet immanent, set of dimensional interrelations which, in common, the observers call 'the world'. And so far as this thesis is concerned, behind that pluriverse of agents, residing in common language, there is no monolithic 'universe' of 'space', of 'space-time', of 'fields' or 'ethers', that it makes sense to consider. All interactions are quantised (i.e., discontinuous) and thoroughly relative. And whatever takes place on any macroscopic scale is governed, if at all, by phenomenological laws, of which the one approaching nearest to the old mechanistic or deterministic ideal is that of probability.

Although the thesis thus presented expresses ideas that are more or less commonplace in certain areas of science and philosophy, the consequences of bringing them together in this fashion, if properly appreciated, might seem alarming. Indeed, one is driven to think, sometimes, that this is the reason why such an obviously valid undertaking seems almost to have been studiously avoided. Nevertheless, these (or, if not, then something very much like them) are the logical consequences of putting those ideas together. If there is any heresy committed by this thesis, therefore, it is that by exploring their full logical consequences it gives to those humanistic trends in modern science and philosophy a prominence which might be considered indecent so soon after the demise of classical absolutism. Are we so soon to forsake the memory of that 'Mother Nature' from whose bosom we were so untimely torn? Perhaps it is just too much self-responsibility for mankind to take on just now!

But — 'in for a penny, in for a pound!': Perhaps it is now high time man measured up to his responsibilities!

N. V. Pope,

Autumn, 1979

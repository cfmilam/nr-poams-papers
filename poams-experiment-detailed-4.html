N. Vivian Pope & Anthony D. Osborne

1. The Orbital Angular Momentum of Spinning Bodies

POAMS is the acronym for the Pope-Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis. Its principals are N. Vivian (Viv) Pope and Anthony D. Osborne, and the Synthesis is explained as follows. In Newton’s seventeenth century description of orbital motion no significant account was taken of the spin angular momentum of orbiting bodies.

According to Newtonian theory, orbiting bodies are kept in orbit by a hypothetical in vacuo ‘gravitational’ attraction which opposes their assumed tendency to fly off in straight lines, the so-called ‘centrifugal force’. In this present twenty-first century, POAMS proposes a long overdue updating of that Newtonian theory which is still used today, by NASA, for instance in calculating the trajectories of their space-probes and by astronomers in calculating the accumulated masses of the spiral galaxies.

Aside from questions of Relativity, the more basic modification POAMS offers is, in accordance with the law of angular momentum conservation, to include any spin angular momentum an orbiting body might have in its overall total angular momentum. First, however, let us be clear what we understand by angular momentum.

Angular momentum is a quantity of rotational motion in given direction. The directions are called vectors which, like the quantity, or magnitude, of the motion, are also conserved. A prime example of angular momentum is the common gyroscope. Once set in motion, barring the effect of outside forces, the gyroscopic disk typically maintains both the magnitude of its angular momentum and its directional (i.e., vector) orientation.

This is not with regard to the earth nor any other planet – not even the sun – but with respect to the universe of matter in general. This is known in physics as Mach’s Principle, according to which, all stars, planets, spiral galaxies and other bodies, from the galaxies to spinning microphysical particles are part of an overall conserved nexus of interconnected motions.

In this nexus, by the principle of conservation, any change in angular momentum (torque) in one place instantly perturbs the motion of some other body, or bodies, elsewhere. We say ‘instantly’ because there is no way in which conservation can be suspended during the time it would take for the influence to ‘travel’ in any way – for perhaps millennia – in a metaphysical limbo between one perturbation event and another.

Such distant interactions must therefore take place at the fastest ‘speed’ possible. This is at the ‘speed of light’, which, on the level of quantum interaction is proper-time instantaneous. (Any idea of ‘superluminality’, or ‘faster than light’ interaction is nonsense, since that would imply a proper time period running backwards.

All this is dealt with in the POAMS literature. This present dissertation aims to find experimental evidence of this holistic interconnection between distance-separated bodies in angular momentum relations. What, then, is angular momentum? Angular momentum is a vectorial quantity – that is, basically, an amount, or magnitude of motion in a given direction.

We all know what momentum is. It is a quantity of motion, in a straight line. Angular momentum (symbol L) is simply that straight-line momentum mv on a radius r, hence: angular momentum = $L = mvr$

where m (in this present case) is the mass of a freely orbiting body – such as, say, a space-probe in solar orbit – v is its orbital velocity and r is orbit radius, which we shall assume is circular for the sake of simplicity. What, then, makes angular momentum a vector quantity? Our answer is as follows. A spinning wheel has angular momentum. Imagine, then, a wheel of, say, one kilogram mass, standing stationary on the earth’s equator. Now the earth rotates, we are told, at a surface speed of 465.1 metres per second, and the wheel, situated on that spot, lies at a distance of 6.371e 6 metres from the earth’s centre. In this case, the diurnal angular momentum of the stationary wheel will be, roughly, $L = mvr$  2,900,000,000 kg m s⁻¹.

Now imagine that wheel rolling along the equator in a direction due West. Since the earth spins in the opposite – that is, the easterly – direction, the spin angular momentum of the wheel is subtracted from that of the earth. On the other hand, if the wheel rolls along the equator in a direction due East, then its spin angular momentum is added to that of the earth. This is intuitively obvious. Let us now take the case of the wheel rolling in the direction due north, that is, at an angle of 90 degrees to the direction of the earth’s spin. In that northerly direction, there will, obviously, be no addition or subtraction of the wheel’s angular momentum from that of the earth, and the same goes for the wheel rolling in the direction south. In other words, the magnitudes of the angular momenta of the earth and the wheel add or subtract according to the angles of direction (vectors) of the motion . This is from a maximum addition in the case of the wheel rolling in the same direction as the earth (east), to a minimum in the case of the wheel rolling

at right angles to the earth’s spin, in the direction north or south. This rises to a maximum s ubtraction at 180 degrees in the direction west, opposite to that of the earth’s spin. Between these full 360 degrees of directional variation, the precise amounts of addition and subtraction of the vector magnitudes are calculable according to the well-known principle of the Composition of Vectors. Intuitively obvious, also, is that even where opposite spin-vectors of the wheel and the earth may sum to zero, this does not stop the wheel or the earth from spinning. They keep spinning, albeit in opposite directions with whatever amount of angular momentum they possessed initially. This means that the total amount, or magnitude of the overall angular momentum remains the same regardless of the orientations of the vectors. Moreover, what is so for the conservation of angular momentum of the wheel rolling on the earth’s surface has to be the same for free-orbiting bodies. Since this is so, it is plain that where both the amount and direction of angular momentum are conserved, the more there is of spin angular momentum the less there has to be of orbital angular momentum and vice versa. This implies a proportioning or partitioning, of angular momentum as depicted schematically in the diagrams below.

The main thing to note about this compartmentalisation of spin and orbital angular momentum is that in all the instances shown in the diagrams, from Figs. 2 to 4, the effect of the spin, regardless of direction, is to reduce the orbital angular momentum to some extent or other, hence the radius of orbit of the spinning body.

In none of these instances is the orbit radius increased. Plainly, then, experiment should be designed to detect the largest measure of decrease in the orbit radius due to spin. This is in the situation depicted in Fig. 4. that is, with a gyroscopic disk spinning in the same plane as that of the earth’s rotation, just above the equator and counter to that rotation, as if rolling westwards, like the wheel whose angular momentum is most subtracted from that of its diurnal rotation.

Evidence for this predicted effect of including spin in the angular momentum of orbiting bodies may be taken as the so-called ‘Pioneer anomaly’, which is that NASA’s spinning space-probes tend, inexplicably, to orbit the sun nearer than they should according to standard gravity-theory.

Further evidence is provided by the fact that the spins of the spiral galaxies are faster than Newtonian ‘gravity’ theory predicts, which is just another aspect of that same anomaly. These evidences for the validity of the POAMS predictions, speak for themselves and are dealt with elsewhere [i].Our present aim is to add to this astronomical evidence some down-to-earth experimental evidence of this same anomalous effect of rotational motion – between our earth, say, and a gyroscope spinning in a frame fixed to the ground near the equator.

From what we have seen so far, the effect of spin is to shorten orbital radii. This leads us to expect that on the earth’s surface there should be increases in weight for spinning bodies as they seek to orbit closer towards the earth’s centre. Many researchers, of course, have made similar predictions of changes in weight but for all sorts of different and unconnected reasons.

For example, Hideo Kawasaki and his team at Tohoku university, Japan, have carried out weight-change experiments with extremely fast-spinning bodies. Unfortunately, the results of these Japanese experiments are altogether equivocal. They have no discernible theoretical basis and all attempts to replicate them have failed.

From the POAMS point of view, this failure seems inevitable since care has not been taken to ensure correct vector orientation. For instance, Tokai University is situated at about 45 degrees latitude north of the equator– roughly the same as the UK.

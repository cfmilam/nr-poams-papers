No matter which way their light-speed-measuring apparatus was moving, in the earth's orbit around the sun and in its rotation with respect to the fixed stars, that 'speed of light' remained the same. Nonsensical though that consequence was, it was nevertheless taken as a'proven fact of nature', that 'light has a constant speed c in space, which is the same in all reference-frames whether relatively moving or stationary. And it was out of that bewildering 'speed of light' interpretation of c that the current Theory of Relativity sprang. The way it happened was this. In pre-Einsteinian (since called 'classical') physics the laws of mechanics were well understood by everyone. What they stated was simply that from the standpoint ol say, a passenger in a moving railway carriage, the motions of other objects, such as the rails, other trains, people walking along corridors, platforms and so on, at different speeds and in different directions, all add and subtract in a simple, commonsense way. What Einstein did, in effect, was, on the basis of his esoteric studies of 'electrodynamics', to cram into this corlmonsense 'round hole' of ordinary perception the mystifying'square peg'of Michelson and Morley's discovery of the constancy of c for all observers in those situations that have been described. All that was needed, so far as Einstein was concerned, was to make compensatory mathematical adjustments to our ordinary ways of meazuring things and to the laws of mechanics generally, so as to make it all fit what he regarded as the 'fact' that light 'travels' at a speed which is constant with regard to the train, the tracks ald whatever. It was like straightening a picture-frame by tilting the whole house. However, this Draconian adjustment worked (and how could it possibly not have!). It was lauded Einstein's contemporaries as a bold and imaginative, 'most elegant' mathematical solution. However, by this new, 'positive' method of doing physics (of simply accepting the appearances and ignoring the logic) signified, from then on, a complete departure from commonsense, on which deviant track Modern Physics has continued ever since.

But why was this abandonment of commonsense necessary? There was a definite logical alternative available which, for some strange unknown reason, was never considered. This was, as it were, to 'tilt the picture rather than the house'; that is, to retain the commonsense laws of mechanics and reexamine the historical justification for having interpreted the constant c as a 'speed'. Unquestionably, c is a distance divided by a time, but this does not mean that all distances divided by time are speeds, and it is a howler of a fallacy to assume that it does (like saying that because all Mexicans are human, all humans are Mexicans). This is why the well-known scientist, Herman Bondi preferred to interpret c as no more than a dimensionql constqnt relating distance-measures in metres and time-measures in seconds in the way that was discovered, originally, by Romer, but without having to think of that constant as a'speed'. Let us start at the basis of all time-measure, which is on the level of atoms. Atoms are the ultimate clocks, of which all physical objects including ourselves and all other time-measuring devices are made. Let's also assume that there is no time other than what consists, basically, of those atomic events (no time in the vacuum, for instance). Now those atoms di;play themselves, in the light-spectrum, as momentary flashes of illumination called quanta whic[ in large numbers, form bar-codeJike arrangements like that of the rainbow. From these informational patterns of illumination can be read the chemical compositions, dispositions and duration-rates, not only of astronomical objects such as the sun and the stars but of substances in general. When an object is travelling away from us we see its whole bar-code shifted away from its known standard position, towards the red. (Ihat is to say, consistently with R6mer's discovery, all the periods of the atoms are slowed, or reddened.). And when the object moves towards us the bar-code is shifted the opposite way. (The periods of its atoms are relatively quickened, or blued.) For our purposes, then, a spectroscope may be regarded as an instrument for c.omparing the timerates of the atoms of distant objects with those of our own clocks and other time-measuring devices. It then follows, as a matter of straightforward accounting that, as Einstein was the flrst to demonstrate (albeit in his

extremely over-intellectualised way), time is not the same for everything and everyone everywhere, as scientists had hitherto assumed. For instance, let Jack and his twin sister, Jill, be astronauts floating together in deep space. Now suppose that one or other of the twins, let's say Jack, with a burst of rocket power, shoots away into space, leaving his sister behind. Tkoughout this operation, we may suppose that each of the twins has a spectroscope continually trained on the other. From what has been said so far, we know that in their spectroscopic views of each other, each will see the periods of the other's atoms slowed - or, in spectroscopic terms, shifted towards the red. Let's say, then, that after coasting away for a while, Jack, at some mutually agreed, prearranged time as read by his clocks, turns his rocket around and with another burst of his engine heads back towards his sister, at the same speed relative to her on this return journey as on his outward one. We know that at some point in this turning manoeuwe he will see his spectrographic readings of his sister's atomic periods change from red-shifted (slowed) to blue-shifted (quickened).

Meanwhile, this changeover in Jack's spectroscope from counting slowed to quickened atomic periods from Jill is an event which occurs for Jill, not immediately, as it does for Jack, but only after a time which Qruce Romer) is one second for every 300 thousand kilometres of distance between her and that event. Now because these respective motions are, up to that event, perfectly symmetrical, the distance by which Jill will be separated from Jack, at that point, will be the same as that by which Jack is separated from Jill. Just for the sake of argument, let the distance between them be 300 thousand kilometres. This means that for Jill, that changeover-event in Jack's spectroscope occurs one second later for her, according to her clocks than it does for Jack according to his. It follows, therefore, that in the interim between Jack's departure and return, which is symmetrical in all other respects, the asymmetry in the time of the ehangeover from reading redshift to blueshift means that the overall proportion of slowed to quickened atomic periods from Jack, as read by Jill, will be greaterthan that of Jill's atomic periods as read by Jack. Now that is the crux of the whole mind-bending issue of relativity, the difficulty of which no amount of simplifying of the mathematics may alleviate. It was never the maths that made Einstein's discovery so diffrcult and revolutionary. It was that it goes against our 'instinctive' feeling that no matter how any number of observers may move relatively to one another, and regardless of whatever that motion does to them and their atomic clocks, when they all arrive back on the same spot their ageing-rates, in the interim will have been the same. This is what made Einstein's discovery so revolutionary, namely, in revealing that there is no overall universal ageing-rate for everything, as science had previously assumed. Nor is it any let-out that these relative staggerings ofliving-rates are usually too small to be noticed in even our fastest journeys in trains, ships and planes. Microphysicists are familiar with bits of matter that travel so fast that it becomes essential to take these time-dilational effects into account in designing their apparatuses and making physical measurements. Besides, even on ordinary levels of motion these effects have been measured. Atomic clocks of the sort described in the 'twins' example have confirmed that when one of them remains at an airport and another is sent flying in all directions around the world, when the two return together they show time-differences ofjust the sort that have been described.tiil

Why, then, when this discovery was announced, did it create such a furore, and why does it continue to do, even nowadays, in the burgeoning 'dissident' sectors of science? One reason is that the extended philosophical implications of this radical change in our ideas about time are so profound as to be disturbing to anyone who may be even the least bit prepared to think about it. For instance, what is called

the mass of a body is, by definition, its reluctance to move or to change its motion in response to applied energy. But the slowing of the time of a body with increasing relative speed means that there are diminishing returns on the energy supplied to increase the motion, which entails a gradual increase in the mass of the accelerated body in proportion to the applied energy. In other words, motion stores energt in the /orm af mass, from which it follows that energy is mass and mass is energy, according to the well-

known nuclear-physics formula 'E : $mc^2$'. Every material object, therefore, even when apparently motionless, is implicitly, a package of energy proportional to its mass, the mass-energy being, in that case, the motion-energy of the body's internal constituents. It was this logical train of reasoning, following from the fact of the dilation and contraction of time due to relative motion, that led scientists to the discovery of how to unlock matter to release its prodigious store of nuclear energy.

However, even scientists who accept these revolutionary consequences of time-dilation and apply them for the practical purposes of physics and technology, cop out from following their fuIl philosophical implications. A problem thus consigned to philosophical limbo is that of how people iike Jack and Jill, in the 'twins' example, whose consciousnesses occupy no more than a split second of time, can maintain a lifelong informational contact when their bodies age at different rates. This problem of the accumulated time-staggering of consciousnesses due to relative motion has therefore, to this writer's knowledge, never tiiil. This makes the existence of any one thing, be it a brick, a brain or whatever, essentially a set of interrelations which the thing has with other bits of matter - which, of course, underlines the meaning of 'relativity'. The universe thus becomes an indeterminate, relativistic, interaction-communicational whole, in the way that was envisaged by the nineteenth-century philosopher-scientist Emst Mach, Einstein's Philosophy teacher who regarded his

To the question, then, of whether there is any advance on 'Relativity', the answer has to be: not as long as it remains cloaked in its original, 1905 'velocity of light' esotery This obscures the whole issue of relativism as Mach understood it. If commonsense is ever to accommodate itself to the fact of time-

staggering due to motion and its train of logical implications for society generally, then it must be presented, not \vith fabricated mysteries about 'light-velocity' but with the two clearly delineated philosophical alternatives in ways of thinking about time.Is nature's time the same everywhere, as in the absolutistic 'God's-eye-view' tradition of thought stretching from Democritus vla Descartes and Galileo to Newton, or does it vary with relative motion as in the observationist tradition extending from Berkeley and Hume, to Mach via Kant? As philosophical foundations for science and society generally, these are as irreconcilable as, at the point of transition from Mediaval to Modem Science, were the earth-centred and sun-centred views of planetary motion. In our present state of suspended transition between the one scientific philosophy and the other we are like pre-Copernican astronomers contemplating astronautics. However, if only it could be generally realised that Einstein held no monopoly on relativity, that the theory remains logically negotiable, as all truly scientific theories must, then undoubtedly, the century-long burden of obscurity that Einsteinian 'Relativity' has imposed on us might be lifted, leaving progress in what was once called Natural Philosophy to be resumed. r

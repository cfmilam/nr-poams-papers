What Is Philosophy?

What is philosophy? Philosophy means, literally, 'love of wisdom' But what is wisdom? True wisdom, it has been said, is 'to know what is best worth knowing and to do what is best worth doing'. Philosophy, then, according to these definitions, is the subject that is essentially concerned with true knowledge and right action.

But who decides the true knowledge and the right action? Well, the question is not so much who as what: and what decides these things is experience. Is philosophy, then, just a posh name for experience? Some philosophers have tried to identify the subject with experience. These are called Empiricists. But it is now more or less generally recognised that experience alone does not bring wisdom and understanding. Experience may well bring disenchantment and the kind of resignation which some would call 'philosophical'. But the fact that wisdom does not come automatically with age and experience is expressed in the truism 'There is no fool like an old fool'

True wisdom, then, comes not simply from experience, but from the careful rational contemplation of experience. Academic philosophers who emphasise this contemplative aspect of philosophy are called Rationalists; and disputes between Rationalists and Empiricists over the extent to which philosophy should be the measure of 'pure contemplation' or 'pure observation' constitute much -perhaps far too much- of the history of that subject. But academics apart, philosophical understanding is no automatic result of 'experience' and no assured consequence of 'pure reason' but always some balanced measure of both experience and reason.

Well, why bother, anyway? What can be wrong with just taking things as they come, simply 'doing what comes naturally'? Isn't this being philosophical? People of this kind of persuasion are called Existentialists. These see human destiny as shaped, not by reason but by the blind gropings of human will in the following of its own natural inclinations. However this may be, the fact is that the more advanced civilization becomes, the more complex the life-style that a man is constrained to lead; and the emphasis falls, whether we like it or not, less and less on our 'natural inclinations' and more and more on the proper and practical use of reason -in order even to survive.

Would it not be better, then, to cut out all the complications and live the simple life, close to nature, like the hermit or the hippie -or like Diogenes in his tub? At the present stage in history that suggestion seems rather pointless, since it is no longer possible, nor even (consistently) desirable, to live in this way. Nevertheless it reveals another popular view of the philosopher as the contented social 'dropout', as the sage living in splendid isolation from society and all its concerns. But it is not an intrinsic love of isolation that leads to this 'philosophical' kind of withdrawal. Rather it is a sense of dissatisfaction with contemporary society; and the same dissatisfaction felt by more spirited persons turns the reclusive cynicism of a Diogenes into the reforming zeal of a Plato or an Aristotle. But in any event, it is the feeling of discontent not of contentment that makes for philosophy, and anyone who can say that he is contented with life in these fractious times is claiming to be not a sage but a simpleton.

Philosophy, then, may be said to be the highest and most systematic expression of that continuing conflict with natural circumstance which sets man apart from the beasts -which makes him the rational and contemplative creature that he is. In his mind a man can create worlds other than the one which he inhabits, and it is those comparisons that he makes between his ideals and the actual world, that form the sources of his 'divine discontent'. Within certain limits set by nature he can, of course, transform the actual world so as to conform to his ideals, and this removes from him the burden of blind obedience to nature, giving him instead a measure of partnership and responsibility in the business of shaping his destinies.

Some say that all this places far too great a burden on ordinary folk. They therefore dismiss philosophy as something totally impracticable, as something for gods rather than men. Maybe so! But the time is long since past when we had any choice in the matter of whether to live 'naturally' or rationally. Those who dismiss philosophy must therefore realise that what they are dismissing is wisdom. And in refusing to be wise they can scarcely complain, thereafter, that they are governed by fools.

Normal Realism: a Declaration and Reiteration of Aims

Members of the various Philosophy departments of universities in the United Kingdom will no doubt, by now, have at least heard of the name of Normal Realism, of which philosophy this journal is the official expression. Professor Gilbert Ryle does not like our use of the suffix 'ism'. He says that 'isms' are a nuisance in that they may prejudice philosophical discussion. 'They suggest', he says, 'that philosophers are engaged in continuing bye-elections'. But how, we reply, can you avoid discussing 'isms' in philosophy -especially since adopting this attitude, anyway, implies an 'ism' of some kind or another! Isms, so far as we can see, are whole constellations of philosophical ideas and attitudes which have to be identified in order to know what they entail; and it is difficult to see what else could be discussed in philosophy other than how things appear from some particular, definable and declarable viewpoint -unless, of course, one here to espouse complete eclect-

icism (another inevitable ism).

We, the undersigned, therefore feel it encumbent on us to make this present declaration of the tenets of our philosophy of Normal Realism, thus giving maximum opportunity for criticism of our ideas. The details of this philosophy will be given in due course, but for the moment it may be said that, in general, we see common understanding as having ended, like the dinosaurs in a kind of evolutionary cul de sacs -or, rather, two separate cul de sacs called 'Modern Science' and 'Modern Philosophy'. This bifurcation of concerns, far from producing anything like a single, consistent and reliable, basis for common understanding, seems more nearly to have precluded all possibility of such a thing.

So far as main-line philosophy is concerned, the meaning of a word is little -if anything- more than its use according to the conventions of ordinary everyday language; whereas science, on the other hand, breaks almost completely away from ordinary conventions, establishing an eclectic method which is more or less indifferent to the claims of commonsense. Normal Realism seeks, in the interests of maintaining progress in common understanding, to re-unite science and philosophy within a single, evolving, framework of language. In this way we would hope that some definite move might be made towards the coordination of human ingenuity, under the auspices of philosophy so as to re-establish in this new and scientific age something analogous to what, in less complicated times, was known as 'commonsense'. In the pages of this present issue we shall set out in some detail the method by which we think this aim might be achieved -as well as providing one or two examples of how the method may be applied.

Perception and Deception

Normal Realism is a new (or, perhaps, old and refurbished) philosophical system whose basic method is that of advancing knowledge of the world by systematically applying elementary logic to phenomena

Now a phenomenon, as a modern dictionary* defines it, is 1 something visible or directly observable as an appearance, action, change or occurrence of any kind as distinguished from the force by which or the law by which it may be produced; 2 any fact, appearance or occurrence in consciousness; that which is apprehended by the senses, in contrast with, or in opposition to that which really exists or to things in themselves: contrasted with noumenon.

We feel that these definitions (there are other, more literary uses, of course, which do not concern us here) suffer from certain distortions introduced by earlier and out-dated epistemologies. The distinction, for instance, between an 'appearance, action, change or occurrence' and the 'force or law in accordance with which it is produced' is a vestige of a kind of dualism which we think is no longer acceptable. Nor, in respect of 2, would we wish, by our use of the word, to imply unreality: that is to say, we would not want to oppose 'phenomenon' to any more fundamental reality suggested by the meaning of 'noumenon'. So far as Normal Realism is concerned, whatever exists or takes place —be it a 'fact, appearance ...' etc., or a 'force, or law', or any other set of relations governing the same, is either an actual or a possible item of experience: that is to say, a phenomenon of some kind or another.

Normal Realism, then, like most modern philosophies, rejects any dualistic division between 'phenomenon' and 'noumenon' and follows the usual empiricistic line of thinking in placing everything that exists in the one immanent category of phenomena. Like the modern Naive Realist, we then observe that ordinary commonsense distinction between real and unreal among phenomena which does not imply dualism. Phenomenon A, for instance, may be an oasis and phenomenon B a mirage —hence an illusion of phenomenon A. Some phenomena may thus be no more than illusions —that is, misrepresentations— of other phenomena. But always, in perception, even when it is said to be 'illusory', there is some real object —that is to say, some phenomenal paradigm— subsumed as the ultimate reference of all phenomenal misrepresentations. Like its otherwise enfeebled conceptual cousin, Naive Realism, Normal Realism thus sets definite limits to the possibility of illusion. One cannot sensibly talk of 'illusion' without there being paradigm cases of reality with which these may be compared; and in this sense there is a level of reality, in common-or-garden perception, which is immune to philosophical scepticism.

On the other hand there is another kind of thinking which Normal Realism takes into account and thereby signifies its essential point of departure from Naive Realism. This is the way, typical of thinkers like Locke and Descartes, of seeing everything on the ordinary, naive or commonsense, levels of perception —even the so-called 'paradigms' thereof— as being in a sense unreal or 'illusory', and which sees the real as something transcending ordinary perception. We think that too many philosophers, fearful of the inroads made into commonsense by advancing scientific knowledge, have taken a negative view of scientific scepticism and have therefore failed to appreciate the validity, within its proper context, of this doubt (or 'illusion') methodology. They have thereby, in our view, over-reacted in encouraging commonsense to sever its connections with those scientific advances without which connections, we feel, both commonsense and science must eventually stultify.

But to give due credence to the natural, or scientific, philosophers we must consider the valid point about philosophical curiosity and scepticism, which, in our view, is as follows. Let us suppose that there are listed in some given language, all the characteristics necessary to the existence of a certain object. When these characteristics are all supposed present the object is normally said to exist. But in any actual instance of perception, unless the language is so rudimentary as to list only a very small number of characteristics, not all of the characteristics listed may be present. Some will, inevitably, be only assumed to accompany those that are actually sensed. Those missing characteristics will usually, of course, be fully available to subsequent inspection. That is to say, their existence can be confirmed, or verified. Nevertheless, since any particular examination of the object which may be carried out in fact, reveals only a limited number of characteristics at a time, it is perfectly sensible to say that it is (at that time) no more than a limited 'impression' or 'representation' of the real (i.e., assumed complete) object.

But then, insofar as any perception whatsoever is no more than what is perceived at a time; and insofar as any subsequent perception may reveal characteristics about an object which were not hitherto perceived, then perception always contains something that is assumed and therefore inherently uncertain

A real object, in one very normal and important sense of 'real', therefore -considering the open-ended or heuristic nature of experience in general- is never a finished empirical presentation, but always something extrapolated, or guessed at -something which, in its entirety, is never actually present but is always assumed to exist on the strength of the available evidence. In this way it makes very good sense to claim that as compared with any extrapolated 'final knowledge' of an object, all actual perception is to some extent or other 'illusory' (that is, according to a definite and philosophically specialised use of the word). This, we think, is the correct and sympathetic analysis of what the traditional scientific philosophers really (or, if not, should have) meant when they talked about the inherent 'illusoriness' of perception.

There is thus, we would say, a perfectly legitimate and normal sense of 'illusion', when the word is used in the context of careful philosophy, in which all perception is to some extent or other no more than guesswork about the true nature of things. By the same token, the reality of things is always something transcending actual (that is to say, de facto) perception, and those philosophers who used to talk about 'transcendental realities' are, if our analysis is correct, to some extent vindicated.

Those who, following Berkeley, see something problematical in such 'transcendental' references therefore commit, in our view, the fundamental mistake of imputing to their opponents a very narrow interpretation of the meaning of 'perception'. As stated in the first issue of phi, if all sense-perception were adjudged illusory by the standards of sense-perception, then any such 'argument from illusion' would be meaningless. But those who are accustomed to use this highly simplistic 'refutation' of the argument from illusion neglect to consider the fact that in any normal interpretation of 'perception', any and every perception occurs in time, and is therefore subject to its own judgment of itself according to the criterion of experience.

There is thus no contradiction in supposing that perception may be adjudged generally illusory according to the standards of perception, just so long as it is understood that by 'perception' we normally mean the (never completely finished) product of experience. The modern critics of the 'argument from illusion' are therefore fundamentally mistaken, we think, in taking it to imply some kind of metaphysical and totally inaccessible 'other world' of being, between which and the observer there interposes some altogether problematical 'veil', or 'iron curtain' of absolute illusion. As we have described it there is no such epistemological 'veil', and the relative paradigms of commonsense perception and the 'transcendental realities' of scientific philosophy are just different, but thoroughly compatible, aspects of the same (phenomenal) world.

In this way, we would reconcile what we believe to be the best in rationalistic with the best in empiricistic philosophy, to produce a balanced philosophy of nature which is not divisive, like present philosophy, of the respective interests of commonsense and science. For the interests of commonsense, everything about an object has to be fully accessible (in principle, at any rate) to sense-perception -but not necessarily actually perceived in order to exist. For the purposes of science, on the other hand, there always remains about an object much that is unperceived and therefore uncertain. Some of the things that exist in science may thus exist more as things of calculation and conjecture (objects of reason) than of actual sensation; being not so much seen as inferred from what is seen. And it makes good sense to say, concerning these things, that they 'exist unseen', or that they are 'unperceived and imperceptible', in the manner of speaking used by Locke. Thus, for the purposes of commonsense as much as of science, we accept as candidates for reality not only things that are sensed or are sensible but also, if necessary, things that are purely inferential; and we see no conflict of interests whatsoever between science and commonsense in this very normal kind of transcendental realism.

One of the things that characterises Normal Realism, then, is its normal interpretation of 'perception' as including not only the perceiving of things (particulars) but also the perceiving of relations between things. We reject as unempirical and unnatural all elementistic or reductionistic modes of philosophy which assume (for no good reason that we can see) that perception is not the perceiving of wholes but only of isolated and unrelated 'parts' (elements). On the basis of the evidence provided by that science which is concerned with perception (and empirical philosophy, so far as Normal Realism is concerned is always properly cognisant of such scientific evidence) perception as such is never the receipt of isolated and unrelated sensations.

THE 'VELOCITY OF LIGHT':-

RELATIVISTIC REPERCUSSIONS

A number of readers have reacted to our article 'The Velocity of Light' (see Issue 3, Jan. 1975) by pointing out the intimate connection between the concept of light-velocity and Einstein's Theory of Relativity. How, they ask, can you propose to get rid of the 'velocity of light' when so much of modern science is based upon it? And since the Theory of Relativity is so firmly established (runs the argument) then to jettison the 'light-velocity' concept, which is central to that theory, would be a disaster.

To kill two birds with one stone, let us also take another, allied, objection which is raised against Normal Realism, -again in connection with relativity theory. This argument is that a 'purely logical' kind of method, like that of Normal Realism, could never come up with anything like the Theory of Relativity, which is essentially the result of observational research -using the very thing we have declared redundant, namely, 'light-rays'

We are required, then, in vindication of Normal Realism, to show how the Theory of Relativity may be reconstructed on a 'purely logical' basis, without referring to experiments using 'light-rays'; and here, in this present article, is our answer to this challenge.

Now the method of Normal Realism is that described in our article beginning on page 4 of this issue, namely, that of applying elementary logic to phenomena Thus the phenomenon that we must start with, so far as relativity is concerned, is that of motion. The concept of motion normally requires us to make the logical distinction between three sub-concepts, or classes: 1, that of a body located in space; 2, the concept of a change in spatial location of that body; and, 3, the concept of differences in the speed of this change.

In order to quantify these differences in motion it is, of course, necessary to establish some kind of standard of motion as the datum by which all other motions are judged. This would, naturally, consist of some comparatively regular or uniform motion, such as that of the sun or moon across the sky or of the hands of a clock across a clock-face. The distance travelled in such a case is then called 'time', and we refer to these lengths in terms of units such as 'hours', 'minutes', 'seconds', etc. With each 'clock' there is thus selected some arbitrary ratio of spatial extension, or 'length', to the standard of motion chosen, so that we have unit of length / standard motion = unit of time.

Now, ideally, the relationship between these measures would be such that there is some absolute standard of motion on which all observers can agree, which would establish, at the outset, a unitary ratio between length-units and time-units. In such a case, one unit of length would be the distance travelled by a body at standard (unitary) velocity in one unit of time; and one unit of time would be the length travelled by the body at the standard velocity (of 1) divided by that velocity. In this way we would establish a fundamental equivalence between space and time; because in saying that time-units are space-units divided by one we are saying, in effect, that time is equal to space -on the assumption, of course, of such an absolute and universal standard

This ideal situation, however, does not apply in fact, because there has always been the question of which one of the numerous standards of motion will be taken as the absolute standard. The sheer arbitrariness of selection of such standards, in history, has therefore led to the assumption that just about any motion, provided, of course, it is uniform, may be selected as the measure of time. So arbitrary, is the relation made to appear in this way, between space-measure and time-measure that it is not surprising that in the past the two kinds of measure were taken to be independent of one another (that is, as vector-quantity to scalar quantity, respectively).

Let us return, however, from the exigencies of history to our logical ideal. Having established, by our selection of an absolute standard of motion, a constant and unitary (absolute and universal) relationship between space-measure and time-measure, let us now look at the various other motions which are met with in experience. Some of these may be uniform and others non-uniform, but for the sake of simplicity at this stage we shall consider only uniform motions, which we shall signify simply by the term 'velocity'. Any velocity which differs from our absolute standard will now have to be portrayed as a curve against two measures 'space' (or 'length') and 'time', which are distinguished in the normal way as two rectangular axes S and T (see Fig. 1, below).

FIG. 1

The velocity (v) of the (non-standard) body is then shown as a line whose gradient with respect to the two axes is the distance s (in absolute units of length) travelled by the body, divided by the duration of the motion t (in absolute units of time). It is to be remembered, of course, that measures in the axial directions S and T are now equivalent, whence v may be referred to as the absolute motion of a body (not to be confused with the use of that term in the context of classical physics).

Now it will be noticed that in this ideal case the only velocity that appears in Fig.1 is that of the body whose motion we are measuring. The 'velocity of the standard' does not appear anywhere, having been made logically redundant by the selection of absolute units for the measures S and T. Another thing which may be noticed is that the measures s and t, giving the slope of the curve v, are now related as quantities of the same kind; so they are related, not as vector to scalar quantities, but as vector quantities. This means that not only does the slope of the velocity-curve-v have logical significance, but so, also, does its length. In other words, the length measured along the curve from O to O₁ (see fig.) is now expressible as the resultant of two rectangular vectors s and t. In the velocity as depicted, the length of the resultant will be $$\begin{array}{l} \text { length } \mathrm{O}-\mathrm{O}_{1}=\sqrt{s^{2}+t^{2}} \\ =t_{1} \equiv s_{1} \ldots(1) \end{array}$$ Something else that arises by logical implication is as follows. The length O-O₁, in terms of the axis S, is some fixed distance s in the reference-frame of the observer, and the duration O-O₁, taking into account this distance s, is now, effectively, t₁, the vector-resultant given according to (1). As compared, then, with the theoretical absolute velocity v, the effective or relative velocity of the body is the measure v, expressed as

$$v=s / t_{1} \ldots(2)$$

We now have all that is necessary for our 'theory of relativity', for the rest is no more than a matter of straight algebra. For instance, from (2), by transposition, we obtain

$$s=v t_{1} \ldots(3)$$

Substituting this expression vt₁ for s in (1) gives

$$t_{1}=\sqrt{\left(v t_{1}\right)^{2}+t^{2}} \ldots(4)$$

Squaring both sides:-

$$t_{1}^{2}=\left(v t_{1}\right)^{2}+t^{2} \ldots(5) ;$$

simplifying:-

$$\begin{array}{l} t_{1}^{2}-\left(v t_{1}\right)^{2}=t^{2} \ldots(6) \\ t_{1}^{2}\left(1-v^{2}\right)=t^{2} \ldots(7) \\ t_{1}^{2}=\frac{t^{2}}{1-v^{2}} \ldots(8) \end{array}$$

and, taking the square root,

$$t_{1}=\frac{t}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}} \ldots(9)$$

What this signifies, of course, is that according to the hypothesis that there is some ultimate and universal, absolute relationship between units of space-measure and time-measure we may logically deduce that the motions of bodies will incur a relative time-dilation in accordance with formula (9). On this same principle, but taking account of the fact that the space and time units we do actually employ are not those (hypothetical) absolute but only arbitrary units, we can express the same formula in terms of metres and seconds by inserting a constant 'c' in the velocity-formula (2), viz.:-

$$v / c \propto s / t_{1} \ldots(2 a) .$$

And, finally, by carrying this empirical modification right through to the conclusion, we have

$$t_{1}=\frac{t}{\sqrt{1-v^{2} / c^{2}}} \ldots(9 a)$$

This formula, giving the relative dilation of time-measure due to motion, is identical to the one given in Einstein's Theory of Relativity. In (9a) however, c is no more than a constant relating the arbitrary units of space-measure to time-measure, whereas in Relativity it is the 'velocity of propagation of electrostatic and electromagnetic forces in a vacuum' -more popularly conceived as 'the velocity of light'. But we claim that this latter kind of conception is logically redundant, since the same formula may be derived by straightforward logical deduction from ordinary non-technical definitions of time, space and motion, and without any reference whatsoever to physical experiments involving light-rays. Had relativity theory been evolved on this purely logical, or philosophical, basis instead of on the basis of experimental physics, time-dilation might by now be regarded as a most natural -and quite inevitable- result of motion -instead of something infinitely puzzling concerning the journeyings of 'light-rays' in the void. Of course, we can all be very wise in retrospect, and no denigration of the work of the great physicists is intended by that last remark. The point is, nevertheless, that the relativity of time is a perfectly logical and natural deduction which, far from being accessible only to those initiated into the esoteries of science, can be made by anyone who can apply his reason, in philosophical fashion, to what he sees. Anyone who had really pondered on what was meant by 'space', 'time' and 'motion' would have been able, in principle at any rate, to predict the result of Einstein's Relativity. In Issue No.3 of phi (page 13, Col.1) we note Plotinus' prediction that there must, logically, be some 'outside standard' of motion which is not motion in any ordinary sense but which is 'more properly called Time'. Had this piece of philosophy been taken seriously, prior to the modern 'mechanisation' of optical theory, Roemer's reputed 'discovery of the velocity of light' might, instead, have been hailed as the establishment of the absolute relationship of space to time.

The Wise and the Otherwise

A busy rat, while scuttling through a sewer, Chanced upon another, strolling slow. 'Please tell me,' said the first, 'why do you idle? Have you no work to do, I'd like to know?'

The rat-philosopher replied: 'I'm thinking Of this, that and the other, how, and why.' My Goodness!' said the first, 'you must be ailing! A rat can't think and thrive; no use to try!

'You do not understand,' replied the thinker, 'I cannot see, in truth, how rats should live.' 'My Word, you must be stupid!' said the wise rat. 'Your thinking is entirely negative.

'A rat, you see, lives for a mighty purpose. It's clearly . . . -Well, it scarcely needs explaining. But you can think as healthily as I do, When once you've had the proper social training.

'You'll see there's Purpose in this World of Sewers, And a healthy rat may help fulfil this aim. Be POSITIVE, and you may see your children Thrive, till they bulge out at every drain.'

In the normal way of things, then, objects which are unseen (or unsensed) may be just as much 'in evidence' (i.e., just as real) as those that are seen -so long as their existence (as unseen entities) is necessary to the relational coherence and integrity of the perceived whole. We say, for instance, that we see a pig, when all that may be actually visible to us is its head and hindquarters -the rest being obscured, perhaps, by a tree-trunk; and people like Descartes can say, with equanimity, that they see people passing in the street, when there may be no more actual- ly visible than their hats and cloaks. We perceive, in other words, to some extent or other always inferentially, and in this respect commonsense and science are perfectly in harmony.

The only difference, then, we would say, between a commonsense and a scientific object is not that the one is somehow 'directly sensed' and the other only infer- red -far less that the one is immediately and uniquely real while the other is no more than 'metaphysical' or 'imaginary'. Both kinds of object are essen- tially relational and inferential and both are, in a straightforward and not over-simplistic way, sensible. All that distinguishes the two is that the measure of rational deliberation that goes into the one is far greater than what goes into the other. Those inferences which are contained in commonsense judgments of objectivity and reality are more or less matters of straight reflex (much of which is, perhaps, innate and much, no doubt, due to cultural conditioning in the context of previous science and philosophy (what Hume would call 'habit' or 'sentiment'). On the other hand, those inferences which are made in science are made more explicitly, by suppressing the usual language-conditioning which dictates commonsense responses, and giving the initiat- ive, instead, to personal reason and imagination in the more un- conventional contemplation of objects and situations (what is sometimes called 'scientific ingenuity'). Whenever this same ingenuity is extended, not only to particular areas of special interest, but to experience as a whole, then science, we say, merges into philosophy. Commonsense, science and philosophy, therefore, in the context supplied by Normal Realism, are not divided and competing fields of interest but simply different subdivisions of a single, continuous, spectrum of common concern.

Is Philosophy ...

Dead or Alive?

These men were not scholars but individual and creative thinkers. They were essentially REBELS against tradition. Each introduced to mankind a new and fundamentally different way of looking at the world, and in the dialogue which was set up between them was forged no less than the basis of our present civilization.

Plato

But this does not make them gods -superhuman beings that we have no hope of emulating. They were just men, and what one man or group of men can do for mankind others can do, also. But it is just because we are men and not gods that we cannot allow our ideas ever to become enshrined beyond the reach of criticism. The dialogue which these men began must continue, so that we can do for the future at least as much as they did for the past and present.

Socrates

What is WANTED, therefore, is an injection into modern philosophy of that critical, freethinking spirit which characterised this early but illustrious era of philosophy. The HUNT for new ideas must still go on, to sustain an ever-evolving society; and by 'hunt' is meant something more than that mere scholastic paper-chase that is called 'Philosophy' in our universities.

WHERE, then, is this hunt going on? Where, indeed? But this magazine offers a forum for those who, like the men portrayed here, have sufficient of the true spirit of philosophy to follow their own rational initiative. To think as these thought we must cease to be overawed by what men call 'genius' and recognise it as just something that exists AT LARGE, in ordinary human ingenuity. In such periods as may lack the 'shining lights' of 'pure genius' philosophical activity need not therefore be suspended, but may continue by the diffuse light generated by the pooling of many, more modest, resources.

What THIS MAGAZINE OFFERS, therefore, in this new 'Dark Age' of philosophy, is an opportunity of producing some small glimmer of the kind of questioning spirit which was exemplified by Socrates, Plato and Aristotle. Philosophy or Science?

Professor Sir Karl Popper, who writes that he was 'quite impressed' with the October Issue, (No.2) of phi, would like us to explain what was the point, in Issue 3 (Jan.) of the two articles dealing with what most readers saw as 'Science' (i.e., those dealing with the 'Relativistic Space-Time Discontinuum' and the 'Speed of Light', on pages 8 and 12, respectively.

Now in order to offset the so far highly polemical nature of this journal we tried, in the third issue, to develop some of the more positive aspects of the philosophy of Normal Realism. Normal Realism stresses that there is not just one but many possible ways of looking at the world, and that the one which we call 'Science' is no more than one—and perhaps not the most logical one—of all the various logically possible alternatives.

So far as the 'Relativistic Discontinuum' article was concerned, therefore, the point was to give an example of how Normal Realism works by demonstrating one logical alternative to present 'Science'. In the article, the present 'electrodynamical' metaphysic, which is now more or less obligatory in science, was logically 'by-passed' by an appeal to ordinary geometry and arithmetic. In short, it was found that simply by taking the physical definition of action (i.e., energy x time) and applying it to the appropriate formulae in the theory of relativity we obtain, by straight algebra, a formula for energy-distribution which is the same as that established experimentally by Balmer and Rydberg for atomic spectra. Moreover, by expressing these action-lengths as closed loops (cycles) we obtained a schematic representation which is indistinguishable from the 'electrodynamical' atom of Niels Bohr. The point about all this was that this was done without any reference whatsoever to the customary 'electrodynamic-

al' paraphernalia of present science, thus giving support to the stipulated method of Normal Realism.

Many of our readers, however, made the mistake of supposing that the article in question dealt with science. The mistake was, perhaps, understandable, since it certainly looked like science—especially the picture on page 9, which was a point-for-point philosophical parallel of the hydrogen atom of Niels Bohr. However, the title of the drawing did quite explicitly state that it was a 'Picture of Relativity', not a representation of the Bohr atom. But it seems that habitual associations are too deeply ingrained, at present, to dissociate very easily the philosophical from the customary 'scientific' treatment of natural phenomena.

Now Normal Realism is also, essentially, an empirical philosophy, and the aim of the other article, on the 'Velocity of Light' (see Repercussions, on page 6) was to show that in the accepted, so-called, 'fact' of the 'velocity of light in space' there is so much unempirical (metaphysical) supposition. So in this article, too, certain scientific presuppositions were being subjected to philosophical analysis in the declared style of Normal Realism. What the article concluded was that the 'velocity of light', as understood in science, is very largely a fiction—or, perhaps, just a very loose (and, we think, confusing) way of stating the true fact that for any observer, space-measure and time-measure are fundamentally related in the constant ratio of units c (= 3 x 10^8 in mks units).

Now Professor Popper felt that we had omitted to explain in sufficient detail what 'the problem' was that was answered in the two articles in question. To make good that defect we would now say that in order to understand what was attempted it is essential that the issues raised be seen in the context of the method of Normal Realism, and that they be clearly distinguished from those of science. In philosophy, as we have presented it, there are problems of a very definite kind which cannot be interpreted at the level of 'science' any more than the problems of chess can be interpreted at the level of dominoes. In philosophy, as we see it, the main business is criticism: and criticism, as P.K. Feyerabend says: 'must use alternatives (which) will be the more efficient the more radically they differ from the point of view to be investigated.' But in science, as such, the problem of producing alternatives does not arise unless there are pressing reasons for changing existing theories; and such a 'pressing reason' can only be the appearance of some unexplained or contradictory fact of observation. Thus, where there are no facts cited there is no problem, so far as science is concerned, and the theoretical or metaphysical status quo may then be regarded as unassailable.

Since we do not cite any experimental anomaly, or any contradiction in terms of 'factual evidence', it must therefore seem to the scientifically conditioned thinker that we are stirring things up unnecessarily in proposing answers to non-existing problems. Problems, in philosophy, however, are essentially non-specific—which makes so many people feel that it has no relevance whatsoever to daily life. But from the philosophical point of view one very large and pressing problem is that there is, at the present time, no common philosophy of nature—or, which is the same thing, no shared and reliable understanding of ourselves or of the world we live in.

SWORD OF DAMOCLES

Stated philosophically, the problem is that commonsense, in recent times, has become so intellectually battered and bemused by masses of 'scientific evidence' and by so many outlandish and conflicting claims made by experts in so many diverse fields of research, that it seems to have succumbed to a kind of woolly confusion. In such a situation the emphasis falls on severely limited notions of 'expediency' and 'practicality' which, if continued, must surely jeopardise the very concept of civilization. And it is scarcely surprising that in such a situation the academic disputations of the moralising philosophers seem to have no more significance than 'noises in a swound'. From the point of view of philosophy, therefore, as opposed to science, nothing is more abundantly manifest in present society than a general social disorientation—a complete loss, one might say, of evolutionary 'sense of direction'.

The problem which we have set ourselves, therefore, is that of tracing the extent to which sheer illusion has spread from the direction of the sciences into what, fancifully, is 'just plain commonsense'. Far from taking the internal view of science our aim is to take the philosophical over-view of science vis a vis ordinary understanding. It is also to conduct, in the context of what is revealed in this overview, a renewed assault on illusion in the name of a newly articulated philosophy of empiricism, called Normal Realism.

What we thus have in mind is not just another scholarly supplication, nor just another iconoclastic expression of dissatisfaction with the present 'regime'. We are neither academics nor hooligan reactionaries. We represent, perhaps, no more than some faint stirrings within commonsense itself, towards a new philosophical awareness; of men's rights in the matter of understanding.

Dialogue

Dear Mr. Pope,

Having devoted some little time to studying your publication, I feel I ought to offer you an opinion, a personal subjective view of one who is neither a scientist nor a philosopher. It will naturally be close to that expressed by the man who, encountering the Grand Cham in that bookseller's shop, remarked that although he respected philosophers he could not follow in their footsteps because cheer- fulness always kept breaking through.

I was interested -perhaps because of its relative compre- hensibility- in your (article in Issue 3 entitled 'Are People Still Interested in Philosophy?') which advocates the philosophical approach as the only remedy against the sort of difficulties society experiences. The burden of the article (with its odd title. Why 'still'?) seems to be that philosophers (are they an identifiable group?) are held in general contempt. This seems an extraordinary censure of what must be one of the most tolerant phases British society has so far achieved. Indifference, perhaps. But looking back over the failure of philosophers over the centur- ies to induce enlightenment in the conduct of public affairs, can you seriously put them forward as a sort of elite capable of put- ing an end to our woes? Personal- ly I should prefer to put my money on some sort of religious cult as a moderator of human behaviour.

Nonetheless I should be interested to learn more of your enterprises. From the repeated references to 'normal realism' I assume this idea is somehow central to your argument, but why you should call yourselves 'Welsh realists' intrigues me.

Ian F Low, Social Editor of the NEW SCIENTIST

Dear Mr. Low

Yes, your letter more or less confirms my estimate, in the article you mention, of the general low regard which people have nowadays for philosophers and philosophy. You speak of the failure of philosophers, over the centuries, to induce enlightenment in the conduct of public affairs. Precisely! And that is just what concerns us! We think that the trouble is due, however, not to philosophy (indeed, how could it be, since social disharmony and a lack of coordinating philosophy are practically one and the same) but to philosophers.

You ask whether we can seriously put philosophers forward as a sort of elite capable of putting an end to our woes. Our answer to that question would be no. But if you had asked whether we can seriously put forward philosophy as an elite subject, our answer would be yes -because it is there already, being, by its very nature, the most elite of subjects. Its business is, by definition, that of wisdom, and as such it has (or at least should have) complete oversight over all that a man thinks and does.

But like all other subjects, philosophy functions on certain basic objective principles and presuppositions which (unlike those of religion) are amenable to rational criticism. The aim of this journal is to provide a forum for the democratic discuss- ion of these principles, and the aim of what we have called Normal Realism is to try to clear away some of the traditional clutter so as to allow discussion to get under way.

Your point, by the way, about us calling ourselves 'Welsh Realists' has intrigued many other readers in the same way. The three principals of phi are of Welsh nationality -two of them being graduates of the University of Wales.

Dear Mr. Pope,

In the light of your further explanation, perhaps there is another point to be made, on the analogy of science and its applied form, technology. Philos- ophy is an adornment to any culture that calls itself civil- ised, but philosophers, unlike the similarly disputatious scientists, do not seem at last to arrive at a consensus which in its turn can be transformed into a practical axiom in the way that over a time, a scientific theory becomes a technologist's tool. It is this lack of a visible connection which may be at the root of the popular disenchantment with philosophy.

Dear Mr. Low,

Yes, I agree with your further point that philosophy, nowadays, is little more than an adornment -just as science was in more primitive times. My view is that philosophy, in this Twentieth Century, is at a stage of develop- ment similar to that of alchemy in the Middle Ages. One hopes, however, that man will eventually succeed in applying as much science to his moral and politic- al life as he now does to his technology. Meanwhile it seems that philosophy must remain comparatively useless -until, as Plato says, society learns to make proper use of it.

Your point about the lack of consensus on practical axioms in philosophy follows very logically. I would say, from your view of the lack of practicality in the subject. There would, no doubt, be as little consensus in science if it were not for the feeling of practical necessity that has developed around scientific thinking. By this same token, the aim of philosophers, in truly Socratic tradition, should be to discourage people from merely fooling around with the subject and get them to feel the necessity of philosophical under-

standing. This is what happened with science, and we think it is now high time that philosophy followed suit.

So far as we in the phi movement are concerned there has been far too much idealism in the philosophical tradition, and far too many vestiges of this, in our considered opinion, still remain. Our aim, therefore, is to bring the subject, so far as its nature permits, 'down to earth': to bring into it some sense of normality and practical importance—indeed, of necessity. This signifies, of course, that some pressure must be brought to bear, on those who make no more than intellectual sport of the subject, to consider its real importance, and to show some practical concern for the increasingly high levels of sheer academic pollution that clogs the channels of communication and turns extramural philosophy into a veritable wasteland.

We hope that sufficient pressure towards practicality in philosophy will be exerted so soon as people begin to realise, even to the smallest extent, just how morally and intellectually deprived, in this new, scientific and technological age, all of us really are.

Q.

The implication in your paper seems to be that philosophy has to justify itself to the practical world; that it should, like science, be able to present something saleable. But Philosophy, as an Art, has no more need to answer to the layman than has academic History, English, Classics etc. Philosophy of Science perhaps, as you indicate, may have to subject itself to the rigours of scientific reasoning, but what about Ethical Philosophy, Theory of Knowledge, Political Philosophy and the many other non-scientific philosophies? They are not intended as offerings of anything other than individual interpretations of the world. A degree in Philosophy is an indication that someone has an intelligence of a certain level. Why should University have to be a training school? We are supposed at this stage in civilisation to be sufficiently emancipated to educate without expecting a saleable return.

A.

You seem to be saying that all Philosophy, with the possible exception of Philosophy of Science, is an art; and that as an art it comes under the claim made for Art, by Oscar Wilde, that 'Art is useless.' This claim might seem, on the face of it, to be supported by Plato's similar statement: 'Philosophy is useless.' There is, however, one very important difference between the two claims. The claim that Oscar Wilde was making was a prescriptive one: he was saying that Art should by rights be useless. Plato's statement, on the other hand, was more descriptive: he was saying that Philosophy was in fact useless, but only because society (to its cost) does not know how to make use of it.

If you had said that economics should not dictate creativity, then we would be the first to agree with you. Our main aim, in the phi movement, is to signify the existence of the possibility of the emancipation of human reason and creativity from its present enslavement under the tyranny of Economic Necessity. But freedom, surely, is nothing if it is just freedom to do nothing. If ideas are, in accordance with the Wildean claim, essentially useless, then this implies that all that is useful and practical—all that is real—falls under the monopoly of blind mechanical forces, such as those postulated by the economic theorists. Such a 'freedom', claimed on behalf of mind, would amount to no more, in the end, than a freedom to dream.

We think this a very poor kind of freedom, the pursuit of which can do no more than help to sustain the dogma of economic determinance. We want 'intellectual freedom' to mean something; and if it is to mean something more than just a freedom to dream it must be something which is not only creative but also practically and effectively creative.

We want, more than anything, to break the present economic stranglehold on human creativity—the publication of this magazine, for instance, is an intellectual, practical, and distinctly un-economic venture. But we don't think it helps the cause of mental freedom to make Wilde claims on behalf of either Art or Philosophy.

Q.

Very simply: what particular advance does your 'Normal Realism' propose on modern commonsense philosophy: the philosophy that you refer to as 'Naive Realism'?

A.

Very simply: the advance that it proposes is to make the criterion of the real not just commonsense but an evolving commonsense. In other words, our aim is to replace Naive Realism by a Not-so-naive Realism.

Q.

How normal, would you say, is 'Normal Realism'?

A.

We estimate that Normal Realism is about as normal as Commonsense is common.

PHI EXTENDS ITS DIALOGUE TO MATTERS OF RELIGION

Q.

With regard to your article in 'phi', Volume 3, p. 4, I don't hold, either, with the idea of 'pearly gates' or any other allegorical composition of heaven. But I do believe in God as being in us with us and around us. It is true that one's faith is in constant need of repair, but it is because we humans are imperfect. We live in an imperfect world, with imperfect solutions. Anything from religion, art, etc., even argument, is a form of escape, and why not?

A.

Yes, why not? But, surely, it is illusion that we need to escape from, not reality! And what characterises illusion is precisely those imperfections you speak of, and which are, invariably, man-made. The idea, nowadays, that such imperfections are 'natural and inevitable' is, we think, today's most surpassing illusion; and the 'realism' which operates in terms of this illusion is, we think, today's most surpassing vanity.

True philosophy and true religion are not, we think, hostile to each other; and in the context of philosophy the concept of a spirit force which is 'in us, with us and around us' claims at least as much credence as many modern scientific concepts (such as those of 'electricity' and 'magnetism') — and might well, in the end, prove more correct. But that is as far as philosophy goes. To go further one must go in faith, in the name of religion, not philosophy. The only further jurisdiction that philosophy would claim over religion is that of ensuring that religious ideas are at least consistent with (that is, not contrary to) reason — otherwise, of course, they are by definition mere mystery and illusion.

There is, however, nothing that we can see contrary to reason in a belief in God as the ultimate source of all the perfection — and, therefore, of the reality — that man, however imperfectly, perceives.

Q.

In Issue 3 of 'phi', page 6, you answer a question sent in by someone (apparently, a physicist) who was concerned with physical measurements. You state that the only entities which signify in Normal Realism are logical classifications of phenomena. But it seems to me that there are too many things, accepted as common manifestations of experience, which your method banishes to the realm of fiction or 'metaphysics'.

There are testimonies, for instance, from various religious groups of people who believe that a supernatural order exists, and experiences of this order, which are received by individuals are confirmed by others who have similar experiences. I do not think that such beliefs and faiths can be lightly pushed aside by any scientifically orientated modern philosophy.

A.

The charge of 'scientific orientation' is one which is frequently levelled at our kind of philosophy. It is, however, a spurious charge, as we hope has been made clear in the article on page ten of this present issue.

So far as we are concerned, science, which once promised the final emancipation of human reason from superstition and religious dogma by getting people to look at the world as it is, with their own eyes and brains, now imposes its own peculiar limitations on perception — as we hope to demonstrate from time to time. Only very rarely does a man see in strictly logical terms what his senses present to him, and when that happens progress is made in understanding. But for the most part a man interprets his experiences according to some convention or other; and in the conflict of conventions (as, e.g., between 'religion' and 'science') that leads to our present ways of thinking we have the source of that perceptual 'schizophrenia' which is expressed in the dichotomy omy between 'natural' and 'supernatural' — a mystique which ascribes to reality the human failing of a split, or multiple personality.

What nonsense this dichotomy must seem to the future historian. Will he give any more credence to today's 'scientific' than to yesterday's 'spiritual' realities — or will he see such divisions as mere cracks caused by a too rapid change in a particularly inflexible system of thought, which is cemented into the bedrock of tradition rather than allowed to turn freely in the gymbals of reason?

In anticipation of such future criticism we do not distinguish, in our realistic philosophy, between 'natural' and 'supernatural' in experience. Experience is experience. The only question is that of whether the interpretation of that experience is logical or illogical. We accept that there may well be realities which are beyond (present) reason. But no reality, we would say, is ever contrary to reason. Our philosophy therefore leaves plenty of room for faith — faith in the ultimate orderliness and meaningfulness (in a word, the rationality) of true existence, which religious people regard as the most essential prerequisite of a belief in God.

So, while we feel that as a matter of general policy and method, philosophy should be as agnostic as mathematics in its approach to problems, we by no means banish or 'push aside' religious faith. Far less do we do so in the name of 'science'!

Alex Thomas

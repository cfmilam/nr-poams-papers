Theoretical Egalitarianism

N. Vivian Pope

Or: 'Gullible's Trammels'

Jonathan Swift, disgusted with the society of his time, commented on it with his allegory of the Kingdom of Lilliput in his book Gulliver's Travels. Let us imagine a modern version of Lilliput, an allegorical society organised on the principle of 'political correctness' to the extent that town-planning building regulations dictate that in the building of, say, a bridge or a skyscraper, no single architectural design is to be chosen above any of the others or, worse, that every building plan or proposal is to be regarded as equal, all to be put into effect together, indiscriminately, with no plan or design to be excluded. In this way, engineers and construction engineers are presented with a huge batch of blueprints with the instruction not to select any particular one of them but to build according to them all, so as to be equally fair to all the designers and architects. Would it be possible to build anything of any worth according to that egalitarian principle? Of course not! So what could possibly be done with all those different plans except, perhaps, put them all into something like an art-gallery for aesthetic display only, abandoning any prospect of advancing any practical building project by means of them, outside that theoretical art-gallery.

Now for this passage, substitute for 'Lilliput' the name 'Modern Science'. For 'plan' or 'design', read 'theory' or 'hypothesis'. With these and one or two other substitutions the paraphrase reads as follows. Let us imagine a modern version of Physical Science organised on the principle of 'political correctness' to the extent that its regulations dictate that no single physical or cosmological theory is to be chosen above any of the others — or, worse, that every theory or hypothesis presented is to be regarded as equal, all to be put into effect, together, indiscriminately with no single theory or hypothesis to be excluded. All practical physicists and astronomers are to be presented with a huge batch of theoretical interpretations of phenomena with the instruction not to select any particular one of them as a foundation but to construct a world-view according to them all, so as to be equally fair to everyone. Would this be possible? Of course not! So what could possibly be done with all those different theories except, perhaps, to put them all into something like an art-gallery, for aesthetic display only, abandoning any prospect of advancing any practical advancement outside that theoretical art-gallery?

There is only one sensible way for the development of science to proceed, which is to select, on rational grounds, from among as many plans as possible, which is the most suitable construction.

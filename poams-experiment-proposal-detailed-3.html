1. The orbital angular momentum of spinning bodies. POAMS is the acronym for the Pope-Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis. Its principals are N. Vivian (Viv) Pope and Anthony D. Osborne, and the Synthesis is explained as follows. In Newton’s seventeenth century description of orbital motion no significant account was taken of the spin angular momentum of orbiting bodies. According to Newtonian theory, orbiting bodies are kept in orbit by a hypothetical in vacuo ‘gravitational’ attraction which opposes their assumed tendency to fly off in straight lines, the so-called ‘centrifugal force’. In this present twentyfirst century, POAMS proposes a long overdue updating of that Newtonian theory which is still used today by NASA, for instance, in calculating the trajectories of their space-probes and by astronomers in calculating the masses of the spiral galaxies. Aside from questions of Relativity, this more basic modification is, in accordance with the law of angular momentum conservation, to include any spin angular momentum an orbiting body might have in its overall angular momentum. Evidence for this modification exists in the so-called ‘anomalous’ deviations of the tracks of the space-probes and the masses of the galaxies from expectations formed on the basis of Newton’s gravitational hypothesis. As evidences for POAMS, these anomalies speak for themselves, and are dealt with elsewhere [1]. In this present dissertation our aim will be to consider the feasibility of a ground-based experiment to detect the POAMS prediction of similarly anomalous small changes in the weight of ordinary – that is to say, non-relativistically fast spinning bodies. First, however. Let us be clear what we understand by angular momentum. Angular momentum is a conserved quantity of rotational motion in given direction. The directions are called vectors which, like the quantity, or magnitude, of the motion, are also conserved. Perhaps the best example of angular momentum is the common gyroscope. Once set in motion, barring the effect of outside forces, from then on the gyroscopic disk typically maintains both the magnitude and orientation (vector) with regard – not to the earth nor any other planet nor even to the sun, but (mysteriously, some say) with respect to the universe in general. This is known in physics as Mach’s Principle, according to which, all stars, planets, spiral galaxies and other bodies, from those galaxies to spinning microphysical particles such as electrons and protons are part of an overall gyroscopically conserved nexus of interconnected motions in which, by the principle of conservation, any change in angular momentum (torque) in one place instantly perturbs the motion of some other body, or bodies, somewhere else. We say ‘instantly’ because there is no way in which conservation can be maintained during the time it would take for the influence to ‘travel’ in any way – for perhaps millennia – in a metaphysical limbo between one perturbation event and another. Such distant interactions must therefore take place at the fastest ‘speed’ possible. This is a the ‘speed of light’, which is proper-time instantaneous. All this is dealt with in depth in the POAMS literature. For the present our aim is simply to add to the astronomical evidence for this anomalous influence, some experimental evidence of this same anomalous effect of rotational motion on a terrestrial level – between our earth, say, and a gyroscope spinning in a frame fixed to the earth’s surface What, then, are the effects which POAMS predicts for spinning objects on the earth’s surface? For example, Hideo Kawasaki and his team at Tohoku university, Japan, have carried out weight-change experiments with extremely fast-spinning bodies, and Prof. Mordecai Milgrom, of the Department of Condensed Matter Physics Weizmann Institute, Israel, has suggested changes in G as an explanation of the so-called ‘Missing Mass’ anomaly, namely that there seems to be much more matter in the observed universe than can be accounted for by the Newtonian ‘gravitational’ hypothesis. Unfortunately, these independent explanations have no common theoretical basis, which makes them no more than speculative and ad hoc. The Milgrom explanation, for instance, offers no experimental evidence for its hypothesis, and the results of the Tokai experiment are altogether equivocal; all attempts, to replicate them in order to confirm the reported results having failed. In contrast to these theories, the predictions of POAMS for spinning bodies are sufficiently unambiguous to make them refutable according to Popper’s Falsifiability Criterion for proper scientific theories. That is to say, if its predictions are confirmed, then the theory survives; if not, then the theory fails. POAMS accepts that there is no middle way to be contemplated.

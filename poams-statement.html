POAMS Statement

N. Vivian Pope & Anthony D. Osborne

POAMS (The Pope-Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis)

There is something very strange about our customary way of thinking about light. Although we can never see light leave an object and then travel through space to enter our eye or some other optical instrument we nevertheless insist on thinking of light as travelling in that way.

Plainly, however, what we see, in the very first instance, is the light itself, and then, from the information that is in the light we project our physical world, much in the way that, from the pixels on a television screen, we project the world of a video scenario. And from the way objects behave in that field of observational information, the way they are distanced, the way they move in relation to one another and so on, we project all the dimensions of space and time and everything else we can know about those objects. This defines relativity according to the literal interpretation of that word.

But then, in an amazing mental turnabout, we reverse this order of priority in our knowledge of the world and persuade ourselves that we can know nothing of the existence of those objects until – in some cases, long after leaving them – the light reaches us. What made us think like that? The reason is that in 1676 it was discovered by an astronomer, Olaus Römer, that as we see bodies move away from us, so the intervals between events on those bodies become longer and longer. Traditionally, distance and time have been measured in different sets of units – nowadays in metres, and seconds, respectively. What Römer had discovered, in its most economical interpretation, was that in observation these units are constantly related in the ratio of approximately three hundred million metres to the second. Known as c, this constant is now known throughout the whole of physics.

Then something very strange happened. Because Römer's constant c is a ratio of distance to time it was summarily interpreted as a 'speed'. And so began the convention (followed by Einstein, of all people!) of describing c as 'the speed of light in a vacuum'. Logically, this is nonsense, not only because a vacuum cannot be regarded as a reference-zero for a speed, constant or otherwise, but also because it puts all our knowledge of things behind and beyond our actual perceptions of them. When we see a distant star, although there is no way we can know of that star's existence until we actually see it we are conned into thinking that what we see is not the real star but only an after-image of what that star once was at some time now long past. This has torn modern physics away from its roots in commonsense perception and put it into realms apart where it has become a playground for some of the wildest and most harebrained of theoreticians.

POAMS reverses this classical inversion of our knowledge by banishing that theoretical nether-world and restoring to science the original, more natural order of priorities. It advocates the systematic removal of all the competing traditional theories about what we think physical reality is behind and beyond the light and relocates our physical science on the simplest and most minimal logical interpretations of what we actually perceive with our senses and instrument systems. Basic to this agenda is to reinterpret what is traditionally called 'the constant speed c of light in space' in the way that the Cambridge mathematician, Herman Bondi does. This is simply as a constant ratio of length-units to time-units in our observational projections of optical phenomena.

The programme for science that this presents is that pioneered, originally, by the philosophers Berkeley, Hume and Kant. Brought to near fruition in the work of Einstein's mentor, Ernst Mach, it has been developed further by the POAMS group of natural philosophers. This defines an empirical purism, or radical relativism, the physical implications of which, for modern relativistic and quantum physics have been explored in depth for, now, more than forty years. Carried through consistently, this 'Copernican revolution' in our ideas of space and time produces a slimmed-down and logically consistent version of relativity which also incorporates the elements of quantum physics. This unifies the notoriously divided relativity and quantum theory which, in the customary way of thinking have resisted all attempts at logical reconciliation.

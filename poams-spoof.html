How the Universe Began For the Universe to begin, the time had to just right. But since there was, at first, no such thing as time, then in order to begin, the universe had to wait for ages and ages for that momentous event of time-creation to occur. When time did eventually come into existence it was ten past eight in the morning. Space did not yet exist; it was eleven-twenty-four in the evening before that started. This was at a point of zero dimension, which was by then so packed with matter that it just had to explode, which it did with such a bang that it could be heard just about everywhere. This was, of course, the celebrated Big Bang. After 2.563(46) microseconds from kick-off, space was as big as about one and three-quarter footballs. But because there was nothing for the space to expand into, there was much pressure and friction at the interface between the space and the nothing. This caused a tremendous rise in temperature at the interface (actually, 18.25 billion degrees Kelvin). But then, suddenly, at eleven-twenty-four p.m. plus 2.563(47) microseconds, the barrier between the space and the nothing was shattered and the space, with its packed-in matter, penetrated into the nowhere. This consequently became a somewhere – which was also, of course, an everywhere – in which the shock-waves of the event reverberated and into which the space could now expand and cool. This it did into globules which we now know as stars and galaxies, and the shock-waves became what we now perceive as light, which travels relative to both the nothing and the something at a constant speed of about 2.99792458 metres to the second. Meanwhile, the integrity of the whole was conserved by means of interactions involving nonexistrons, little interactional ‘middle-men’ which whizzed to and fro, like angels, keeping everything in contact. These non-particles are bosons, bits of non-matter which do not have to obey the laws of ordinary mechanics or, indeed, any other laws, including those of ordinary logic. Unlike photons, these nonexistrons travel everywhere at the speed of dark, which is, of course, instantaneous, being always there ahead of the light and therefore distinctly superluminal. Moreover, being instantaneous, they are everywhere all at once; but that’s okay, because being non-existent there is no danger of them bumping into one another, which certainly solves that problem. Up till that time of eleven-twenty-four p.m. plus the 2.563(47) microseconds, light did not exist in the space, which was too packed with matter for light to get going in it (see our coloured illustration, below). At the later time of exactly eleven-twenty-four plus 2.563(481) microseconds, however, gaps began to appear in the (dark) matter, into which gaps the light could now propagate, as it already had (after the earlier 2.563(47) microseconds) into the nothing (void or vacuum) when it was punctured by the expanding space. As the explosion continued, light, at its finite speed, had further and further to go between the expanding gaps between the globules of matter until, eventually, the distances

Now why was Mach so implacably opposed to Einsteinian relativity? Very few physicists, it seems, know the reason for this, which is why it needs to emphasised at the ouiset. It was that although Einstein claimed that his relativism was a natural expression of Mach's philosophical ideas, and although he claimed to be, like Mach, a philosopher first and a physicists second - as all truly innovative scientists ever were - he, Einstein, had completely misunderstood the relativistic philosophy he claimed to be expressing.

Let us see why this is so. The first move towards modem relativism was made, in the 18tr century by George Berkeley. The physics of Berkeley's time was based on the atomic materialism of Democritus, approximately 300 years B.C., which had been introduced into nascent Westem science, by the 17e century philosopher Ren6 Descartes. In that mechanistic way of thinking, the reality of physical objects lay, not in the appearances they p.es"rri to us in observation Qthenomena) but in their inscrutable atomic c-onstituents. What Berkeley did, presaging the relativism of Einstein, was to invert that Democritan way of thinking, giving precedence instead to the observational informationdata - the phenomena - as opposed to what Democritus had envisaged as those absolutely rota *a self-suffrcient, absolutely localised bits of matter. In other words, for Berkeley, the reality of anything consisted, first and foremost, in being observed, or 'perceived'. Anything else we may think of or talk about, 'space', 'time' and even the .*i.t"rr"" of atoms and the alleged 'passage' of light between them is projected, primarily, from information supplied by the senses.

Berkeley's choice of the word 'perceived', however, was unfortunate because it made it seem that, in order to exist, a material object has to be actually observed by some human observer (ultimately, oneself), which made the object's unobserved existence as a thing in itself indescribable. The problem Berkeley thus bequeathed to his followers was that of how we can ever be assured of the reality of anything other than our own subjective impressions - or 'ideas', as Berkeley called them. That was the problem which was handed on vra Berkeley's successor, David Hume to Immanuel Kant. By taking Berkeley's system of ideas (idealism) to its logical

conclusion, Hume had shown that if these 'ideas' ars all there are, then there can be no .system' that can hold them together other than one's own pure 'habits' of perceiving. This reduction to absurdity, by Hume, of Berkeley's 'way by ideas' galvanised Kant. He could not see how it was possible, if what Hume had deduced was true, that the mechanistic science of Isaac Newton had been so spectacularly successful. To reconcile Berkeley's phenomenalism with Newtonian science, Kant dispensed with Berkeley's 'ideas' and replaced them with 'concepts'. To dispose of Hume's 'habits' as thi sole supplier of connections between these entities he sorted them into a system of logical classes and categories designed to paraphrase, in his phenomenological terms, the science of Descartes, Galileo and Newton.

In this way, Berkeley's initial move towards modern relativism was brought, by Kant, to its second stage of development. Unfortunately, Kant's 'concepts' were no improvement on Berkeley's 'ideas', and his system of 'categories' was no advance on Hume's 'habits', because it was all still rooted in the human psyche. As viewed from the standpoint of objective science, therefore, Kant's version of 'relativism' was no more than just another form of 'subjective idealism'.

The third stage in the move towards modern relativism was made by Ernst Mach, the philosophical mentor of Albert Einstein. Mach replaced Kant's psychological system of 'concepts' with a system of objective relations between objects as viewed in different, inter-communicated observational frameworks, or perspectives. This, in effect, was the system that became the famous Special Theory of Relativity presented by Albert Einstein in 1905. To Mach's consternation, however, Einstein took the backwards step of basing his relativism on the atomic materialism of Democritus, which was why Mach wrote his notorious disclaimer. The reason for his rejection was that light, n the sense simply of illuminatioz, was for him no more nor less than the fundamental sensory/instrumental data fromwhich the phenomena of space, time and motion are communally projected. In conhas! Einstein presented lighg not as an integral part of the phenomenal scenario but as something which, in the manner of the atomists' 'streaming substance', acts as a subphenomenal or 'behind-the-scenes' and invisible conveyor of bits of observational information. For Einstein, this underlying 'streaming substance' took the form of esoterically conceived 'wave-particles', which he called 'photons', issuing from the subworld of atoms that Democritus had supposed was light's underlying root source. This so-called 'Einstein separation' between the world of self-sufficient atoms and the world of the observer had simply reconstituted the materialistic science of Democritus, making matter the primary reality and our information regarding it as, at best, a secondary and

This, for Mach, meant that the philosophical advance that Berkeley had initiated from Democritan materialism to modern relativism had stalled. Einstein's version of 'relativity' had ended-up in a 'hung state' in which it was neither ttre one thing nor the other, which was why Mach saw it as no more than a 'transitory inspiration in the history of science'.

This opinion of Mach's is now supported by the growing number of conferences, nowadays, convened to accommodate the increasing dissatisfactions of scientists with Einstein's idiosyncratic version of Machian relativism, not least on account of the well-known paradoxes that the 'Einstein separation' generates when employed in contexts of quantum physics.

It offers testimony to the fact that despite the well-known practical scientific advances made by Einstein and their proof of the undoubted genius of the man, the philosophical transition from metaphysical materialism to true relationism - that is, from a classical matter-based to a truly empirical information-based science - remains to be completed.

I see the business of this conference, as being to break that chronic deadlock in current theoretical physics and allow the subject to continue its rational progress towards the consistent philosophical position indicated by Mach. Finally, it needs to be said that only in the context of Machian relationism can the title of this conference make any sense.

Whatever Happened to Real Physics?

N. Vivian Pope — Swansea Science Café, 31st August, 2005

Transcript of a talk delivered to the Science Café, at the Dylan Thomas Centre, Swansea UK.

Preamble: It is reported that Niels Bohr, after a lecture, said to his audience: 'Anyone who is not disturbed by this cannot have understood a word I've said.' All the really significant moves in the development of science have been heresies in their time, and heresies are always profoundly disturbing. This paper presents a heresy in the face of orthodox Modern Physics. To reflect what Bohr stated, may I say that anyone who is not disturbed by it cannot have understood a word I've said.

What is Physics?

Physics started out as the commonsense study of the whys and wherefores of material events and processes. Its aim was to answer questions like: Why does this piece of matter push or pull that other piece? What forces are involved? How are these forces measured? … and so on.

Now all this, of course, necessarily involves language. For instance, what are those forces to be called? Ordinary contact forces present no problem here. Everyday words like 'hit', 'knock', 'strike', 'smack', 'smash' etc., readily serve to describe those kinds of contact. But what about those non-contact forces that are manifest when, say, a piece of amber is rubbed by fur, as a result of which it attracts bits of paper and fluff? From the people who first studied this phenomenon we have the name 'electric force', from the Greek word for amber, elektra. And what about the force exerted by the lodestone on pieces of iron, or in orientating itself in the manner of a compass? This other non-contact force was distinguished as 'magnetic', from the Greek word magnes for the lodestone discovered in Magnesia in ancient Asia Minor. And not least, of course, is the force whereby all material objects press on the earth in what is manifest as weight. In both its contact and its non-contact manifestations — as, for instance, in the case of objects falling from a height — this was called 'gravity', from the Latin gravis for weight.

In this way, physics started and evolved as the classifying, dividing and categorising, by creative uses of language, of directly perceived physical phenomena. Note that in physics, as in all sectors of science, the efficacy of the whole process of development is crucially dependent on the best, most efficient and economical use of language. Unfortunately, for many physicists this utter dependence of their subject on language may come as a surprise. All too often words like 'electricity', 'magnetism', 'gravity' and so on are treated as if they had a life of their own, as though the phenomena so named appear in the way they do, already labelled with these inscriptions by 'Mother Nature' — or by 'God', as it were.

In fact, however, since scientific experience is always open-ended, or heuristic, the appropriateness of all this linguistic labelling is never automatically assured. The words and sentences we use, therefore, in scientific contexts generally, are always provisional, held on lease, as it were, pending their logical suitability in ongoing scientific experience. Failure to realise this essential revisability of physics language is one of the main sources of stultification in the subject, having sometimes inhibited progress for decades and centuries — even millennia in some cases. Added to this is, of course, the inevitable conservative resistance against revisions of language, even where logic may demand such revisions.

However, for all its flaws in this regard, the Physics I have so far described may justifiably be called the Real Physics, being the root-foundation of all physics, both ancient and modern.

What is Non-Real Physics?

The short answer is that it is Theoretical Physics, the physics of pure theory. Now there are two kinds of theories in physics. One consists of discovering, by informed trial and error, the best and most efficient interpretations of direct observational and experimental phenomena. In Real Physics, since every observation is, in effect, an interpretation, there is always some element of subjectivity in even our most objective employment of physics language. Theories that are used as working hypotheses to fill the inevitable gaps in our open-ended and incomplete physical experience of nature are therefore a necessary and integral part of all Real Physics.

In complete contrast to these pragmatical sorts of theories is what might be called pure theory. This does not deal directly with phenomena in the way that Real Physics does, but only indirectly. Let me explain.

Up until the late seventeenth century, it would have seemed strange, if not absurd, to have suggested that the phenomena with which physical science deals are never real but are no more than 'pale shades' of real physical happenings that are radically remote from actual perception and observation, that even on the brightest and best of sunny days, even right under our very noses, we are not observing physical reality but only apparitions of the same.

The reasoning behind this was, of course, the discovery in 1676, by the Danish astronomer, Olaus Rømer, and which has been confirmed by all subsequent observations and experiments, that all distances of objects in our observational frames of reference are also times, in the ratio of units (e.g., metres to seconds) called $c$. This was summarily interpreted as the 'velocity of light in space'. It implied that we never see distant things as they really are but only as they were at some time in the past, so that our seeing them is always out of date by the time the light reaches us.

However, despite the philosophical can of worms this interpretation creates, it has now become so deeply embedded in our language that nowadays we can scarcely imagine it being interpreted in any other way. Strictly, however, there is no logical necessity for interpreting Rømer's discovery in that facile manner. For instance, the fact that all velocities are distances divided by time does not mean that all distances divided by time are velocities. That would be like saying that because all Welshmen are human beings, all human beings are Welshmen.

Nevertheless, the commonsense facility of that 'velocity' interpretation of Rømer's distance-time ratio $c$ has established it as a fixture in language. This is to the extent that to think of light as having a 'velocity' now seems practically synonymous with commonsense. However, with further advances in our physical experience, that same 'velocity' interpretation of $c$ has become more and more a source of confusion.

For instance, anything properly called a velocity has to conform to the law of the composition of velocities according to which velocities add or subtract in terms of magnitude and direction. This means that anything not obeying that law cannot, logically, be a velocity. One of the biggest embarrassments to advancing physics, therefore, was the famous 19th century discovery, by A. A. Michelson and E. W. Morley, that $c$ does not obey that law. The logical 'bottom line', therefore, when all these observations by Rømer, Michelson, et al., are taken into account, is that far from being a velocity, the distance-time ratio of units $c$ is a pure constant for converting distance units into time-units in our observational frames of reference.

Nevertheless, following the Michelson-Morley discovery that unlike any true velocity, $c$ is constant in all frames of reference, contrivances were made to square this discovery with the stubbornly held precept that $c$ is a velocity, giving rise to the most abstruse and intellectually convoluted theory in scientific history. This is Einstein's Theory of Relativity which even nowadays no-one really understands.

In that theory, the separation between the observer and physical objects becomes complete and inexorable. This is due to what has been called 'Einstein separation', which forbids that any influence whatsoever can pass between objects faster than this so-called finite velocity-limit $c$ of 300,000 kilometres per second. In common parlance, this is usually expressed as: 'Nothing can travel faster than the speed of light.'

Moreover, for anyone familiar with the Theory of Relativity, it is plain that Einstein's idea of the 'photon' as a quantum particle of light travelling at the 'speed $c$' is completely absurd. The Theory says that the mass of anything travelling at the 'speed of light' becomes infinite. For a 'photon' to be a particle it has to have mass. So according to the Theory, the mass of the 'photon', at 'speed $c$' would have to be infinite, which means that it would have to be as massive as — or even more massive than — the universe itself. To be bathed by a barrage of 'photons' in sunlight or moonlight — or even the weakest starlight — would be, to say the very least, to be flattened out of existence. A single photon would just obliterate anything and everything. And if that is not nonsense, then please tell me what is!

Yet despite these obvious failings of the 'speed of light' interpretation of the constant $c$, that interpretation continues, certainly not by its logic but by the sheer force of tradition. And its effect is to dispel any idea that physical reality can be actually observed. This implies that any inkling we may have of physical reality becomes purely theoretical, with the world of ordinary observation completely severed from the alleged 'absolute world of matter'.

What else, then, can that 'absolute world of matter' be but, implicitly, something unperceived by ordinary observation and known only to some supposed unseen, superhuman presence, whose assumed absolute knowledge of that remote world is only accessible to mankind, if at all, through the vicarious agency of our expert theorists and mathematicians.

A particular irony and stumbling block, therefore, to any commonsense understanding of the Theory of Relativity is that while it severs the physical object from our observations of it, it seeks to unite the observer and the object by expressing the basic material properties and qualities of the object in purely observational — or relative — terms. This makes a complete conundrum of the Theory of Relativity in the mind of any commonsense-logical thinker; for how can physical objects possibly exist both 'relatively to the observer' and be separated from observation in that inexorable way?

The situation so described is incomprehensible, hence the notorious 'intellectuality' of the theory that makes it so inaccessible to ordinary commonsense reason. Nevertheless, despite this rather obvious duplicity — indeed schizophrenia — of Einstein's Relativity in that regard, this separatist conception of light has now become so ingrained in modern scientific and common language as virtually to block any further progress in physics other than along those entirely esoteric lines.

This is not least on account of the stalemate that persists between Einsteinian Relativity and Standard Model Quantum Theory, according to which there have to be influences between atomic particles which break the Einsteinian 'light-barrier' $c$ by being, in some instances, instantaneous. Attempts, however, to reconcile evidences for quantum instantaneity with those for Einsteinian time-delay have led to the notorious proliferation of theories upon theories to the extent that, as one of the foremost scientists of our age, Richard Feynman, has remarked: 'No-one really understands Quantum Physics.'

So the effect is that in one way or another, theories upon theories have proliferated in a manner that is now way out of rational control. Throughout it all, however, the object and the observer remain separated by a 'light barrier'. This is in the sort of pure theory that defines what we have here called Non-Real Physics.

The Alternative: Real Physics Restored

By contrast, in Real Physics there is no such barrier. Instead, the customary 'velocity $c$' is replaced by a pure constant, with exactly the same value and dimensions as in the formulae of Einsteinian Relativity. Moreover, in its own time (that is, the proper time) each quantum of physical interaction is instantaneous. Not only does this reconcile Relativity and Quantum Theory; it also makes the ultimate quantum components of physical objects the same as those of the light by which we see them, in the same way that the pixel events in a video scenario are common to both the viewer of the scenario and the objects portrayed.

In that case, physics, in both its relativistic and quantum modes, returns to its origins as the straightforward interpretation of phenomena — that is, observational data as directly presented to the observer and his instruments of observation. This is relativity in its true sense, as opposed to the entirely esoteric, so-called 'Relativity' of Einstein which his relativistic predecessor and mentor, Ernst Mach, regarded as a travesty of misuse of language in this regard.

Summary

What, then, happened to Real Physics? We have seen that the Real Physics is phenomenalism, in which physical objects, including ourselves as observers, interact directly, not indirectly as in the physics that followed Rømer's 17th century discovery of the so-called 'speed of light'. What happened to this Real Physics is that due to a discovery, made independently by myself and Herman Bondi in the 1960s, that light cannot, logically, be interpreted in that 'travelling' sort of way, that Real Physics has reappeared under the name of Normal Realism.

This is a new and updated form of phenomenalism, stripped of all previous taint of the 'subjective idealism' that was implied by the way George Berkeley originally presented it. Instigated in the early 1970s, at the University of North Wales, Bangor, not only does Normal Realism reconnect true relativity with its philosophical roots in the phenomenalism of Ernst Mach; it also provides a philosophical foundation for modern physics generally, which is currently bereft of any such solid foundation in either philosophy or commonsense logic.

Concluding Remarks

One would hope, then, that the very least you folks would take away from this talk is a firm knowledge of the fact — and it is a fact — that it is by no means logically necessary to think of light as something travelling across space from the object to the observer at the 'speed $c$'. Light can still validly be thought of in the old way as just the opposite of dark — or, in its full spectral range, as the information, in quantum bits, or pixels, out of which all our knowledge of the world is phenomenologically projected.

Anyone who saw the TV documentary, 'Light Fantastic', on BBC 1, 16th of August, this year, will have learned that this was the way light was originally thought of in the Muslim tradition, which identified light both with God (Allah) and all knowledge of the world.

Now, anyone who really thinks about it and who is not enslaved by convention should be able to work out for themselves that all the evidences that are claimed for 'light travelling' are even more clearly evidences for the fact that $c$ is a pure constant of observational projection in the way that people once thought about light. Far from being trivial, therefore, the upshot of the issue of whether $c$ is a velocity or a pure constant is the deciding factor in the question of which is most likely to be the real physics of the future: the physics of what we see (that is, phenomenalism) or the physics of what we never see but can only theorise about (that is, Modern Theoretical Physics). ■

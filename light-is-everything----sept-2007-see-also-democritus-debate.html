Abstract In 1946, Bertrand Russell, in his popular book: A History of Western Philosophy, wrote: The philosophy appropriate to quantum theoryhas not yet been adequately developed. I suspect that it will demand even more radical departures from the traditional doctrine of space and time than those demanded by the theory of relativity. Now, sixty years on, no such ‘philosophy appropriate to quantum theory’ has so far emerged. Theories have been presented in abundance, but no systematic philosophical analysis has been seriously attempted since the time of Democritus. Could the following be Russell’s long-awaited philosophy appropriate to quantum phenomena?

Part 1: Matter and Void 1.1: The mechanistic underpinning of traditional Physics The tradition of natural philosophy which pointed the way, right at the start, for what we nowadays call ‘Physics’ was set by the materialist philosopher, Democritus (circa 460-370 B.C.). For him, the sole and primary reality was ‘material substance’ of which, he reasoned, all things are made. Anything that is not matter and mechanical motion, he believed, is mere illusion (‘ideas’ or ‘conventions’). This view began with the teachings of Parmenides (6th - 5th century B.C.), the Eleatic philosopher who argued that by its very definition ‘what is not’ (i.e., literally, nothing) cannot be (exist). In other words, the Eleatic philosophers thought that the notion of an identifiable ‘nothing’ actually existing was logically self-contradictory. From this they reasoned that since nothing, as such, is literally non-existent (i.e., ‘is not’), then everything that is, or exists, forms a permanent, indestructible and changeless Unity, or Whole, which they called The One – what we would nowadays call the Universe. This ‘what is’ they regarded as the basic substance, literally a ‘standing under’ of everything, the logical substratum of all true existence. ‘What is not’, they concurred, was simply nothing, which made no sense even to speak about. Regardless of how strange this sort of argument may

seem to us, today, it was what led to the way in which our physical science, even today, is formulated. 1.1.1 The Problem of Immobility (the Eleatic stasis) Originally, this substance, or ‘what is’, was not just matter. It was whatever there is in all categories of existence, animal, vegetable or mineral. Just so long as the subject of a statement, such as, e.g., ‘Socrates’, can be described by some set of predicates, such as, say, ‘short’, ‘stout’, Greek etc., and linked to that subject by some variant or other of the verb ‘to be’ (i.e., ‘is’, ‘was’or ‘will be’) existence of that subject was assured. For example, to say ‘Socrates is (or was) short and stout was sufficient to assert the existence of Socrates, as the subject, or substance of that statement. However, for some of the more materialistically minded followers of Parmenides the only true interpretation of this ‘substance’ was that it was material substance, or matter, and this interpretation gradually prevailed. This meant that The One was completely immobile, because without any spaces or gaps into which the substance can move in order to change position that whole unity was solid, permanent and unchanging. This continuum of being was the archetype of permanent and indestructible ‘substance’ which became established, from then on, as matter. It implied a universal ‘motion-gridlock’, known as the Eleatic Stasis in which existence was jammed solid, so that what ‘is not’ was nowhere. But, of course, motion and change are undeniable facts of nature. This was a problem for the Eleatics, leading to the notorious Paradoxes of Zeno in which it was made to seem that all motion and change are somehow illusory. 1.1.2 The Democritan solution to the problem of immobility That Eleatic problem of immobility was tackled by Democritus. To accommodate the fact of motion and change, the nothing or void whose existence the Eleatics had denied somehow had to be reinstated. This Democritus did by introducing what has since become known as ‘space’ (vacuum or void) and interposing this between the ultimate bits of the Eleatic substance, which he called ‘atoms’ (in Greek, meaning ‘indivisible’). In this way, the materially interpreted Eleatic One was shattered into space-separated atomic fragments which moved and interacted

mechanically with one another in the void (the reified nothing) to create the changing forms and constitutions of things that are witnessed on the level of ordinary physical phenomena. The stage was thus set for the next two-and-a-half millennia of Physics as we know it [1]. This was the tradition of atomic materialism, or ‘atomism’, which was reinforced by the experimental findings of Dalton (1766-1844) and on which the basic sciences such as Physics and Chemistry have been founded ever since. In keeping with this mechanistic precept, it was supposed that our perceptions are caused by hidden mechanical processes among these atoms which cast off atomic ‘effluvia’, little images of themselves in the form of light, flying about everywhere to collide with things in general. These theoretical light particles, the forerunners of what Einstein eventually called ‘photons’, were conceived as the ultimate bits of information that impinge incidentally, on our sense organs, about an assumed mechanical and otherwise invisible underworld. Undoubtedly, this is why, nowadays, we find it so ‘natural’ to think of physical reality as residing in material ‘atoms’ and of our ideas or perceptions of this remote reality as, by comparison, no more than figmentary or illusory (i.e., as ‘mere appearances’, or phenomena). 1.2: The Problem of Knowledge However, there is something very strange about this notion that the information on which all our knowledge of the world depends comes from atomic sources of which we have no knowledge other than in terms of that very same information. In that case, how can we ever be sure that the world we assume is the source of all our knowledge really is, in itself, as we perceive and conceive it? Well, let’s face it, we can’t. This is a problem that has plagued philosophers for centuries in the name of the Epistemological Problem, or Problem of Knowledge. It is as though we had dealings with some person of repute, only to find that his reputation and all guarantees regarding it had been fabricated by that person himself. This is the situation with which our traditional science presents us, which is the question of

how much, if at all, we can trust our senses to represent to us the physical world as it really is behind the artificial ‘veil of perception’ that Democritus had created. 1.3: The Pragmatic solution In science, therefore, established as it now is, so firmly on the Democritan tradition of atomic materialism, we are always suspended somewhere between trusting everything and trusting nothing that our senses and instruments tell us. However, science cannot operate at either of those extremes. Unable to obtain any direct, independent evidence of the reality of anything behind the appearances, our traditional science is constrained to judge the validity of any one appearance only with reference to others, which is like a group of suspects in court, each vouching, on oath, for the innocence of all the others but with no independent evidence whatsoever to back their story. In those traditional terms in which it is couched, this Problem, which has occupied generations of philosophers, has never been solved. As for scientists, they have simply ignored it, regarding it as a problem for academic Philosophy alone, having nothing to do with their particular discipline. Science proceeds, therefore, on the vague assumption that, by and large, everything is more or less okay, that whatever we observe, in our best, most rational ‘clear-day’ direct and instrumental experiences of the world is ‘good enough’. It is taken for granted that the fund of sensory and instrumental information that has been accumulated throughout our scientific history provides a reliable conceptual template or paradigm of ‘realistic’ perception that matches the way things are in themselves behind the way they appear to us. This assumption that ‘seeing is believing’ persists even though there have been occasions on which, spectacularly, our customary perceptions have been proved wrong. One thinks, for instance, of the time when people were confident that the earth was flat, as it appears to be, that the sun goes around the earth, that the whale is a fish and so on. These confidences, of course, did not last. The voyages of exploration in the 15 th and 16th centuries showed that the earth is round, not flat, and Copernicus discovered that despite appearances to the contrary, the sun and the other planets do not orbit the

earth but that our earth and all the other planets orbit the sun. Like the demise of the classical ‘ether’ paradigm of electrodynamics after Einstein, these were all scientific revolutions. They have shown that regardless of whatever confidence science may place in the ‘absolute’ truth of its observations at any particular stage in its history – including right now, of course – that confidence, in itself, provides no guarantee that what appears to be so in the context of any contemporary paradigm, or ‘standard model’ of perception, will remain so indefinitely. In scientific perception and conception, therefore, nothing is absolutely immune to the possibility of radical or revolutionary change. 1.4: A logical alternative to Democritus Such, then, is the predicament in which, inextricably, scientific so-called ‘realism’ finds itself. Without being able to place absolute trust in even our very best, most direct observations, what price our knowledge of what we assume are the ultimate realities underlying and inaccessible to those observations? The word ‘realism’ applied to traditional scientific method is therefore, plainly, a misnomer. All our ideas of what underlies our perceptions are no more than just that: ideas framed in the language of our culture. This takes us right back to Democritus, the root source of all subsequent Physics. There, however, we discern an altogether neglected alternative logical route that physical science – or Natural Philosophy as it then was – might have taken at that historical juncture. Democritus had solved the Eleatic stasis problem by thinking of the primary reality as not just matter but as the conceptual trio, atoms, void and mechanical motion. This, for him and his followers, was the ultimate reality that underlies all our perceptions of the world, all else being, as he presented it, merely subjective ideas or ‘conventions’. This ‘atomising’ of matter was the inspiration which led not only to our Western science but also to the ‘materialist’ society that has grown out of it. Like Democritus, we still tend to think of everything but brute matter as ‘conventions’: that is, abstractions, ideas or just names in language (the view called Nominalism). Any other view, which puts matter into the same category of ideas, or relative perceptions, as the Theory of Relativity does, is therefore automatically

regarded as ‘idealism’, as opposed to the convention according to which matter is something absolutely self-sufficient, existing independently of how we view (observe) it or describe it in language. This later is, of course the ‘absolutist’ view of matter, space and time which has militated so strongly among those who have been opposed to the Theory of Relativity, right from its very start. These are the so-called ‘Dissidents’, mostly led by Herbert Dingle, the first to object to Einstein’s Theory of Relativity on absolutist grounds [2]. Such, then, is the scientific legacy of Democritus, as it now manifests itself in Modern Physics, where the physical world is still regarded as one absolute thing and our ideas of it (observations’ in Relativity) as something entirely other. However, despite the difficulty, at this present point in time, of thinking of the world in any other way, the fact is that thinking in that absolutistic, ‘God’s-eye view’ way about an underlying physical reality was never logically necessary. For instance, some contemporary of Democritus might well have argued that what is undeniably real, above all else, is not these ‘atoms’ – which, in the end-analysis, turn out to be no more than ideas in our minds – but information. This is in the form, basically of light, in all ranges of the spectrum from the deepest infra-red to the farthest ultra-violet and gamma. From light, both empirical science and commonsense project the external world in all categories of sensation and instrumentation. Since there is no other source that can be positively identified, this information is undeniably primary and a priori real. Whether it is supplied by ‘atoms’, by ‘God’ or whatever, is never more than secondary and speculative (a posteriori), that is, essentially theoretical as opposed to directly observational or empirical. To switch from thinking of matter to information as the basic ‘stuff’ of empirical science signifies a complete inversion of Democritan ‘realism’. This portends to be every bit as revolutionary, hence as psychologically and sociologically disturbing as might ever be contemplated – more so, potentially, than even the Copernican revolution and, in Russell’s words, ‘even the theory of relativity’.

Part 2: The Quantum Alternative 2.1: Light, the informational basis of all knowledge of Nature It is well known, from the experiments of scientists such as Max Planck and Albert Einstein, that light, the ultimate source of all our knowledge of the world, analyses-out, eventually, into minute irreducible amounts of energy called quanta. These are the basic informational units, or bits, in integer units of Planck’s quantum, h, from which, in all categories of perception and instrumentation the physical world is projected. Times are long gone since information was regarded as immaterial, that is, as something purely abstract in comparison with measurable physical quantities such as mass, length and time. As well as being the basic irreducible quantum of information, h is also, and essentially, the basic irreducible quantum of physical energy – or, rather, of action, which is the product of energy and time. Since Planck’s discovery of the quantum and the institution of modern Information Technology after Shannon and Weaver [3], information is now as quantifiable as any other physical property or quality. This does not mean that information can exist independently, as something floating about on its own. The concept of information necessarily involves an encoder (sender) and decoder (receiver), which are as integrated and correlated with the information itself as are the inside and outside of a box. Without either of these (the encoder or decoder), ‘information’ cannot signify as such. For information to be information, then, it has to be a double-ended, reciprocally connected and completely consummated affair. This means that the customary interpretation of the light quantum as a space-travelling particle, which Einstein named the ‘photon’, is a misconception – as, indeed, Einstein himself came to realise. Since the so-called ‘photon’ is supposed to be an irreducible quantum of energy (action), it has no portion of that energy to spare in manifesting itself in any way between a source and sink. Any attempt to detect it ‘in flight’ would immediately destroy it by absorbing, in a single flash, all the

energy that it has. To talk about a photon as ‘travelling’ in space is therefore to say something for which there can be no evidence whatsoever [4]. Moreover, to think of the ‘photon’ as ‘travelling’ like a projectile (the ‘ballistic’ concept) suggests a quantum of energy consigned on emission to a limbo of uncertainty where there is no assured goal for its completion, which suggestion is forbidden by the law of the conservation of energy. A quantum, therefore, cannot be conceived as both integral and one-ended in that way. This makes the supposedly space-travelling ‘photon’ no more logical to contemplate than a one-ended piece of string. Besides, experiments such as the well-known Thomas Young two-slit experiment reveal that the integrity of the quantum is never split in that way [5] but that it always manifests itself as a complete and consummate inter-resonance between the source and receptor, regardless of distance. This is also demanded by Newton’s law of reciprocal and therefore simultaneous action/reaction. Every quantum of basic light-information that signifies as such, therefore, immediately signals, in energy terms, the existence of both an ‘emitter’ and a ‘receptor’ and, in informational terms, an encoder and a decoder of the information. The integrity of the quantum makes it, in effect, a single event, common to both the sender and the receiver, so that on the quantum level the world is not split, in the way Democritus envisaged, between the material thingin-itself and the observer (between the two worlds of ‘matter’ and ‘mind’ as these respective categories came to be called). In the case, then, of quantum information there can be no ‘out there’ other than what is signified by that information itself. All space, matter, motion and whatever are projections from the information as directly read. There is no independent underlying ‘space’ or ‘void’ through which that information can be thought of as ‘travelling’ between the encoder and the decoder, far less at the ‘finite speed c’, as Einstein claimed. The sender, the receiver, the information and whatever it signifies are all part and parcel of the complete, energy-conserved phenomenological package, which cannot be said of the ‘photon’ concept of the

light-quantum. These quanta are the ultimate informational ‘bits’ into which our common experience of the physical world analyses [6][7]. They are, to observation, what the pixels on a video screen are to the viewer. In other words, it is from the patterns and sequences of these quantum events – but, of course, in an immensely more fine-grained way – that all the defining dimensions and qualities of physical phenomena, such as distance, time, mass, motion, etc., are projected. Even the phenomena associated with what is customarily interpreted as ‘light-velocity’ in experiments with the same, are all part of that same relatively projected scenario, as shall be seen in due course. 2.2: The objectivity of relative perspectives (demise of ‘solipsism’) The ‘encoders’ of this quantum information may be things both like and unlike ourselves, human or non-human, animal, vegetable, mineral or whatever. All these things, from objects on the microscale to those on the macroscale such as rocks, plants, animals, stars and galaxies, are sources of information for one another, albeit in energetic interactions that are ultimately reducible to these quantum-informational bits. There is, then, no way in which these objects that we ordinarily perceive as existing externally to and independently of us can be identified entirely with our own subjective perceptions, as some philosophers have suggested. They are definitely things other than oneself, but not in the radical way that leads to the classical ‘Problem of Knowledge’. Insofar as things interact with other things, they do so in terms of quanta which are as much units of energy as of information, and are decoded by their recipients according to their capacities, no matter how limited in some cases (obviously, a pebble and a porpoise have different capacities in that respect). Nevertheless, distinct though they may be from ourselves and one another, these interagents can be quite sensibly thought of as residing all in one and the same inter-communicational network in which oneself is no more than just one finite point-perspective among countless others, all distributed with respect to one another in the way that the sciences of geometry and geography describe. As A. J. Ayer neatly put it,

Places are places in a visual field and the times those that furnish the temporal order of experiencesWe can identify a sense-field by reference not only to its own character but also that of its neighbours, and if necessary to that of their neighbours and so on [8].

What Ayer was describing here is, of course, the essentially communal nature of perspective. Indeed, any single, completely isolated self-sufficient personal and private

perspective is impossible to imagine [9]. As perception

psychologists know very well, all perception takes place in the context of one’s knowledge of others and how they respond to the world and to ourselves. Without that communal connection, there can be no perception as we know it. This solves the alleged ‘Problem of Knowledge’ by the simple process of systematically comparing and contrasting the various individual sensory and instrumental perspectives provided by other sources, other things, other animals and other people. And, as modern philosophers like Austin, Ryle, Wittgenstein, et al. have shown, the way we perceive and conceive is vitally dependent on language [10]. Language, in all those inter-translatable forms known to mankind, is our ultimate connection with the logical language of natural reality, or logos, as the Greeks called it [11]. 2.3: Normal Realism, or Objective Empiricism In Philosophical circles, this global norm of purely commonsense objective empiricism has been called Normal Realism [12]. The way in which this realism differs from Classical scientific realism is that in Normal Realism it scarcely, if ever, occurs to us that the world we observe in everyday life may not be the same world as that occupied by those objects themselves and their various parts as described in our best uses of commonsense language. Animals don’t make metaphysical projections as to the existence of any world radically different from that of their ordinary experience, nor would we but for the peculiar quirk in our social history that has so artificially alienated what is conceived as ‘physical reality’ from ordinary ‘observation’. This dualistic metaphysic, arbitrarily introduced by Democritus and established in Western physics by René Descartes [13], makes us think of ‘physical reality’ as lying always and inaccessibly behind and beyond normal everyday appearances. Democritus and Descartes both

thought of observation as a characteristic purely of ‘mind’, which they regarded as synonymous with ‘illusion’ and human subjectivity in general. The famous Theory of Relativity, especially in the hands of Mach, its philosophical originator and Einstein’s mentor, should have disposed of that artificial partition between ‘Mind’ and ‘Matter’ in our scientific dealings with nature. So, also, should Quantum Theory. These theories, together, would surely have removed that dualistic divide had it not been for Einstein’s idiosyncratic interpretation of the fundamental constant, c, as the ‘speed of the photon’ thus perpetuating, in Modern Physics, a relic of the very dualism that relativity logically eradicates. 2.4: Einstein’s Most Basic Mistake: the ‘velocity of light in vacuo’ Nor was Mach the only one to challenge Democritan materialism and its Cartesian spin-off, mind-matter dualism. Eighteenth century phenomenalists such as George Berkeley (1685-1753), David Hume (1711-1776), Immanuel Kant (1724-1804) sought to oppose it. Unfortunately, in their reactionary efforts to avoid it they fell right into the trap of subjective idealism, the very opposite of Democritan materialism (material monism) [14]. What was conspicuously lacking from that nascent phenomenalism was, surprisingly, any serious attempt to deal satisfactorily with the vital issue of the ‘speed of light’ in connection with Römer’s earlier discovery, in 1676. Even the later, more developed phenomenalism of Ernst Mach (1838-1916) failed to deal adequately with this altogether crucial problem. The problem arose as a result of Römer’s studies of the moons of Jupiter. As he reasoned, the orbits of that planet’s principal satellite, Io, virtually constitutes a distant ‘clock’, whose ticks (eclipses by the parent planet) should occur with a pendulum-like regularity. What he found, however, was that these periods slowed as the distance of Jupiter from earth increased, and advanced as the distance decreased, all with a hundred percent correlation. This ratio of distance to time was measured by Römer and, with later refinements, became known as the constant c, of 300,000 metres (or 186,000 miles) to the second. It was this constant, c which from then on, notably in Einsteinian Relativity, was

interpreted as ‘the constant velocity of light in vacuo’. What Römer actually discovered, of course, was not ‘the speed of light’ but simply a constant numerical ratio between the respective conventional units of distance-measure (miles or kilometres) and those of time-measure (seconds, minutes, etc.). This was similar to a discovery, if it can be imagined, that there are 39.37 conventional inches in one conventional metre. In other words, the discovery was that in the phenomenon of distance, the space-measure is also a time-measure in that constant ratio of different units. Now, logically, the fact that all velocities are ratios of distance-measure to time-measure does not imply that all ratios of distance-measure to time-measure are velocities, which would be like saying that because all vertebrates are animals, all animals are vertebrates. To assume, therefore, that Römer’s discovery was necessarily that of the ‘speed of light’ was not only an over-elaboration of the true fact but also, logically speaking, a fallacy, a fallacy that has remained at the centre of modern relativistic physics right up to this present day. The logical alternative to Einstein’s concept of c as the ‘constant velocity of light in vacuo’ was, arguably, first suggested by Herman Bondi. In his book [15] Bondi writes: Any attempt to measure the velocity of light is . . . not an attempt at measuring the velocity of light but an attempt at ascertaining the length of the standard metre in Paris in terms of time-units.

This interpretation of c, as no more than what Bondi has called a dimensional ‘conversion factor’, is the same, in principle as the Normal Realist interpretation of c as purely a dimensional constant. This interpretation, had also been advanced independently by Pope and concurred between him and Bondi in 1985 [16]. Concurred also was that in both of these renderings, relativity theory is very much simplified [17]. Indeed, the only difference between Bondi and Pope on this point was, as it transpired, that whereas Bondi found that jettisoning the ‘velocity’ interpretation of c made it far easier to teach Relativity to his students, Pope found that it made Relativity not only much more logical and easy to understand but also that it fitted Relativity perfectly into the relativistic philosophy of Einstein’s

mentor and critic, Ernst Mach. So it seems that if, instead of departing from Mach’s relativism by his unnecessary interpretation of c as a ‘velocity’, Einstein had followed truly in Mach’s phenomenalist footsteps, he would have adopted the alternative logical interpretation of c as simply a dimensional scale-constant. Not only would this have massively simplified and de-mystified his theory but also it would remove the notorious conflict between Relativity and Quantum theory known as the EPR paradox [18]. If that cooperation between Mach and Einstein had taken place in its natural way, Mach’s failure to deal with Römer’s discovery would have been made good and Phenomenalism would surely have come into its own as a clear relativistic alternative to classical absolutism. Democritan materialism and the truncated Subjective-Idealist reaction to it would have become things of the past. The world of direct observation and the world of physics that Descartes had theoretically divorced from each other would have been reunited, within a common understanding based on a de-intellectualised language-system applicable throughout both science and commonsense. Unfortunately for that prospect, however, the separatist ‘light-speed’, or ‘travelling photon’, interpretation of the distance-time constant c has prevailed to the extent of being now virtually ingrained in our language, which makes us think automatically, in the Democritan/Cartesian way according to which we never observe something as it actually is but only ‘as it was’ due to the time taken for the light to reach us from the object. By contrast, in Normal Realism, whatever something is, what it is composed of, what it does, what processes it is involved in, etc., etc., is all observationally ‘up front’, projected from quantum information directly or instrumentally and communicated in common language. Any processes conceived as preceding and ‘leading to’ these presentations, such as, the assumed ‘time taken for light to reach us in proportion to the distance from the source’, are backwards extrapolations or retrospective inferences from the information supplied by light itself. For instance, the common conception of the sun as being ‘eight minutes away’ and our next nearest star as ‘four light-years away’ can be formulated only, in the first place, as projections made from information as

presented in direct perception. This was the way in which Römer discovered the distance-time constant c conventionally interpreted as the ‘speed of light’. The same applies, of course, by implication, to all other observational and experimental methods of measuring that same constant. Dispensing with dualism means that all the defining properties, qualities, etc., of things are there to be discovered by direct inspection in ordinary macrophenomenal experience, using the appropriate tools and the best tried and tested practical scientific methods in paradigm instances of perception. All the things we can claim to know about analyse-out to those terms. But, of course, such ‘direct inspection’ of anything does not automatically guarantee its reality. All observations, no matter how meticulously ‘scientific’ they might be, have to be analysed for logical coherence and consistency of interpretation in the context of the developing structure of language in general. When this is carried out to exhaustion, having satisfied all the logical and observational criteria, there remains no sensible question as to what might lie ‘behind’, ‘beyond’ or ‘beneath’ that ultimate level, in the way of hidden ‘mechanisms’ or ‘causes’ responsible for what there is. As J. L. Austin has put it: ‘If that is not a tree (a duck or a barn) then I don’t know what is.’ It never makes sense, he argued, to declare that what we are seeing in paradigm cases of perception is an impression or an image of such an object –unless, of course, we are thinking of that object as seen in a mirror, in a painting, a photograph, or suchlike. On directly seeing a duck, says Austin, what person who is not either a philosopher or insane – or both - would normally say that what he sees is an ‘image’ of a duck? Besides, he says, we are scarcely, if ever, really fooled by natural mirages or by illusions. Such misperceptions are always, in the end-analysis, especially in consultation with others, judged illusory or delusory with reference to the normal or paradigm instances of objective perception [19]. By that same token, the only causes or mechanisms it makes sense to contemplate in Normal Realism are those that can be directly examined and logically analysed on ordinary levels of engineering, chemistry, agriculture, biology, geology and, ultimately, practical physics.

Theoretical speculations as to what underlies that terminal of logical analysis as the hidden ‘causes’ of physical phenomena in general is not physics but metaphysics. As soon as we are satisfied that our perceptions make good logical sense, such speculations as to what lies ‘beyond’ those perceptions are as pointless as a musician seeking to tune his instrument beyond what everyone agrees is ‘perfect pitch’. 2.5: The quantum limit of microphenomenal reduction It is in practical physics, of course, that we plumb the depths of microphysical reduction. Towards that microlevel, unfortunately, the phenomena we encounter are nothing like as distinctive and assured as Austin’s trees, ducks and barns. The same goes for phenomena at the opposite end of the scale, that is, at astronomical distances where we may be dependant entirely on the sparsest data supplied by our instruments, hence on almost pure guesswork as to what those instruments are telling us. At those extremities of expanding experience our language is under constant stress, being prone to the necessity of radical revision in some cases. This makes it unlikely, to say the least, that the integrity of human language can be secured for all time purely by an analysis of ‘language itself’, such as the Ordinary Language, projected by the so-called Linguistic Philosophers, Austin, Ryle, Wittgenstein, et al. [20]. Truly ordinary language is never so finished and static as these disparagingly named ‘Language Police’ imagined it could be. In the face of expanding experience, language is always evolving and restructuring itself, not just at its scientific edges but often radically, and as a consequence, throughout the whole of human language. Nevertheless, the logical integrity of truly ‘ordinary language’ has to be maintained to some extent if human knowledge is not to disintegrate, at the instigation of poplar science, into a veritable ‘Tower of Babel’ of pure jargon – assuming, of course, that it hasn’t done so already. For that reason, Normal Realism rejects the Cartesian duplication of common language into, on the one hand, concepts of things as we perceive them and, on the other hand, purely theoretical concepts of what things are ‘in themselves’ underlying the actual phenomena. This means that entities of a purely

theoretical and inscrutable character, such as the space-travelling ‘photons’ of conventional (e.g., Einsteinian) light-theory, the ‘fields’ and in vacuo ‘forces’ of classical ‘gravitation’, ‘electrodynamics’, ‘nucleonics’ and so on, do not figure in Normal Realism. Such abstractions are regarded as not only surplus to logical requirements but also intrinsically meaningless, hence inimical to any prospect of a natural understanding [21]. Now, plainly, what we make of phenomena at the astronomical extreme depends very much on our interpretations of phenomena on the lower, microphenomenal end of the scale, that is on our current concepts of ‘atoms’, ‘electrons’, ‘protons’, ‘neutrons’, ‘gravitons’, ‘photons’ and so on. It is this propensity of events on the microphenomenal level, simply to disappear at the source and reappear at the sink that has led to the popular notion of the ‘quantum jump’. Being completely consummate, their ‘transfer’ is immediate and proper-time-instantaneous, which makes them appear to seek their destinations as if with foreknowledge, throughout, of their places and conditions of arrival. This disposes of any idea that these ‘particles’ travel freely in space in the classical ‘ballistic’ manner conceived for bullets and other solid objects [46]. As these experiments attest, it is the environment that both creates and directs these ‘particles’, not the ‘particles themselves’. The same must apply, logically, to microphysical particles of all kinds, whether they are envisaged as travelling over intergalactic distances or no more than the length of an experimental apparatus. As for those distances, there is no sensible way, in phenomenalism, of thinking of them in the absolutist, God’s eye-view way of those distances as a existing autonomously, as a precondition for the motions of particles. Instead, as we have seen, in Normal Realism, those distances are observational projections out of light information, received in large amounts. A single quantum event in the eye or a camera conveys, in itself, no information as to ‘how far’ it has travelled. It is only in statistical (i.e., information-statistical) numbers of similar events – as accumulated, for example, on time-exposed photographic plates or on the spread of cells in the retinas of a focussed pair of eyes - that we can tell whether the source (encoder) lies a foot away from the camera or at the distance of a star or galaxy.

4.6: The holistic laws of cause and effect. Because these ‘fundamental particles’ (howsoever we may prefer to think of them) have no powers in themselves, any notion of the world as we know it being the evolutionary result of ‘fortuitous combinations’ among these imagined randomly distributed and moving ‘particles’ is, in Normal Realism, a non-starter. The customary way of thinking of causality is that of one individual thing autonomously striking, pushing or pulling another. This presupposes that the motions of physical particles are governed by nothing but the classically conceived laws of mechanics applied to chance encounters among bits of matter whose positions and motions are absolutely determinate in accordance with these laws whilst otherwise acting independently of one another. This leads to the assumption, commonly attributed to Laplace [47], that an absolute God’s-eye-view knowledge of these determinate positions and motions of all particles in the universe implies absolute determinacy according to which the end-state of all the various particle interactions is decided from the very beginning of everything – wherever or whenever that ‘beginning’ might be. This is what inspired the poet, Omar Khayyam to write: ‘Yea, the first morning of Creation wrote what the last day of reckoning shall read’ [48]. However, on the quantum level, causality is nothing like that. For instance, all fundamental particle interactions on that level are indeterminate, in accordance with the Uncertainty Principle enunciated by Werner Heisenberg [49]. This rules that no interaction between particles can manifest, in one and the same instance, their positions in that event and their directions thenceforward, so that future interactions with other particles are manifest only probabilistically. This by no means implies that these interactions are entirely capricious, because probabilities are strictly law-governed. The two laws which govern the probabilities of particle interactions are, one: the Energy Conservation law, that no particle in a closed system may move in any way that would disconserve the overall energy of the system as a whole; and, two, the Second Law of Thermodynamics, according to which no exchange of energy between particles may take place in any way which does not increase the

randomness of future such interactions. It is for this reason that the Second Law is often referred to as the Law of Entropy, or the Law of Randomness. It is to be stressed, then, that according to these laws, in none of these interactions can any particle act autonomously, far less mindfully of any future ‘advantage’ to be gained by this or that particular action. This makes the natural phenomenon of life, with its characteristic anticipatory activities, a complete miracle. Logically, then, forbidding miracles, the facts signify that those laws are inadequate for explaining natural phenomena, that something is missing in the classical, mechanistic explanation of natural phenomena. What is missing, so far as Normal Realism is concerned, are two things. One is the discreteness and indeterminacy (i.e., quantisation) of physical action, and the other is the overall interconnectedness of things in quantum action-at-adistance that angular momentum ensures. Angular momentum is a conserved quantity, and what the law of conservation forbids is any ‘single-ended interaction’; that is to say, any interaction between particles that is not immediately and instantly consummated in the response of some other particle or particles, regardless of any intervening distance, in accordance with Newton’s Third Law of equal and reciprocal action/reaction. This, as we have already discussed in relation to the alleged ‘photon’, makes it unthinkable that a quantum of energy may be released from a source with no immediately assured billet anywhere for its absorption. This is why particles on the quantum level are regarded in some experimental situations as behaving in ways that suggest some sort of non-local, ‘telepathic’ knowledge of the state of the whole so as to fulfil the conditions of that end-state. A prime example of this is the above-mentioned two-slit experiment, in which particles emitted from the source ‘strike’ the target area as if with foreknowledge of the reception conditions at those destinations [50]. Quantum causality is therefore essentially law-governed, not by the classical mechanical, deterministic laws by holistic laws, like that of statistical probability – closely related to the rules of information theory. As we have seen, the Law of the Conservation of Energy forbids any particle to act capriciously in

its own right, and the Second Law of Thermodynamics, or Law of Entropy ensures that no particle interactions may take place other than within the jurisdiction of that law of randomness. This Law makes profound sense. If the particle events were not random, then there would be no such thing as Charles’ Law [51] ensuring a regular distribution of pressure on the walls of a container, such as a steam boiler or cylinder. If this were not so, then designers of engines would not know whether the highly pressurised molecules of fuel in a cylinder would push the piston out or suck it in. There could be no certainty as to whether an engine would run forward or backward, or whether a kettle of water placed on a gas ring or in a refrigerator would boil or freeze. In that case, not only would engineering as we know it be impossible but so would every process that leads to and sustains life itself. The assumption, therefore, that life can evolve out of pure randomness ‘eats its own tail’. For life to be possible, there has to be a strictly defined sequence of causality, and there cannot be causality without the conservation of basic randomness that is assured by the second law of thermodynamics which, together with the energyconservation law, governs all mechanical phenomena. It is to be noted that all these laws, of entropy, energy conservation, the conservation of angular momentum and so on are, essentially, holistic laws. They all state, in effect, that the whole determines the parts and not the parts the whole. If the (ultimate) parts determined the whole there would be no assurance of anything. Paradoxical though it might seem, without the assurance of basic randomness (entropy) there would be complete chaos, with everything interacting autonomously and capriciously, with no predictable outcome. Typical of the scientific legacy of Democritus is the view of causality as directed always from earlier to later. Time’s ‘arrow’, it is said points inexorably forwards from the present to the future, with nothing in that future capable of influencing anything at the present time. This view conflicts, notoriously, with the fact, exemplified throughout the organic kingdom, that there are, in nature, actions of things, such as animals and plants, for example, which are anticipatory of

future states. Nor is this phenomenon of goal-seeking behaviour confined to living organisms. As we have seen, the statistical laws which govern inorganic processes are also directed towards the fulfilment of future states, in a way which it is anathema even to contemplate in classical causality theory. Consider, for instance the example of a railway engine. Leaving aside the fact that the whole thing is constructed by design, with the purpose of satisfying the needs of travellers, the steam molecules in the boiler and the cylinders have to act in obedience to statistical laws, such as those of energy conservation and entropy. Much as it may appear, then, that those molecules move at random – which, indeed, they do – none of those molecules can move in any way other than towards those future states, or end-states which are assured by those laws. The fact is, then, that future states can affect present states, which confounds the standard expectations of mechanistic physics. In other words, Democritan physics fails, as always, in the face of the phenomenon of futuredirected activity that is manifest in nature generally. However, not all end-states have to conform entirely to those particular laws. A pebble has no capacity other than to warm in sunshine and then cool down afterwards, precisely in accordance with the law of entropy. On the other hand, the same sunshine falling on a leaf conforms to the rule of photosynthesis, which is the capacity to turn solar energy into organic material in the process called negentropic, because it acts counter to the law of entropy, as do all the living processes that abound in nature. Some say that the concept of negentropy is spurious, that the whole process is powered by the law of entropy. According to this view, the system involving just the sun and the organic end-product – the tree, the fruit or the animals feeding off it – is a process, every stage in which is accounted for by the law of entropy. However, that law, in itself, makes no allowance for capricious breaches in the law at any stage in the process where the ‘arrow of time’ is reversed, not even for the smallest period of time. To imagine that it can is no

Wells, the hero asks: ‘Can there be an instantaneous cube?’ The incontrovertible point Wells was making here was that a three-dimensional object without duration is impossible to imagine. This means that every physical object has to be defined in terms of at least four dimensions, the fourth being that of duration or time. Now for the sake of suspended incredulity, the reader of Wells’ story has to imagine a whole history of events, laid out, statically, along that fourth dimension, analogously to points on a map. The time-traveller then navigates backwards along that fourth dimension, as though travelling along a road. For that to be possible, however, there would have to be another, fifth, dimension, which is analogous to that of the fourth which measures the progress of a traveller in three-dimensional space. Is there, in fact, such a fifth dimension. In relativistic physics there definitely is. In relativity, as we have seen, there are, altogether, five dimensions. First, there are the three dimensions of ordinary space. Then there is the fourth dimension, the dimension of proper time, which is the time of an observer relative to himself: that is, the time he reads by his own clocks and other physical processes. The fifth dimension is that of relative time, which is the time of an observer’s clocks and physical processes as read by some other observer relative to which that first observer is moving [58] . Now it is to be emphasised here, that regardless of how ‘abstract’, or ‘spooky’ these fourth and fifth dimensions might seem, they are, in fact, real physical dimensions, central to the formalism of relativistic physics. Take any one

of them away and the whole physical formalism collapses. So there is nothing speculative or metaphysical about these dimensions. The last two are every bit as real as the first three. Returning, then, to the question of immortality, let us ask ourselves, in which of these dimensions might immortality conceivably extend? The answer is, the fourth dimension, the dimension of proper time. In that dimension instants occur like the stills (still frames) in a cinematographic film, which are the permanent components of the action. The fifth dimension is that of relative time, which is the temporary cinematic sequence of proper-time instants, as in the running of the film [59]. It is the proper-time dimension, the fourth dimension, then, that is the prime candidate for consideration as dimension of immortality. We shall return to this later. Meanwhile, there is something else which seems undeniable. This is that whatever happens can never be unhappened. For instance, it is difficult – one might almost say impossible – to think that even if our earth, with all its natural and human history were wiped out, say, by the sun turning into a supernova, all those events that have happened were somehow cancelled, so that they never took place. Let this, then, be our first premise, that whatever happens cannot be unhappened. If this premise is correct, then whatever that observer was and what he may have done in his life will, in a sense, forever remain. One obvious sense in which this may be conceived is that in which those events are items in the memories of surviving witnesses to those events, or as some recorded history, in libraries, rocks and so on. However, in our example of the sun becoming a supernova, all those records would certainly be annihilated, yet it is difficult to think that even this would unhappen those events in themselves, that it would somehow make them never to have occurred. So the question remains: in what sense can those past happenings be thought of as existing? Histories may be true or false, whereas, according to our

premise, events that have truly happened, in this ‘immortal’ sense, have to be absolutely true, regardless of whether we know it or not. Now it is well known that in standard relativistic physics there is no overall time dimension that is shared by all the individuals in any manifold of observers whose durations vary with respect to one another with relative motion. Nevertheless, it is also a fact of relativity that if a pair of twins move apart and then return together, at the end of the motion they will concur, objectively, as to which of them, during the interval, has aged least and which has aged most [60]. The same goes for any number of individuals in an ordinary observer manifold. That is to say, as in any scale of variations there has to be a least and a most. Accordingly, those clocks which record the least time are the ones with respect to which all others are, to some extent or other, more and more time-dilated. This principle of the least time defines the datum time of the universe in question [61]. It seems, then, that despite the variations in duration among all the different observers, there is an underlying absolute time, the cosmic, or nonrelative, datum-time of the whole. This is the Normal Realist alternative to the ‘God’s time’ of classical intuition, which is supposed to be the same everywhere. So it has to be in this absolute sense, then, that those happenings are recorded and which defines the required absolute time-dimension in which, according to our premise, whatever has happened cannot be unhappened. But if all the times of the various observers differ, then where, in this absolute or cosmic time, is the present moment? The answer is as follows. When a star explodes, although the relative time of that event is different for all the different observers, the proper time of that event is the same for them all. This means that the present moment of the star is the time when it happens relatively to itself. If there were clocks on that star, then the time registered by those clocks, as read by the observer in his telescope, has to be the proper time, or cosmic time, of that event. The relative time of that same event is then the time of that event as

actually seen by the observer and registered by the clock of the observatory, which is different for all the different observers. The present moment of the cosmos, then, is that in which objects interact. The moment when I actually see the star explode is the present moment for both me and the star – in proper time, that is. For me, personally, the time of that event is whatever time I read on my watch or the observatory clock, whereas for the star it is whatever time its own clocks and other processes record at the time of the event, which may be vastly different from mine, due to distance, time-dilation and so on. Any cosmic present moment, then, has to be the instant in which all the motions of all the bodies in the overall angular momentum nexus interrelate, in the proper-time-instantaneous way that POAMS describes. In other words, the cosmic present moment is the proper-time instantaneous cross-section of the whole collection of bodies whose relative times are different. So again, it turns out that the proper time of that whole universe is the time that is required to make sense of the concept of immortality? However, we must recall that the point of intersection between all these dimensions, no matter how many there might be is the observer in the relativistic (i.e., Machian) sense that has been described. When that observer dies, all those (polar) dimensions associated with that particular point-perspective are terminated. Nevertheless, according to our first premise, his prior existence in absolute time remains in perpetuity. Whatever he has done or been involved in up to that point cannot be undone. What is important for religion, however, is how to think of this deceased individual’s whole life being available for review and judgment in some way or other. Certainly there is no way in which it can it be accessed physically from the present moment by means of any instrument such as, for example, the ‘timemachine’ in the story by Wells. Such fictions may be entertaining, but they fall foul of what philosophers call the ‘grandfather paradox’, whereby if it were possible to travel back in time one might kill one’s own grandfather, thereby cancelling ones own existence in the process.

But, of course, there can be no such thing as life after death in the sense of living beyond one’s life into the communal future. ‘Living or surviving beyond life’ is literally meaningless. If immortality means anything, then, it means survival, if such it may be called, in those dimensions defining the past. The ‘cutting edge’ of the whole set of dimensions is, of course, the present moment, which separates the past from the future at the point of interaction between the observer (interagent) and the rest of the world. As we have seen, this point of observational interaction is also the overall present moment of the whole nexus, albeit that it occurs at different times relative to the different observers. From that point forward in time, the sequences of events are indeterminate, whereas from that point backwards the sequence of events is fixed, or determinate. The continued progression from the one to the other takes place at the present moment in accordance with the rules of physics described in this book. And when the observer dies, that person no longer has any active place in that present process or in the communal future. His only place from then on is in the absolutely unalterable and (for us humans) inaccessible past. In what conceivable way, then, can the events in that absolute past be accessible or reviewable in the way religion requires? Not by ourselves, that’s certain, nor in any way that may be deemed ‘natural’ according to the current laws of physics. Nor, needless to say, can this cosmic time-dimension be thought of as concerned solely with human events, personal or communal. It has to apply to all past events without exception, from the microscopic to the astronomic. Insofar, then, as these deceased human observers may be regarded as subject to retrospective evaluation and judgement, it has to be within this very much wider, cosmical context. Moreover, this absolute past has to be defined, not in terms of just this one dimension but in terms of the whole set of dimensions which normally describes the world. This past is ‘supernatural’ in the sense of being physically inaccessible but without necessarily implying that it is nonsensical in the way that is customarily assumed according to the Democritan tradition of science.

So at the very least we seem to have established that there is room in a truly rational science for what is customarily dismissed as ‘supernatural’ or ‘paranormal’.

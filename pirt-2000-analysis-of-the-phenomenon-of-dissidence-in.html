PIRT 2000 Analysis of the Phenomenon of 'Dissidence' in Modern Physics

N. Vivian Pope — 2000

Analysis of the Phenomenon of ‘Dissidence’ in Modern Physics by Viv Pope The author’s recent contacts with certain ‘dissident’ groups of physicists has revealed to him a general crisis of indecision as to the status of Reason in approaches to the subject. The following is a personal analysis of that phenomenon and a suggestion for its removal. There are, it seems, two main divisions of modern physicists. One consists of those who can accept without any demur, an approach to knowledge in general (and scientific knowledge in particular) which confines itself to just one spatial location at a time. This is the viewpoint of what is commonly called 'the observer', which is not necessarily a human being but may be an observational instrument of some kind. In this finitist approach, the only knowledge we may ever have of other things and other people, their physiological constitutions, masses, thoughts, chemical compositions, ageing-rates ... and so on, has to be from what we personally observe of their locations, dispositions and behaviour - in short, from our physical interactions and communications with those things as they relate to one another and, ultimately, to ourselves. Let this be called the Principle of the Finitude and Relativity of Knowledge or, as it is traditionally called, Empiricism. The other division consists of physicists who reject this principle of spatio-temporal confinement and who feel that the only truly scientific way of describing the world is from a completely depersonalised, 'non-subjectivist' ('Realist') standpoint which releases us from ordinary spatio-temporal confinements into a realm of purely objective Reason. From the standpoint of this approach, true natural laws plus their accompanying measures and mathematical formulae must have absolute and impersonal signification. This is the approach to natural philosophy which its Empiricist opponents describe as 'God's-eye-view realism' or 'Metaphysical Realism', classically called Rationalism. Each of these approaches to knowledge has its own philosophical drawbacks. For Empiricists, for instance, there is the problem of how to avoid falling into extreme subjectivism, as expressed in the old Protagorean maxim: 'Such as appears to me is for me and such as appears to you is for you.' In this way, 'to exist' becomes coextensive with being perceived, as finally expressed in Berkeley's notoriously subjectivist dictum: 'esse is percipi' (to be is to be perceived). In that extreme case, the 'is for you' of the Protagorean maxim drops out, leaving only its first part: 'Such as appears for me is for me'. This leads, in turn, to complete solipsism, which is the

conclusion that nothing assuredly exists but oneself and one's own subjective impressions, making everything, in effect, a dream. Indeed, until recently, practically the whole of what is called Philosophy, as taught in English and American universities, has been little more than a historical account of the strenuous but ineffectual attempts to circumvent these subjective-idealist implications of Empiricism. Meanwhile, many physicists, disdaining all this philosophical fuss as irrelevant to their subject, whilst also vaguely intuiting the disastrous subjectivist consequences of Empiricism, opt for its antithesis, Rationalism. But that, too, has its characteristic problems. One of these is that whatever 'natural laws' and so on, we may ascribe to nature can never be more than laws of our own human devising. Another is that of maintaining a Rationalist view of the absolute

universality and continuity of the fundamental measures mass, length and time in the face of modern theories of relativity and indeterminacy, over which Rationalists and Empiricists find themselves fundamentally at odds. The plain fact is that, quite apart from the theories of Einstein, et al., if one accepts the simple fact that a pair of spectroscopes trained on each other will register reddened (slowed) periods when moving apart, and blued (quickened) periods when returning together, then it is plain that if one or the other, but not both, of these instruments initiates a reversing action, the period-counts of the two spectroscopes, at the end of the exercise, will not tally. This, of course, is because for the one whose attendant observer initiates the reversal, the switch from the red to blue is on-the-spot immediate, while for the non-reversing spectroscope that event is delayed by the time incurred by its remoteness from that instrument. The result is that between their departing and returning together the spectroscope which has not initiated the reversing action will have registered more slowed periods than the active one, while the one that has will have registered more quickened periods than its opposite number, so that on returning together the times of the two instruments, if they were synchronised at the start, are no longer be so from then on. So, insofar as a spectrograph is representative of all the processes, chemical, biological, mechanical or whatever, on the body that is observed, it follows that relative motion affects everything, from the rates of clocks to those of mind-processes and cell-divisions. This, of course, is what, since Einstein, has come to be called 'relativistic time-dilation', any evidences of which hard-line Rationalists are bound to deny – because, of course, it confounds any absolutistic conception of a universal space and time. There is, however, a third alternative which avoids falling into these polar extremes of Empiricism and Rationalism. This third way is a Rational Empiricism, which has been called Normal Realism. Like Empiricism, Normal Realism relates all scientific knowledge to just one observational standpoint at a time, But also, like Rationalism, it takes natural laws and their associated equations as signifying absolutely, or universally. Such a synthesis of Empiricism and Rationalism was attempted by Kant, in his famous Critique of Pure Reason. However, whereas Kant sought to make Pure Reason an innate, or a priori set of ‘intuitions’ programmed into every human being at birth (i.e., psychologically a priori), Normal Realism ascribes that same Pure Reason to the nature of which man is seen as no more than a finite part. This makes Reason no longer psychologically or subjectively a priori but a posteriori; that is, something we discover in nature (i.e., empirically) not something we impose upon it. In Normal Realism, then, nature itself is logical, in the original Greek sense of being a logos, which is the task of the sciences (the various –ologies) to uncover. Logic, in the sense given to this word by Aristotle, is then simply our evolved way of representing this natural logos in our characteristically human language of words and other signs and symbols. Reason as we know it, therefore, is a priori, not in the God’s-eye-view sense of the Rationalists, nor in the psychological or subjective sense of the Empiricists, but in an a posteriori sense – in Kant’s nomenclature, ‘synthetic a priori’. By this is meant that the logic of nature is in itself believed to be a priori in a manner which Heraclitus regarded as absolute, or ‘divine’, but that from our inescapably finite, human perspectives we can know this only by the well-tried methods of classification and division, implication and so on, that are provided by our language in the form which Aristotle and Plato labelled ‘logic’ and ‘mathematics’. In Normal Realism, therefore, the only guarantee we may ever have that nature is, in itself, rational is that irrationality (the signature of which is contradiction) is essentially human and cannot be ascribed to nature itself. ■ e-mail: n.v.pope@swansea.ac.uk Website: http://members.tripod.co.uk/Vivpope/ Postal address: ‘Llys Alaw’, 10 West End, Penclawdd, Swansea, SA4 3YX, UK.

THE PHILOSOPHICAL VIEWSPAPER

A JOURNAL OF APPLIED PHILOSOPHY

WHICH INVITES CRITICISM AND CONTROVERSY

DECLARATION OF INTENT

PUBLISHED BY PHI PHILOSOPHICAL PUBLICATIONS CO. REG. EDITOR N. V. POPE. ADDRESS: THE OLD GRAMMAR SCHOOL, BOND STREET, BURTON ON TRENT, STAFFS.

JULY 5TH 1974

Nero, it is said, fiddled while Rome burned. Nowadays, amidst a philosophical conflagration which is truly global we may find, tucked away in their paper citadels, highly paid Philosophical 'fiddlers', whose professional aim, it seems, is to make Philosophy the most useless and contemptible of all subjects.

Scholasticism, we feel, is the enemy of true philosophy in any age, and the present age seems to be no exception. Our view is that in Philosophy, perhaps more than in any other subject, the emphasis should fall less on scholarship and more on the things that are relevant to the way we live - or, perhaps, should live. Its aim, in other words, should be, by the careful use of creative reason, to maintain a continually developing understanding of the world that is revealed to us by the pioneers in all the areas of knowledge known to science and commonsense. In this way it should provide, in the interests of social stability, a reliable and realistic general synthesis of experience, which keeps abreast of the times and is not hampered by slavish over-attachments to personages or ideologies, past or present.

Men act in accordance with how they perceive. But perception, in the ordinary sense of the word, is no longer a simple and straightforward 'commonsense' matter. In addition to what is presented to his 'five senses' man has nowadays to deal with a mass of information provided by the various instruments which science has placed at his disposal, like the voltmeter, the telescope, the microscope and the mathematical computer; and in trying to understand what he observes by means of such instruments he often finds 'commonsense' more of a hindrance than a help. In trying to comprehend his experience, therefore, the philosophical person is thrown not so much on his familiarity with the texts of Plato and Aristotle as on his own rational ingenuity and inventiveness in trying to make sense of so much complex and often conflicting information.

The biggest barrier to philosophical creativity, at present, seems to us to be due to two things, first the above-mentioned attachment to out-dated ways of thinking, and, second, a kind of job-demarcation policy created by Philosophers and Scientists to prevent each one's professional encroachment on the discipline of the other. We declare that in our view any such academic division works counter to the best interests of education and understanding, and that it is, in any case, unworkable for people living in an age of space and atomic energy. The knowledge that results from scientific discovery cannot be neatly compartmented away from what modern Philosophers call 'commonsense'. The knowledge sneaks abroad, and where it fails to bring understanding it inevitably brings confusion. The most pressing job which the Philosopher should be doing, therefore, as a paid member of society, is, we feel, that of making clear all the philosophical (logical, epistemological, moral, etc.) implications of new scientific and social researches. Its aim, in other words, is to assess the general objective significance of new practical knowledge in all the various sectors of the humanities, thereby providing a firm basis for wisdom in the choice of actions which affect oneself and the community at large

Our view, therefore, is that Philosophy, of all subjects, should always be the most realistic and forward-looking, instead of the most idealistic and backward-looking, subject, in its approach to understanding. Its subject-matter is not (as is sometimes fancied) 'Language', but that which language seeks to explain and describe. Since there seems to us to be no present form of modern philosophy which fully represents this declared set of aims, the void may be filled, we suggest, by a new (or is it, perhaps an old) method which we have called Normal Realism. The essential features of this 'new' form of Philosophy are as outlined below.

NORMAL REALISM

NORMAL REALISM SEES PHILOSOPHY AS A SEARCH FOR TRUTH

It cannot, logically, be true that there is no such thing as truth. We therefore affirm that something-or-other (though we may not know what) must necessarily be true, and Philosophy we conceive as a search for this truth.

BUT WHAT IS TRUTH?

To this question Normal Realism replies 'Truth is whatever can be correctly and consistently affirmed or denied about what is (or what was or will be).

WHAT IS TRUE IS ALWAYS ABOUT WHAT IS REAL

Truth, according to the above definition, cannot therefore be separated from reality. Every truth is a truth about something or some state of affairs that is real. In other words, to say that something is true is to say not one thing but two things: it says (or implies) that something is (exists) and not that 'something' is such-and-such or so-and-so. There is thus nothing true nor false that can be asserted about non-existent entities or classes of entities -except, perhaps, to say that they do not exist. If I say 'The present King of France is half' I am saying both that there is a King of France and that this person is half. But since there is no present King of France my assertion that he is half is neither true nor false. The truth of statements made about non-existent entities thus remains logically undetermined. All truths and untruths are about what is real.

BUT WHAT IS 'THE REAL'?

The question of truth, for the Normal Realist, is therefore very much a matter of what us read (what is and is so) and this requires that we define, carefully and precisely what we mean by 'real'. Let us begin by saying what we believe the real is not.

THE REAL IS NOT JUST A MATTER OF PERSONAL

OPINION

It was once (and to some extent still is) fashionable for philosophers to argue that we never have access to any reality other than that of our own 'ideas' (sense-impressions, longings, etc.) and that what is real is therefore no more than a matter of purely personal opinion. This is highly sceptical and stultifying view of 'truth' and 'reality' stems from a fallacious assumption that the contents of perception may be fitting classified as 'ideas or 'impressions.' If perception is defined in this outlandish way as the 'smoking of ideas' then, since ideas cannot, by definition, exist outside the mind, then to follow that all perception is of something mental, which is of course absurd:

My rejecting such abnormal and unnatural definitions of perception as 'the perceiving of ideas' (whoever was bitten by the idea of a chore?) Normal Realism avoids these spurious and stultifying idealistic or mentalistic conceptions of truth and reality.

THE REAL IS NOT JUST A MATTER OF 'PLAIN COMMONSENSE'

Amongst the philosophers who reject the above idealistic, or mentalistic conception of reality, are those who would say that the real, far from being something either infinitely beyond or absolutely confined to the 'mental' realm of experience, is something which is both objective and immediately accessible (i.e., obvious) to ordinary 'naive' sense-perception. Those who stress the illusory, or 'subjective' nature of ordinary appearances, says the Naive Realist, fail to appreciate that it is only with reference to more veridical appearance that an illusory appearance can be judged illusory. To evidence deceit, they say the senses must provide not only false information but also the true information with reference to which the false information is adjudged false, otherwise there is no point in talking about fasteners and illusion. In this way, the Naive Realists arrive at the logical conclusion that in ordinary naive or 'commonsense' perception or have absolutely disemboldable 'paradigms' of reality which are 'incorrigible' insofar as it 'sides on sense to suppose that they are anything other than they appear to be.

We feel, however, that this emphasis on the 'incorrigibility' or obviousness, of ordinary appearances is not only hostile to philosophic and scientific curiosity (by seeking to make an 'oracle' of commonsense) but is also logically mistaken. If those who stress the illusoriness of sense-perception argued that all sense-perception is illusory according to the standards of sense-perception, then their argument would, of course, be self-contradictory, and the Naivist' refutation of philosophic doubt in the evidence of the senses would be sound. However, that is not what is claimed by those who regard appearance as illusory. Their claim is that all sense-perceptions is illusory according to the standards set by reason, which is a different thing entirely. The Naivists have therefore not dispensed with philosophic doubt in the evidence of the senses, hence their philosophical disposing of ordinary commonsense collapses. When perception is seen to be, not just print the sense-perception (whatever that might be) but perception which often involves specially constructed scientific instruments and sophisticated methods of interpreting information we see that perception of the world, in the modern sense of this phrase, is no very 'plain and obvious' matter. When this is understood the present barriers to philosophical reasoning are removed, opening out to commonsense the new cosmic and microcosmic vistas of modern scientific experience.

THE REAL IS NOT JUST A MATTER OF CONVENTION

Some philosophers argue (usually from the

Naivist standpoint described above) that since the real is (supposedly) immediately accessible or 'obvious' to ordinary sense-perception, complete objectivity in the matter of perception is ensured by the similarities in the way in which different people are disposed to perceive. But since it is impossible to perceive and therefore directly compare what one person perceives with what another person perceives, the only thing that is communicated, so far as perception is concerned is other peoples' reactions (cries, expressions of approval, disapproval, etc.) - in a word, 'behaviour.'

Those who regard perceiving and communicating as forms of 'behaviour' (sometimes comprehended under the heading of 'language') usually divide themselves into two camps. Those in the more 'right wing, or conservative, may regard perception (perceptual behaviour) as something conditioned if not altogether determined, by the conventions of 'Ordinary Language.' If my present perceptions conform clearly and unambiguously to what I have learned to call a 'pig', then what I am presented with is a pig, and that is the end of the matter. Our rejection of this view, however, is the same as that of the 'obviousness' or 'incorrigibility' of ordinary sense-perception. Pigs, like any other obvious, are no more than items in a kind of Natural History (classification and therefore re subject to the rules of logical consistency regarding classification and allusion. In fact, while we may think it unlikely that normal or classification of 'pig' may have to be revised, this is not so of object in general. The term 'fish' had to be withdrawn with reference to the whale, words like 'pilgrim' and 'caterel' had to be withdrawn from physical science. Similarly, we would say, on term in language is ever more than a tentative classification of experience which is subject to the rules of classificatory consistency in an ever-widening contact with the world, the name or use of a named or phrase is ever autonomous.

THE REAL IS NOT A MATTER OF INVENTION

The 'left wing' camp of behaviourist (linguistic) philosophers, on the other hand, are those who would stress, against the conservative view described above, the ability of men to create or invoke new forms of language. These are the radicals, or congressives, who would also turn the role of 'perception' being dictated by 'Ordinary Language' is too dogmatic and stultifying, and that in the pragmatical interests of human progress language is more or less infinitely revisable; that we are more or less free to choose which way we perceive and conceive the world, but that there are some ways more practical or expedient than others - the criterion of the real being always in the end, that convention or invention which proves to be the most useful to man.

As opposed to this we would say that in fact what decides the fitness or practicality of any human invention is ultimately always something outside language, which can be referred to (to use a somewhat economic expression) only as a 'something' - know-and-what'. Moreover, if equalizing were the only criterion of the real, then insofar as what is definitely false or illusory may often prove more 'expedient' than what is true, it would be consistent with Pragmatism to teach illusion rather than reality in any instance where the truth might prove inconceivable. We do not say that this should never be done, but simply that where necessary it should be the plop of the politician rather than the philosopher. So far as Philosophy is concerned, the gradual dispassionate unveiling of experience of reality is its end-sin - which end-sin it largely shares, we should say, with Science.

SO WHAT IS 'THE REAL'?

According to Normal Realism, then, what is is (whatever it is) in its own right, independently of whether it is known or perceived by the likes of ourselves. It is not necessarily central, far less an individual 'idea' or 'opinion', and it is no mere invention or convention of human language. Nor is it any mystical or 'transcendental' conception, but a matter of ordinary developing philosophical awareness.

HOW CAN WE KNOW 'THE REAL'?

So far as Normal Realism is concerned, all knowledge of the real begins in sensation, but is, as we have seen, not to be identified with the same. So far as the Normal Realist is concerned, perception is always sensation that has been presented before the far of reason. To the extent that reason cannot encompass all possible eventualities in perception, perception is never intelligible, but to the extent that reason is reliable in corresponding what is given, then so, too is perception. Knowledge of the real is thus the progressive rational basis of perception. If perception, in this rational sense, proves illusory, then it is no only according to new standards set by other, more comprehensive and more rational perceptions. All truth and reality, therefore, are to be sought in deeper and deeper perception.

This makes our ideas of truth and reality indefinitely revisable in the light of one experience, but something which, in the absence of contradictory perceptions, may be regarded as established sufficiently for all practical purposes. So far as language is concerned, language is never more than a finite representation of an indefinitely extended, indefinitely complex and indefinitely persistent, reality.

WHY 'NORMAL' REALISM?

The term 'normal' is employed because it seems to us that this kind of view is the one which is most normal or natural among practical and intelligent men (not purposed 'intellectuals') who remain untainted by the vanity of modern Academic Philosophy because it is the view which remains when all the philosophical pretentions and indulgence are laid out. It may be, of course that we are wrong in this assessment but it is consistent with the view outlined that we should accept as true whatever fits the case, and change our view only when its falsity is properly demonstrated.

In a series of articles published in phi (No. 3, p. 12; No. 4, p. 6; No. 5, pp. 3 and 13; No. 6, p. 8) it has been argued that this mechanistic notion of 'light-velocity' is by no means the 'correct inference' to be made from the fact discovered by Boemer. It has been shown that this inference is not only logically unnecessary but that it is the source of all the logical confusion that now surrounds the attempts of scientists to explain to commonsense intuition how bodies move in relation to light. Nevertheless, without this unnecessary inference, the fact discovered by Boemer is incontestable.

2 If light were something travelling in space in the way that we normally conceive of the movement of physical bodies, then in the measurements of light-velocity conducted from any such moving body (e.g., the earth) that body's motion with respect to the light would be easily detectable. In a protracted series of experiments conducted by A.A. Michelson and E.W. Morley, for the purpose of measuring this relative motion, the results were completely negative. As summed up by C.H. Blanchard (Introd. to Modern Physics, London, 1969, p. 276) 'The results of the Michelson and Morley experiment were reported in 1887. No subsequent evidence contradicts them. Some lingering doubts were finally laid to rest in an article published in 1955.'

These results, if one sticks to the 'velocity of light' interpretation of Boemer's observations, constitute one of the biggest conceptual headaches in the history of science -and forever remain so far whosoever does not take the esoteric analgesic of Einsteinian Relativity. This latter is the metaphysical assumption that the effect sought by Michelson does exist, but is undetectable due to compensatory relative contractions in length and dilations of time, due to that very notion.

Without this old metaphysic of 'light-velocity', however, the results obtained by Boemer, Michelson and Einstein fall logically and naturally into place, in the manner shown herein.

TOWARDS A NEW ECONOMY OF FUNDAMENTAL PHYSICAL CONCEPTS

What is now called Physical Science was once called Natural Philosophy, which was an expression of man's deepest-felt need to comprehend the logic of natural phenomena. Modern Physical Science, in pursuing its experimentalist policies, is supposed to have dissociated itself completely from philosophy. That supposition, however, is false. What has happened is that Physical Science has arbitrarily selected, from among many philosophical alternatives, just one view which seemed at one time in history to be particularly well-suited to the scientific freethinker, newly emancipated from the monopoly imposed on philosophy by authoritarian religion.

But times change, and what was once a bid for freedom may well become just another form of oppression. It may now be necessary, in the interests once more of intellectual freedom, to take another, longer, look at that philosophy which has become implicit in the pursuit of experimental science.

That implicit philosophy, which nowadays characterises Physical Science, is one which is identified as eclecticism. Under the philosophy of eclecticism concepts arising from the various fields of scientific research are not required to form a logically connected and coherent whole. There are, of course, different degrees of eclecticism. Some eclectics would regard it as desirable to have overall coherence and consistency, but would say that it would be an unnecessary burden to add to the duties of the practising scientist. Others would take a far stronger and more dogmatic view, according to which logical coherence is something which is, of necessity, stultifying, in that it inhibits or 'crystalises' scientific thinking. Then there are, in the extreme, those who have a kind of superstitious dread of logic. These see logic as a kind of bogey - a kind of intellectual fascism - which seeks to manacle the scientific imagination, or to place it in some kind of mental straightjacket. The High Priests of the Church must once have feared the 'materialism' of nascent science as these scientists now fear the rigours of logic.

But progress, as always, is against fears and superstitions of this kind. An objective consideration of logic reveals that it is nothing more dreadful than a set of rules for avoiding mysticism, for keeping language clean and tidy, so that in our thinking we are not continually cavorting, in negotiating unnecessary obstacles and tripping over intellectual clutter. Far from seeking to inhibit progress, the purpose of logic is simply to 'clear the decks for action'. (What other possible purpose, anyway, would it be supposed to have?) Those who neglect logic in the pursuit of science might just as appropriately neglect to clear the laboratory bench or to wash and to clean their teeth; and they might just as wisely oppose the use of logic in science as oppose the use of antiseptic in the surgery or operating-theatre.

The purpose of this paper, accordingly, is to suggest the possible alternative of a much cleaner, less metaphysical, approach to the interpretation of scientific experience than has been customary to date. For generations, Science students, in order to graduate, have been constrained to follow the strands of scientific tradition. There has thus been woven, by Science, a great web of specialised licences and assumptions which now almost completely obscures the natural logic that is so essential to commonsense understanding. Our aim (as an exercise in philosophy, at least) is to sweep aside these dusty cobwebs and, in the light of an old logic, take a new look at what remains. What is thus revealed, surprisingly, is a very simple set of logical relations between primitive conceptions such as 'length', 'time' and 'motion'. These lead straight to the modern concepts of relativity and quantum theory without having to follow the tortuous paths of academic historicity.

What is demonstrated, in other words, is that these modern concepts, which are at once so central to science and apparently so intrusive or foreign to commonsense, are in fact axiomatic, being implicit in that part of ordinary language which is shared by commonsense and science. Anyone capable of a little application, who can accept as an astronomical fact (without going into metaphysics) that 186,000 miles of space is equivalent to one second of time, and who can accept the ordinary logical definitions of 'mass', 'energy', etc., can deduce relativity and quantum theory without further ado. And for this he will need nothing more than commonsense logic and O-level algebra.

In tracing the process of deduction (see figure enclosed) from its premises to its conclusions, conventionalists may wonder what happened to the customary precepts relating to 'the velocity of light', 'field-forces', 'dielectric displacement currents', etc. - a fairly thorough knowledge of which is usually regarded as prerequisite to an understanding of relativity and quantum theory. It will not be a part of our philosophical brief to answer such essentially Scientific questions, but merely to point to what seems to be at least one alternative way of thinking about relativity and quantum theory, which makes such questions redundant.

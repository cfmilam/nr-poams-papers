Relativity for Non-Mathematicians

N. Vivian Pope — 2004

Relativity is about space and time. Why is it so notoriously difficult to understand? The reason is two-fold. In the first place, for anyone not schooled in mathematics, the theory, as customarily presented, is highly mathematical. In the second place, its consequences are difficult to accept, namely, that contrary to what we ordinarily tend to think, time does not advance at the same rate everywhere. That is to say, according to Relativity there is no 'cosmic GMT' in which everything ages at the same rate – not even for twins if they are in different places and states of motion relatively to each other.

Do we wish to see this proved – in commonsense terms, that is, without having to study deep mathematics? If so, then first we have to talk about dimensions. What, then, are dimensions? Length is a dimension and so is time. When lengths are measured in metres and the times in seconds, the system of measurement is called the Metric System; and when the units of time are also seconds but where length is measured in yards, feet and inches, the system is called the Imperial System.

To distinguish measures in one dimension from those in another, the two dimensions have to be graphically projected at right-angles to each other (i.e., 'orthogonally'), so as not to encroach on each other's domains. Examples of this are the dimensions North-South and East-West, or the three co-exclusive dimensions of ordinary length, breadth and height.

Let us, then, think of how we would measure the distance between two opposite corners of a rectangular field into which no-one is allowed to trespass. (In it, let us say, there lives a very large and dangerous bull.) Two workmen, Sid and Ted, are sent to measure the length and breadth of the field, from which their foreman, Trevor, will estimate the length of a pipe to be buried or burrowed, diagonally between the opposite corners. Let $s$ be the length of the field as measured by Sid, and $t$ be the breadth of the field as measured by Ted. The length of the diagonal, from corner to corner, as calculated by Trevor is then $t_R$. Here, in Figure 1, we see the field whose adjacent sides Sid and Ted were sent out to measure.

Figure 1: The rectangular field with length $s$ (Sid) and width $t$ (Ted).

Now, many centuries ago, a Greek thinker discovered, from knowing the lengths of the two sides of a rectangle, how to measure its diagonal. What he found, mostly by playing around with measurements, was as follows. If the sides of a rectangle are projected into squares, as in Figure 2:

Figure 2: Squares projected on the adjacent sides of the rectangle.

then the sum of the areas of the squares on the two adjacent sides is equal to the area of a square projected on the diagonal (see Figure 3).

Figure 3: The area of the square on the diagonal is the sum of the areas of the other two squares shown in Figure 2.

In other words, measure the length $s$ of what we have called the 'Sid' side, square it (that is, multiply that length by itself to give an area, any ordinary carpet fitter knows that), do the same to the 'Ted' side $t$; then add those two areas together and take the square root of the result (a cheap modern calculator will do all that for you), and that gives you, exactly, the length of the diagonal (or 'hypotenuse' as geometers call it).

The Greek who discovered this method of measuring the diagonal of a rectangle was named Pythagoras, and the method, named after him, was called Pythagoras' theorem. There's no 'big deal' in any of this, because what he discovered was something that can be checked-out by plain commonsense. The foreman, Trevor, knows all about this, of course. It was part of his qualification for becoming a foreman, and Trevor is no hot-shot Mathematician. He is just an ordinary workman who knows it simply as part of his job.

What Trevor has to do, then, in order to calculate the length of the diagonal of the field (let's call it $t_R$) is to proceed in the way Pythagoras did, which is to square Sid's measure $s$, which he does on his calculator, and then add the result, $s^2$, to the square of Ted's measure $t$, which, of course, is $t^2$. This sum of the measures $s^2 + t^2$ as read on his calculator gives Trevor the square on the diagonal, signified by $t_R^2$. He then punches the square root button (marked √) on the calculator and reads off the length $t_R$ of the diagonal of the field. Needless to say, in order to make the correct calculation, the units of measure that Sid recorded for the length of his side of the field have to be the same as those used by Ted for measuring his side. In other words, if Sid had employed Metric units, of metres, say, then so should Ted, and if Ted had employed Imperial units, of yards, say, then so should Sid.

Now let's remind ourselves, at this stage, that there is no abstract 'Mathematics' involved here. Every move made so far is a piece of plain commonsense, and the same goes for all those further moves that may now be described. Let's get back, then, to talking about dimensions. Length is not the only physical dimension. Mass and time are also dimensions. However, there is no constant relation between length and mass in the way that there is between one length dimension and another. But now, 'hang on to your hat', there is such a constant relation between the three dimensions of length and the dimension of time. The first to discover this constant relation between distance and time was the Danish astronomer, Olaus Römer in the 17th century. Studying the eclipses of Jupiter's principal moon, Io, as it disappeared behind its parent planet, was tantamount to observing, at a distance, the ticks of a huge pendulum clock. Now, of course, as the earth and Jupiter orbit the sun at their different speeds, they periodically travel away from and towards one another along the line of sight of the terrestrial observer. Römer noticed that as the distance between Earth and Jupiter increased, the rate of the eclipses slowed and as the distance decreased, they quickened. Since it makes no sense to assume that the orbital motion of Io actually varies in that way, the variation had to be attributed to the change in distance between Jupiter and Earth. From this discovery of Römer's was derived the unit of astronomical distance called the 'light-year'. Since this is as much a unit of time as it is of distance,1 we are enabled to measure space-distances in time-units of years, hours, seconds, or whatever. (For instance every metre of space-distance is 3.3 nanoseconds, that is, 33 thousand millionths of a second.)

Logically, then, measures of optical distance and time are related in the same way as any two dimensions of distance. This gives us, in effect, two dimensions of time that are interrelated in the same way as the rectangular dimensions of length in our Figures 1, 2 and 3, above.

So much, then, for dimensions. Let's now talk about motion. In relativity, motion involves three time-dimensions. One is the distance-time $s$ in units of light-years, travelled by an object with respect to an observer, another is the time $t$ registered by that object in traversing that distance – as seen in the observer's telescope, say – in equivalent units of seconds, and the third is the resultant, also measured in seconds, of those two rectangular dimensions. These three measures are as depicted, here, in Figure 4.

Figure 4: The three time-dimensions of motion: $s/c$, $t$, and $t_R$.

The situation thus described is therefore an exact paraphrase of that in which the field was measured by Sid and Ted in Figures 1, 2 and 3. The only difference is that $s$ and $t$ are now measures in seconds, and $t_R$, instead of being the diagonal of a field, is the resultant time, also measured in seconds, of those two rectangular time-components. Otherwise, the situation, dimensionally speaking, is the same. And Trevor's commonsense formula for measuring $t_R$, which, by Pythagoras, as we have seen, is the square root of the sum of the squares of Sid's and Ted's measures (i.e.,\sqrt{s^2 + t^2}$), can now be applied directly to the situation depicted in Figure 4.

And that, in a nutshell is relativity. What it tells us is that as a body travels a distance $s$ in space relatively to the observer, so its time, or duration is relatively increased (i.e., stretched or 'dilated') according to the simple commonsense formula $t_R = \sqrt{s^2 + t^2}$ as explained above.2 This formula is exactly equivalent to that given by Einstein's theory for what is known as 'relativistic time-dilation'.3 Trevor, the foreman, who had read about Relativity and given up in despair had, ironically, given up 'within half an inch of the treasure', as it were. He already had, at his disposal – as, indeed, we all have – all the commonsense elements sufficient to complete his understanding. It was just that the way Relativity is customarily presented, especially in its scholarly associations with 'Einstein', 'Mathematics' and general 'egg-headedness' had daunted him.4

In short, then, being a 'non-mathematician' is no real excuse for not understanding relativity. All the elements are there for the taking, in ordinary, plain commonsense. And if relativity is a fact, then it has to be a fact of Nature, which is for us all, not just for priestly Mathematicians. ■

Notes

1 This is a distance of 5.98 billion miles or 9.46 billion kilometres.

2 Note that no matter whether $s$ in this formula is positive or negative (that is, whether the motion is away or towards the observer) squaring it makes it uniformly positive. This means that $t_R$ is always stretched, never contracted, as may be seen if the rectangle in Figure 4 is inverted to show a motion of return.

3 If any Mathematician doubts this, let him divide the distance $s$, in the customary units of metres, by the constant $c$, substitute for $s$ the equivalent expression for relative velocity, $v t_R$ and then simplify the equation. The result will be the formula $t_R = t / \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$, exactly as in the standard orthodox Relativity Theory of Einstein.

4 What is so complicated about Einstein's version of relativity is that it is based on a concept (called Einstein's Second Axiom) according to which Römer's discovery of the constant relation between distance and time was interpreted as due to light 'travelling in vacuo'. Like Herman Bondi, we regard this axiom as unnecessary, preferring, as Bondi does, to think of this astronomical ratio of distance to time, first discovered by Römer, as a pure constant, rather than a speed that (altogether perplexingly) is constant relative to a vacuum.

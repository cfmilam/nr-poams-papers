The Social and Moral Consequences of a Possible Change in View of ‘Light in Space’ by Viv Pope What do you think would happen to our society if it were discovered that the light by which we see things does not travel in space, but that what we call ‘space’ (that is, the three dimensions of distance between objects), was in the light? What a revolution that would be! It would surely be on a par with the revolutionary shifts in thinking from seeing the earth as flat to seeing it as round, or from earth-centred to a suncentred view of planetary motion. Both these historical shifts in thinking were socially traumatic in their day, as challenging for some as it was impossible for others. And so it would be for the suggested shift in the way we regard light. If previous revolutions are anything to go by, then anyone proposing such a revolution in our settled ways of thinking would run the gauntlet of social reactions, even to the extent, in some cases, that unless that idea were smothered, its proponents might go in fear of their lives. How, though, could such a revolution as this be possible? How can the space in which we think of light travelling possibly be in the light? Well, of course, it can’t. That would be nonsense – if, that is to say, light does travel in space in the way that is assumed. But what if it doesn’t? What if the ‘speed of light’ turns out to be not a speed at all but simply a constant for converting observational distance-measures in metres into time-units of seconds and vice versa in the measuring of observational distance and time? But whoever would suggest anything so ridiculous? The answer is that Herman Bondi did in 1964. According to Bondi, what, since Roemer’s discovery in 1676, has been popularly called ‘the speed of light c’, can be regarded without contradiction simply as a constant ratio of conventional units of (currently) metres and seconds in the measuring of observational distances. In other words, according to Bondi, the observational distance of a body can be measured either in units of 300 thousand kilometres or in equivalent units of seconds. By this method of measuring, the sun is eight minutes, or 480 seconds away, and our nearest star, 126,144,000 seconds, or four years. This simply follows the method whereby astronomers have traditionally measured distances in time-units of light-years, light-minutes, light-seconds and so on, with the only difference that in the newer way the word ‘light’ in these phrases is dropped as surplus to requirements.

The trouble with thinking of c as a ‘speed’ is this. All speeds characteristically vary between differently moving observers. If a train is travelling at sixty miles per hour and a person is walking forwards along the corridor of that train at a speed of four miles per hour, then that person is travelling at a speed of sixty-four m.p.h. with respect to an observer on the trackside, and if he walks in the opposite direction he travels at a speed of fifty-six m.p.h. with respect to that trackside observer. This is well known as the principle of the composition of velocities. All speeds obey this principle. Anything that doesn’t is therefore not a speed. So if no speed can be constant with respect to differently moving observers, then if c is constant with respect to all differently moving observers, as experiment says it is, then, logically, c cannot be a speed. Simple and seeming unremarkable though the Bondian shift may seem, from thinking of c as a ‘speed’ to thinking of it as simply a dimensional constant, its repercussions for physics are altogether profound. They have now been explored by an association of scientists called the POAMS group who have demonstrated that all the practical consequences of Einstein’s famous Theory of Relativity, both Special and General, can be deduced much more simply than in the usual way from this Bondian interpretation of c, without having to think of c as ‘the speed of light’. Since the details of this deduction are already demonstrated elsewhere (see website www.poams.org ), it need not be repeated here. Instead, let us address the question raised at the start as to what the social consequences would be if that revolutionary change in thinking about light were to be instituted. Would we be able to say, ‘So what? I’m not a scientist, so what could it matter to me?’ But if our society changes, then so must we, otherwise we are left mentally disadvantaged, out-of-date and socially dispossessed. Did the revolution that Copernicus and Galileo created affect only the scientists? Obviously not. And, by the same token, neither would a revolution in thinking about light. What, then, might be the social changes implied by this revolutionary, Bondian interpretation of c, and how would they affect us as individuals? It would start like this. Sooner or later, some logical person would reflect that if light does not have a speed, then our seeing of distant objects must be instantaneous’. In other words, if the Bondian interpretation of the constant c is correct, then light cannot be something travelling from sources far off in space, as we have been traditionally led to believe, but is something that is right there before our eyes. But, surely, to think of light in that way is impossible! It would mean that all those objects we see around us are not separated from us and from one another in the way we suppose. There would be no distance as we commonly conceive it. So how is it possible to think in that way? The whole idea runs counter to commonsense. Yes, to current commonsense. But commonsense evolves. Commonsense can sooner or later accommodate anything that is logical, and to think in that revolutionary way is perfectly logical. For instance, it is well known in modern

physics that light, as we see it, is basically ‘atomised’ in ultimately discrete and irreducible energy-events called quanta. These occur in our eyes and other instruments like the pixels on a video screen, from informational patterns and sequences of which the viewer projects a video scenario. Those patterns and sequences of quantum ‘blips’ are the information from which, as observers, we project the distances of objects from ourselves and from one another in the form we perceive as ‘space’; and the way that phenomenon advances, as measured by our clocks and other processes, is what we perceive as ‘time;’. Logically, then, to adopt the non-speed interpretation c is not to abandon what we ordinarily think of as space, The distances of objects can still be gauged in the ordinary way from information such as retinal focus, comparative brightness and intensity, parallax, perspective and so on, plus all those ways known to the science of trigonometry, from surveying to astronomy. The masses of those objects can be measured by the way they behave in relation to one another (in the same way that the masses of the sun and planets are measured without actually weighing them.) Indeed, every physical property and quality that defines a physical object can be measured by these same methods. So, in fact, nothing changes. The world is still out there, with objects distanced in the ordinary way, and those distances are still times in the ratio of units c. But that c is no longer the ‘speed’ of anything. And because distances are still times, any intercommunication with things at a distance will take time in proportion to that distance. In dusty atmospheres, interactions with dust-particles will form ‘rays’ of illumination which seem to suggest that the illumination is ‘travelling’. But it is not. The ‘rays’ are no more than sequences of illumination, giving the impression of motion, like the advertisement signs that are lit by on-off sequences of flashing lightbulbs. The whole phenomenon is ‘digital’, like a great interactive hologram, and the ultimate ‘digits’ are units of Planck’s quantum h. So, if nothing has changed, then where is the ‘big revolution’? The revolution – and it is big – is that in the switch from thinking of light in space to space in light, science switches from analog to digital. In other words, the basis of our physical science is no longer intuitions about underlying atomic mechanisms but systems of pure information. What disappears is the old idea that what we see are the faded and out-of-date remnants of events that have occurred, in some cases, aeons ago, at sources some of which may no longer be there. What takes the place of that idea is a new understanding of what is truly meant by reality. Reality is what we perceive right now, happening directly as we see it, and whatever we think of as happening ‘behind and beyond’ those directly perceived happenings is pure supposition based first and foremost on information supplied by what we actually see. Certainly, no-one has ever seen light leave a star and then wing its way through space towards us or towards any other observer or instrument. To think, sensibly, of ‘light shining in’ or ‘being conveyed’ by the vacuum is not only practically but also logically impossible. For instance, a quantum of light is, by definition irreducible. This means that it has no

energy to share with anything ‘en route’ between the object and the observer or observing instrument. This makes it impossible to detect and verify, hence pointless to speak about or even contemplate as a travelling entity such as an ‘electromagnetic wave’ or the so-called ‘photon’. There is, then, no space, hence no time separating whatever we may think of as the ‘beginning and end’ of a quantum interaction. Those ‘two events’ therefore collapse into one. All space and time, as we normally perceive it exists solely on the macrophenomenal level, projected, as we say, from informational patterns and sequences of these quantum ‘pixels’. On the microphenomenal (i.e., quantum) level the ultimate constituents of bodies which we call ‘atoms’ inform one another of their existence by direct inter-resonance, with nothing existing or happening in between. And if, on the macrophenomenal level, those inter-resonances are between, say, on the one hand, the atoms of a distant galaxy and, on the other hand, those in the eye of an astronomer or a photographic plate in his observatory, then the distance of that object is a projection made, as in some ultra-fine-grained pointillist painting, from information-statistical numbers of those quantum pixels, in a way which would be impossible from examining those pixels (or paint-spots) themselves. Nor is there any logical contradiction between saying that objects on the macrophenomenal level take time to interact with one another and that on the quantum level they interact instantaneously. For instance, there is no logical contradiction in saying that in cinematography, the dimensions of the individual photographic frames or ‘stills’ are instantaneous, while those frames are timeseparated in the running of the film. Logically, then, time-delay and instantaneity do not necessarily contradict one another, because they can be can be thought of as interdependent components of the same action. As long, then, as we keep thinking of distances and times as macro-informational projections from time-sequences of instantaneous quantum pixels and not as properties of the pixels themselves, we create no contradiction. In all of this, of course, the emphasis falls on direct interactions with eternal bodies, as opposed to the purely passive and belated ‘observation’ of classical science. In the new way of thinking, at the quantum-phenomenal level every observation is an instantaneous and reciprocal interaction in which the object and the observer share at least one single, inter-atomic, quantum ‘jolt’. Reality, therefore, is not something we perceive belatedly at a distance but is something with which, at every passing moment, we are interactively and inextricably engaged. And if someone asks ‘But in the end, where does all this information come from?’, to this the answer is that it comes from nowhere outside the system. The system is self-contained, The only sources of this information that it is sensible to contemplate are the objects themselves. These, in the last analysis, are the ultimate encoders and decoders of the information that circulates within the informational matrix. When these interactions are fully accounted for, there is no metaphysical remainder.`

So now we come to ‘the crunch’, so far as society is concerned. This would be where the leading logical thinkers in the society would come to realise what a literally revolutionary conceptual ‘flipover’ this radical re-interpretation of light implies. It would be to relinquish the classical mode of thinking, according to which the basis of all our knowledge of anything and everything is no more than some purely incidental spin-off from remote atomic mechanisms. Instead, we would think of that basis as a matrix of pure information, and of the World as more like a Great Interactive Hologram than a Great Machine. An essential difference between a machine and an informational system is, of course, that a mechanical process is determinate whereas an informational process is indeterminate, i.e., stochastic or cybernetic. If all the characters in a news-printer’s font mechanically determined their sequences in sentences there could be no such thing as information. For there to be information, or news, every element in the informational sequence has to be discrete and independent of every other, as indeed quantum events are at the ultimate microphysical level. No difference, then, could be greater than a physics based on determinacy and a physics based on informational indeterminacy; and no difference could be greater than a society based on the one kind of physics and a society based on the other. The logical consequence, then, of this revolutionary shift in thinking, in the physics that lies at the root of our personal and social interface with nature would be that we could no longer sensibly think of our perceptions and feelings, our personal loves and desires, our wills and intentions, as mere inconsequential wraiths in the mechanical scheme of things. (‘Ghosts in the Machine’, as someone has said). Our personal and social moral frailties could not be blamed on blind and automatic, determinate and therefore amoral clatterings and bangings among what, since Democritus, have been conceived as self-sufficient material atoms. Each of us would come to realise – or be taught by others – that the responsibility for moral, civilised behaviour, and concern for our own interests as social beings lies with ourselves as free-willed individuals. The whole amoral tradition of Science which arose out of the ideas of atomic automatism and which survives to this day would have to be revised. This would remove the Materialism that has set Western Science completely apart from the humanities (and which left to Western Academic Philosophy the absolutely insoluble problem of how to reconcile the two). Unimportant, therefore, though it might seem to a non-scientist, the end-result for society, of a revolutionary change in interpretation of c, from thinking of it in Einstein’s way to Bondi’s way, if properly carried through, would be profound. It would remove the traditional barrier between the sciences and the humanities, thereby reinstating a seamless, commonsense joining-up of the two. This would be in the context of a rejuvenated, holistic, Natural Philosophy which would once again become the Educational ‘clearing house of the sciences’ incorporating, also, traditionally ‘unscientific’ arts subjects such as History, Theology and the Fine arts.

In the same way, then, that the atomistic materialism of Democritus spawned so many distinct societies, such as those of the Stoics, the Cynics, the Epicureans and the Sophists – and, in more recent times, the Romantics, all desperately directed towards securing some slot for humanity among the atomic ‘cogs and gears’, so the new holism of an information-based as opposed to the traditional mechanics-based science should eventually fall into its most natural mode of expression. Among those in the forefront of science, there would be developments more in keeping with commonsense morality and aesthetics, and artists who now all too dutifully depict the anomie and utter confusion of our modern Western world-view, would be encouraged once again to depict things of reason and beauty rather than simply shock and disgust the public with their desperate nihilism. In short, in all its various labyrinthine ways a society based on a science of nature that merges with the humanities is bound to be vastly different from the Science we now know, which, despite its undoubted material, technological endowments has become remote from all commonsense and humanitarian concerns. This has now created a global polarity between what we see as our materialistically pre-eminent Western culture and those who perceive us plainly for what we really are, that is, simply decadent. And the more morally bereft we become, the more violent and irrational become the reactions against us, as do ours in response. Nothing is more obvious than that there is now no clear distinction to be drawn between rationality and irrationality in our social dealings with one another, either among ourselves or across national boundaries. So, where is true rationality to be found nowadays? Not in our Western society, that’s for sure! And certainly it is not to be found among those who so desperately react against us. How long, then, can these global tensions be sustained? By what possible means can a World Society be held together? Not by any purely political formula, certainly. Only something common to all societies can possibly unite us. And what else can that common factor be but, by definition, commonsense. In the West, what we fancifully regard as ‘commonsense’ is something that has been gradually torn into fragments in the rift between Science and the Humanities. If these two main factions can be joined up, then it might not be too late to avoid global catastrophe. But, surely, you will say, this joining-up can’t be achieved simply by a conceptual flipover in our customary Western way of thinking about light! Well, if not, then by what else?

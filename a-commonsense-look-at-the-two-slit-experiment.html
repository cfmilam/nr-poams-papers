A Commonsense Look at the Two-Slit Experiment

A Commonsense Look at the Two-Slit Experiment

Below is a transcript of a Times article by Science Correspondent, Nigel Hawkes on "The Mystery of quantum physics" in the Science Briefing section of the newspaper, (17th Oct. 95)

The experiment that for Nobel Prize-winning physicist Richard Feynman was the central mystery of quantum physics has become more mysterious still. Easy enough, it defies explanation.

Every physics student has seen interference fringes cause by two beams of light overlapping with each other. Invented by Thomas Young, in the early 1800s, the experiment involves shining a light on to two narrow slits or holes in a screen.

On the other side of the screen, the light spreads out from the holes and the two beams interfere with each other. Where the peaks of the light waves coincide, there is brightness; but when where peak coincides with trough, darkness. A second screen placed in the right place displays the pattern. The experiment provides vivid proof that light is a wave.

But other evidence insists that light also consists of individual particles, called photons. In fact, the particles can be produced one by one. Long after the days of Young physicists showed that if individual photons are fired at the hole one after the other, and the spots of light totted up on the other side by a photoelectric cell, interference still takes place.

Here, then, we have individual photons which one would imagine must pass through one hole or the other. Yet somehow they are interfering with themselves. How can that be?

Quantum physicists would argue that interference arises because it is not possible even in principle to determine which of the holes the photons go through. In a sense, therefore, they go through both, even though they are indivisible. Still with me? It gets worse. If you put polarising filters between the holes and the second screen the interference pattern disappears. By putting one type of filter next to one hole and another type next to the other, it is possible in effect to "label" the photons, identifying which hole they come through. Then the interference pattern disappears, as quantum theorists would predict.

Three physicists have now added another refinement. Doctors Raymond Chiao of the University of California at Berkeley, Paul Kwiat of the University of Innsbruck and Aephraim Steinberg of the US National Institute of Standards and Technology in Maryland, have added a third polarising filter, which they call an eraser, close to the second screen. This cancels out the labelling of the photons and the pattern duly reappears.

From this result it appears that the photons can tell in advance – before they even reach it – that there is an erasing filter waiting for them at the other side of the screen. If interference occurs, the photons must be going through both holes; when there is no

interference, they must be going through the one or the other.

How does a photon approaching the holes know how it is supposed to behave before it even encounters filters? The experiment seems to defy the laws of causation, which insist that results must follow causes, not precede them.

John Gribbin, reporting on the experiments in New Scientist quotes Feynman as saying "Nobody understands quantum physics." Hardly surprising, when it turns up conundrums such as this.

That, surely, is a problem to excite the curiosity of anyone, let alone scientists. I quote it from a newspaper rather than a scientific journal because who better than a newspaper journalist should present it before the bar of democratic commonsense?

In response, a plain commonsense approach towards a solution of that problem might be as follows.

1. The geometrical potential for interference.

Let us, for the moment, forget about light and the technical questions as to whether it consists of waves and/or particles and look at the two-slit apparatus itself. Like any other object, this has built-in geometrical features. For instance, suppose that with a pair of measuring-tapes pulled tight through the slits (that is, bent nowhere but at the slits) we compare the distances – in centimetres, say – from each point at the source to each point on the screen. In this way we would find that there were areas across the screen at which the lengths of the two tapes would vary between no difference at all and any whole or fractional number of centimetres. If anyone doubts this, let him hold out his arms in front of him, with his palms together and with the tips of both middle fingers together touching a wall. Then let him swing his arms from side to side across the wall without moving his shoulders. The way his palms slide across one another, by more than a palm's length, shows how the tapes through the two slits measure different distances from the slits to the screen.

Let us now imagine that at positions across the screen where the differences in the lengths of the tapes are even whole numbers of half-centimetres we place two ink-dots (one for each tape), and where they are odd whole numbers of half-centimetres we place no ink at all. The ink at points across the screen will thus vary

between nil and double the density as the tape-lengths vary between those even and odd numbers of half-centimetres. This would give us an ink-pattern of "fringes" which is the same as if the source had transmitted light of one centimetre wavelength through the two slits onto a photoelectric cell or a photosensitive screen.

Now, of course, the wavelengths of visible light are measured not in centimetres but in nanometres – that is, in units of a hundred thousandth of a centimetre. (Centimetre wavelengths are more in the range of microwaves than of light). However our tape-measure model is the same in principle for small wavelengths as for large ones, in any medium whatsoever and regardless of their propagational speed. In this way, in the apparatus itself we have a purely static, geometrical potential for "wave-interference" without any waves or photons – or even tapes or ink – actually being present. Moreover, if we think of either of the slits as blocked to the theoretical tape-measuring process, the "interference pattern" is replaced by an overall, even distribution of single-density dots, precisely as reported with light in the actual experiment.

2. Probability

But Hawkes' article also reports that other evidence, "insists that light also consists of individual particles called photons", and that when individual photons go through the slits one by one they distribute themselves at the receiving end in the same sorts of interference patterns as with waves, which suggests that each single photon, like a wave, passed through both slits. But how can this be, Hawkes asks. How can a single particle be in two places at once? The commonsense answer, we propose, is as follows.

It is well known that a photon is an irreducible quantum of light-energy of specified wavelength (i.e., a single indivisible cycle of action) and that, being irreducible, it cannot, by definition, be split into fractional amounts. It either occurs or it doesn't. In probabilistic terms, this means that the probability of its occurring is between nought and one, with no possibilities in between. So the photon can neither duplicate nor divide itself, like a wave, to go through both slits. In that case, what can possibly cause the interference pattern, since in no way can it be the photons themselves?

The answer has to be that what interferes is the paths which we have described as pure potentialities for source-screen interaction. And that potentiality depends on the question of whether the number of half-wavelengths into which the routes can be divided is odd or even. For a photon wavelength to fit into the path without dividing, that path has to be an even number of half-wavelengths long. And if the potentiality of that path for photon contact between source and screen is exactly cancelled by the odd-number potentiality of the other path, then the likelihood of a photon appearing at that point is nil. In

conventional language, if the path is "in synch" with the wavelength of the photon, the photon will go. If not, it won't.

3. Faster-than-light interaction or "action-at-a-distance"

Well, that makes good logical sense but it is puzzling in another way. How does the photon "know" ahead of time, whether the path for it is "go" or "no go"? This raises another vexing problem, namely, that of so-called "action at a distance". As Hawkes puts it, "It appears that the photons can tell in advance – before they even reach it" – what is there ahead of them, whether it be the screen or some sort of barrier in between (and since a photon is indivisible, any method whatever of detecting it destroys it and is therefore, in effect, a "barrier"). However, every first-year student of relativity theory knows that in its own time – that is, in what is technically called its proper time, a photon is there as soon as it starts out. That is to say, its proper-time over any distance whatsoever is zero and its intrinsic speed therefore infinite. For the photon itself, then, there is no problem of "knowing" what's ahead of it. In the terms used by one scientist, Gilbert Lewis, in 1926, at the quantum-physical level the emitter and absorber of a light-quantum are literally "touching". In that sense, for the photon, whether it is "go" or "no-go" is known immediately.

The mystery, then, of "advance knowledge" on the part of the photon is disposed of by even the most elementary study of relativity. In that case, since the word "photon" suggests a travelling particle and since there is no question of "travelling" in the immediate and instantaneous relativistic quantum-jump Lewis describes, it seems surprising that the word was coined by relativity's founder, Einstein – and no less surprising that it has survived among so many generations of students. In order, then, to avoid confusion let us swap the word "photon" for a new word, photum (in the plural, phota), which avoids any such "travelling" suggestion.

What, then, do physicists mean by "action-at-a-distance"? It means, as physicists describe it, a "spooky superluminal" (i.e., instantaneous) quantum interconnection of the sort demanded by Newton's law of instantaneous and reciprocal action and reaction in the transfer of energy between bodies. Physicists such as Alain Aspect and his colleagues at the University of Paris, Carol Alley and his team at the University of Maryland, and others claim they have verified this "spooky" instantaneous interconnection. But what is so "spooky" about it? As usually described, it is that in Einstein's theory it is axiomatic that no physical influence can travel faster than the finite speed of light, whereas these instantaneous influences demanded by Newton and verified by Aspect, et al., seem to be in complete opposition to relativity.

But, as we have seen, they aren't. In fact, as Lewis showed, that sort of instantaneity is supplied by

3

relativity, in the form of the intrinsically zero proper-time of the photum. What need, then, have we of any special conception of instantaneous "action-at-a-distance" when relativity already provides it? Why should it ever be imagined that relativity and quantum physics are at odds in that respect, when they so clearly support one another?

The language problem

The answer has to be that it is due to a complete misunderstanding of relativity – perpetuated, no doubt, by the recalcitrance of the conventional use of the eminently unsuitable word "photon" to describe the quantum of light-interaction. Scientists will say, of course, that such a misunderstanding on the part of so many of their fellows is highly unlikely. Should commonsense, then, out of respect for scientists, suspect nature of complete mystification? Most people, it seems, do just that! However, philosophers like Wittgenstein have warned us of the dangers of misunderstanding due to "misuse of language" – in particular, that over-intellectualisation of our descriptions of phenomena which his students called "scientism". As commonsense sees it, if scientists were infallible there would be no place for philosophy, so that insofar as there is place for philosophy, scientists are not infallible – which is, of course, why so many conventional scientists detest philosophy in general and "Wittgenstein" in particular.

Significantly, though, even scientists are now turning more and more to philosophy – albeit a "do-it-yourself" variety rather than the proper stuff. So they must be starting to think they have gone wrong somewhere. As reported at the end of Hawkes' article, even the late Richard Feynman admitted that, "Nobody understands quantum physics". From the recent proliferation, in science journals, of articles of the sort described by Hawkes, it seems that more and more scientists are beginning to agree with Feynman.

So it might well be that the trouble with science is science itself and that perhaps an infusion of sheer commonsense is long overdue. What sort of infusion might that be? Well, in the first place, it would be to recommend the systematic removal of the word "photon" from the physicists' vocabulary. Redundant words like "caloric" and "phlogiston" were cleared-out, so why not "photon"? In the second place, phrases like "light velocity", "the speed of light" and associated jargon should go – or, at least, be modified in some suitable respect. And as for "light in vacuo", which is conventionally regarded as central to relativity, how, commonsense asks, can light possibly travel at a constant speed relatively to a vacuum? Besides, there are renderings of relativity, such as Herman Bondi's, which have the same mathematical consequences as Einstein's but are much simpler by virtue of dispensing with all talk of "light-velocity" and "travelling photons". Unfortunately, these logical alternatives tend to be swept away and drowned by the currents of convention.

Otherwise, commonsense is perfectly capable of accepting, and adapting to, the bare scientific evidence, so long as it is presented plain and not wrapped-up in theoretical jargon. And what should be plain is that there is no real logical problem in accepting that light can be both instantaneous and yet propagate at a finite speed. No commonsense person imagines, for instance, that the elements of a wave (like the molecules of water, for instance) have to travel at the speed of the wave. So why is it a problem that the quantum elements of light are instantaneous and that waves of them take time to get from A to B? By definition, no-one can observe a light-quantum travelling – because unlike an ordinary object, an irreducible light-quantum cannot give off light or any other form of energy by which its motion can be seen nor in any other way be detected. (The whole idea, anyway, of light giving-off light in order to be seen is a logical howler.)

In that case, says commonsense, there is no point in trying to look into a quantum jump, nor even in talking about the "distance" of it – as between source and screen in the two-slit experiment. On that understanding, all that can ever be observed and measured are sequences of these intrinsically distance-less and instantaneous quantum jumps. On the further understanding, that all forms of energy are quantised in the same way, it follows that all that we see around us, like the two-slit apparatus, the laboratory containing it and ourselves, our earth, the stars and so on must be analysable, ultimately, into events of that sort. But that, again, presents no real difficulty, since a commonsense model for this exists in our ordinary understanding of how, for instance, all the length, breadth, depth, substance and motion of a TV drama can be informationally projected out of arrangements and sequences of transient and comparatively featureless screen-events. Besides, to imagine the opposite, that everything is analysable right down to zero – to a continuum, in effect – is impossible, since no amount of zeroes whatsoever can ever amount to anything. To settle for the fact that everything is ultimately quantised should therefore be no difficulty for a commonsense which has not become completely corrupted by scientific jargon.

Slower-than-light two-slit interference

Let us return, then, to the questions raised by the more recent developments on the subject of the original, Thomas Young two-slit experiment. It is plain that if photo are instantaneous, then it matters not one iota as to whether the detectors and other barriers to that quantum connection between source and screen are placed before, in or after the slits. So that is another mystery which commonsense disposes of, that detectors placed after the slits affect the trajectories of "photons".

Is it a mystery, then, that "slower-than-light" periodic processes like electron beams and other particle beams manifest the same sort of interference in two-slit-types

4

of experiments? Since these interactions are manifestly non-instantaneous, how do those particles "know", in the language of the Times article, how and where to go in order to distribute themselves into typical, wave-interference patterns at the receiving end? The answer has to be that these particles give off quanta of some kind or another (photo, gravita or whatever) which manifest their emission, and that these quanta instantaneously interconnect source and screen in the same way that the photum does. Technically, that quantum is the kinetic energy of the particle's ejection from the source, divided by Planck's constant h, giving a frequency for that quantum much less than that of light and typical of an electron-beam of the sort which goes from the cathode to the anode in a television tube. What else but these proper-time-instantaneous quanta can both define, or "guide", the path for the particle and provide the typical two-slit pattern of their distribution at the receiving-end?

The advantage of this explanation, if correct, is that it avoids the confusion of talking about "wave particles", the convenience of which parlance to science now seems far outweighed by the confusion it causes, as more and more scientists now freely admit.

The toothed-wheel measure of "light-speed"

In this experiment light is beamed from a source through teeth around the edge of an extremely fast-spinning wheel. As usually described, the signal then travels on towards a mirror and is reflected back to the source, to be interrupted by the interposition of a tooth of the wheel which has moved meantime. The movement of the tooth in the time between the sending and return of the signal is thus taken as the measure of the speed of the signal, which is how Römer's discovery of the "finite speed of light" was precisely measured and confirmed (Actually, as Bondi would say, what was measured in this way was no more than the constant ratio of conventional units of distance-measure to those of time-measure.)

With this "finite speed of light" in mind, some people have the greatest difficulty in conceiving how light-interaction can be proper-time instantaneous. If that is so, they say, then if a light-signal is sent from here to Alpha Centauri, then that instantaneous signal will strike the mirror on that star some four years in our future and return to earth in a further four years to meet some measuring-equivalent of the toothed wheel some eight years on from the sending of the signal, so that the event of seeing that reflected signal and all that has happened in between is predetermined.

Now that would be a paradox if the situation really were as stated. It is, indeed, an extreme example of what is generally known as the "clock paradox", which many people take to be the reductio ad absurdum of the theory of relativity. All that sustains the paradox, however, is - no, not "commonsense, as some suppose, but the sheer recalcitrance, in the face of all reasoning and demonstration to the contrary, of the classical notion that there is always, in the cosmical

background, a single God's-eye-view time-frame in which all this takes place. With this at the backs of our minds we cannot imagine the complete dissociation relativity demands between the three separate time-frames involved. First there is the time between the signal being emitted and being received by the mirror on Alpha - which in this case, for the signal itself, is zero. Second is the time between re-emission of the absorbed signal at Alpha and its re-absorption back on earth, which, for the signal itself is, again, zero. Third is the time between the emission of the signal and its return to earth in the time of earth, which in this case is eight years. Any idea that these different times should all "add up" to the same time manifests a fundamental misunderstanding of relativity due to mental interference by the classical precept we are talking about.

That difficulty disappears once we accept what relativity and quantum theory are trying to tell us, that we can never be in more than one time-frame at a time, that from the standpoint of one reference-frame there is no way of looking into another without interacting with it in the terms provided by quantum theory and relativity. We then see that since there is no way of interacting with the quanta of that first signal "on its way" from earth to Alpha, there is no point in talking about it. Nor, by the same token, is there any point in talking about the quantum interaction between the Alpharian mirror and the terrestrial toothed wheel. To have any "inside-information" of those proper-time-instantaneous quantum-jumps we would have to be "God" as classical deism imagines him.

So if we dispense with that imagined "God" of classical superstition, then we dispense also with the "paradox" of these different times of different things not adding-up to the same time. We may then understand that our instantaneous signal "landing on Alpha" is not "in our future" nor anyone else's future but at the present moment in both places. Nor does the return signal reach earth "in the future" of Alpha but in the present which again, in that instant, is shared by both time-frames. Indeed, so far as relativity and quantum physics is concerned, it is only in those shared present-moments - quantum touchings, in Lewis's terms - that any one thing and its time-frame can interrelate with any other. What happens in the meantime, in each of those frames, other than insofar as they transact other quanta (i.e., fleetingly share other's quantum present-moments), is inscrutable to any other observer.

What this means, of course, is that there is no cosmical continuum of any kind linking things together, as in classical physics (as well as in the misconceived "space-time continuum" of General Relativity). To grasp both these relativistic and quantum aspects of nature we have to abandon any idea that all the various bits of matter are contained in a single continuous spatial receptacle (call it "void", "vacuum", "universe" or whatever) in which time ticks away at the same rate all over. We then settle for what

5

some quantum physicists call a discretist, or digitalised approach to physics, to replace the classical algorithmic one which, they say, fixes nature "in aspic". But, someone says, doesn't this shut us away in a little box of our own, aware of others only as our present-moments touch. The answer to that is, yes. However, in our normal contacts with the physical world these contacts are so abundant that we could never experience the bits of quantum isolation that are involved and which are necessary for our statistically continuous informational interrelation between others and other things – any more than we are aware of the bits of darkness separating the dots on the screen when watching a TV drama.

And just as those interruptions between the dots are necessary to allow those changes from the one dot to another which provide the action in that TV phenomenon – thus avoiding the stasis which would otherwise result from connecting them all permanently together in such a way that no one of them could change without affecting the others – the characteristic discreteness of quantum events is necessary to provide all the change and activity in the world around us. This, of course, is why the Greek atomists had to split up the Eleatic One, or plenum, into atomic fragments, so as to accommodate motion. (Unfortunately, they split up matter into atomic fragments but did not think of splitting-up time in that way, as quantum physics now has to, for much the same reasons.)

Summary

Could it be, then, that all these now rapidly proliferating problems are no more than what the Philosopher Wittgenstein called Scheinprobleme, illusory problems created by a flagrant, albeit now conventionalised abuse of common language? What cannot be denied is that the language of modern science, with its esoteric talk of "photons", "the limiting speed of light in vacuo", "the universe", "the Big Bang", "wave-particles", "superluminality", telepathic "action-at-a-distance"... and so on, has lost touch with, if not deliberately abandoned, common-sense. And what is commonsense? It certainly is not the stereotypical dim-wittedness of "Joe Public" as some intellectuals assume. What else, then, can it be but the logical commonness and connectedness of what ordinary intelligent folk can agree is a wholesome and satisfactory explanation – of any natural phenomenon whatsoever, no matter how novel our experience of it. And what else can an abandonment of that commonness and connectedness of language lead to, however "scientifically" we dress it up, but in the end, a Babelian disintegrity and confusion?

N. V. Pope

The Absolute Instantaneity of Relativistic Light-Interaction

I should like to discuss, here, certain ideas in the history of Western natural philosophy upon which what subsequently became known as 'science' was founded and which have remained, to this day, entirely problematical.

Not least among these current problems is that of understanding how, in the contexts of relativity and quantum theory, fundamental particles of matter are separated and how they move and interact, especially over distances in the measuring of which the speed of light cannot be discounted.

Traditionally, it was thought that what separates things, basically, is 'the void'. This concept of 'the void' originates with the Greeks, around the sixth century B.C. in a tussle between the philosophers of Ionia and Elea over how to conceive the separations and motions of physical objects. The Eleatics had reasoned that since nothing comes from nothing, and since whatever already exists cannot become nothing, existence must be absolutely permanent and uninterrupted, with no gaps and no beginning nor end. So, they said, existence is atomic - which then meant simply indivisible - because since there is no such thing as nothing (a tautology) there is literally nothing to separate things. So all things must somehow exist together and the fact that we see them separated must be an illusion. By that same token, motion must also be an illusion, because in order to move, a body must cease existing where it is and begin existing somewhere else, which breaks the existential continuity. So both separation and motion were impossible and therefore completely illusory according to the Eleatic way of thinking.

However, that did not satisfy the Ionians, who regarded motion and change as real. So their solution was to declare that the 'nothing', which the Eleatics regarded as meaning simply 'non-existent' was, in fact, a real extended emptiness, or 'void'. They still regarded existence as ultimately 'atomic', as in the Eleatic conception, but they shattered the Atomic One so that it became the atomic many, and distributed these 'atoms' into their newly reified 'void'. Thus we have that peculiar idea on which Western science is founded, of the world as consisting of permanent and self-sufficient material particles (atoms) separated and moving about in a permanent and self-sufficient void.

A logical consequence of this, of course, is that we never see those atoms directly but only at distances measured through this 'void'. This requires that there be some void-traversing agency. The atoms therefore, it was supposed, give off little images of themselves (in Greek, εἰδόλα) and these formed the 'streaming substance' which the philosopher Empedocles identified as light. In this way, light, which originally referred to what we actually see – the opposite of dark – assumed, for the purposes of nascent science, a space-travelling, 'middle-man' role in our perceptions of reality.

The tortuous history of all that followed is well documented. Descartes, for instance, reasoned that if we never see things as they really are but only as they appear, then how can we ever be sure that those objects are really there as things in themselves or, if they are, that they are anything like what we observe. He therefore concluded that if there really are things, 'out there', to which our perceptions relate – and there must be, he assumed, because God, who created us and our senses is, by definition, perfect and therefore 'no deceiver' – then those things we perceive must be real, in and of themselves. But since we never perceive those things directly, there must be two realities: one of 'mind', or the world of which we have direct conscious experience; and the other of 'matter', the world which only God sees and which we perceive, if at all, only indirectly and incidentally. Ordinary commonsense reality thus became artificially split down the middle, creating that dualistic division between 'mental' and 'material' which runs throughout the whole of Western thinking, even today.

It is scarcely surprising, then, that in modern science, these difficulties we assumed were long gone have come back to haunt us. For in the present issue of the so-called EPR Paradox and 'action-at-a-distance', we are faced once more with those very same long-unsolved problems concerning the separations and motions of objects. Not least of the difficulties is the sheer semantic problem of what meaning can be given to the term 'void'. As Aristotle put it, since 'void' means, literally, nothing, then to say that things are separated by void is to say they are not separated (i.e., that they are separated by nothing). This either collapses everything back into the Eleatic One or it ends in complete contradiction. Evidently, then, the original concept of 'the void' had to be modified in some way to meet the needs of science. Such attempts, however, have

Relativity

The final answer to those problems, it is popularly believed, was Einstein's Special Theory of Relativity. However, satisfaction among professional physicists, with that standard solution, is now perceptibly waning. Let us see if we can understand why.

Special relativity is based on two postulates, or axioms, expressing the results of physical experiment. These are

  1. The laws of nature are the same in all systems at rest or in uniform motion relatively to one another.

  2. In all such systems, the speed of light in vacuo is a constant, c.

Now let us look carefully at this second postulate. It is a well-known consequence of Special Relativity - which I shall shortly demonstrate, anyway - that in all frames of reference the proper distance and proper time of a light-signal, at the so-called 'speed c', are precisely zero. This is the same as saying that in no system of reference is there any intrinsic extension or duration in a light-signal.

In what sense, then, can light be said to 'travel in space', when the proper motions of its particles (photons) are distanceless and timeless? So the statement that 'light travels in space' is plainly contradictory, which makes it little wonder that 'Relativity', in most people's minds, remains a by-word for obscurity; for what is any logical person to make of the proposition that light travels in space at the finite and constant rate c but takes no time and measures no distance in doing so?

To remove that contradiction is to remove not only the customary space-travelling idea of light but also the old idea of a self-extended void, since those two conceptions stand or fall together. This restores to commonsense that 'naïve' conception of light which prevailed prior to its imputed 'space-travelling' role; that is, as being simply the opposite of dark. In other words, the Ionian 'void' finally disappears, and with it goes any necessity for thinking of a void-bridging acency - be it 'light', 'gravitation' or whatever. The only distances between bodies which it makes sense to speak of are then those we actually see, in the three dimensions of ordinary perception, and which we project from informational patterns in the way we project, from digital information, a 3-D picture or hologram. (shades of the Eleatic idea of the 'illusoriness' of physical separation!)

In the same way, the only 'motion' light may have, according to this interpretation is seen motion, which consists of sequences of illumination in which the position of the illumination, against the general perceptual background, changes in an ordered way from one observational instant to another. In experiments of the sort described by Abramson (ref.), therefore, which are designed to photograph 'light in flight', the effect is the same as shining a torch into fog, where the beam appears as a time-progression of instantaneous point-illuminations across the visual field. (As is well-known, in the absence of particles - of dust, say, or fog - the 'beam' remains completely invisible.)

In each observational frame, or 'still', in this sequence, however, nothing travels. Each of these 'stills', is analysable into ultimate informational bits, or quanta, h, answering to what we call photons. However, these photons are no longer the space-travelling projectiles of mechanistic tradition but intrinsically spaceless and timeless observational/informational 'blips', and the connections between them are those we see directly, in the observational context, or Gestalt, in which they occur, not in the 'hidden mechanisms' of scientific tradition. These contextual interrelations are the information from which we project the distances and directions of things, and without that information, no photonic event is more near or more distant than any other.

The proper-time of the photon-occurrence at a distance and at its point of observation are thus one and the same, consistently with special relativity, but

Purged in this way of all the old and problematical suggestions of dualistic dissociation between objects and their appearances, relativity comes into its own as a consistently observationalistic (or phenomenalistic) approach to physics and to science in general.

For these photon-distributions to be informational and therefore able to provide all that rich variety of phenomena we call the world, the photons themselves have to be intrinsically unconnected; that is to say, dissociate or indeterminate. Relativity, indeterminacy and quantisation are thus brought naturally and logically together as parts of a more comprehensive theory of observational-instrumental communication, the foundations of which were laid by Mach, Wittgenstein and Shannon.

The 'cinematographic illusoriness' of extension and motion.

Something which has always intrigued metaphysicians is the question 'What is time?' In purely empirical terms, however, this presents no problem: time is simply the measure of the observed changes or motions of objects.

But that means there are as many times measured as there are differently changing and moving objects. Which of all these is the true measure of time? Our answer is, each and every one of them. And the scale of observational time

Inertia

This small shift in relativistic interpretation, however, is far from trivial. In getting rid of Ionian space it also gets rid of any barrier, in the shape of 'light', 'the void' or whatever, to the sympathetic interlinking of distant actions demanded by the conservation laws - the instantaneous, so-called 'action-at-a-distance'. For instance, it removes all objection to Mach's contention that inertia is an instantaneous and reciprocally balanced interaction between bodies generally (Ref. Assiz) by solving the paradox which Phipps (Ref.) points out, that if these interactions are limited to the 'speed c' (in space conceived after the Ionian fashion) then the time between the brakes being applied on a subway-train and the passengers feeling the jerk would be spread over millennia. The answer is now, simply, that the instantaneity of balanced interaction required by the conservation laws and the proper-time instantaneity of photon-interaction are one and the same. So, in inertial motion, the balanced interactions of one's body with other bodies, including optical interactions, are unilaterally instantaneous and reciprocal, whilst all bilateral interactions are observationally time-retarded. That is to say, in moving one's body one is simultaneously moving all those other bodies in the solar system and beyond. One cannot, of course, see those sympathetic movements because they are so small; but if they could be seen, then it would be only after the lapse of observational time 2s/c shown in the figure, where s is the distance of the object in question. This not only fits perfectly the requirements of Special Relativity but also fulfils Einstein's intention of incorporating Mach's principle into relativity theory, which attempt was thwarted by Einstein's classically Ionian interpretation of the distance-time constant c as the 'finite and absolutely limiting speed of light in vacuo'.

The 'substance behind the appearances'

Now, so far we have talked about 'interactions between A and B'. But what are these fundamental interagents A and B, themselves? Ultimately, they are the atoms between which the quanta are transacted. The most elementary of these atoms consists of a single quantum of angular momentum $\hbar$, or $h/2\pi$. This is, by definition, a pair of masses classically conceived as orbiting each other (or, rather their common mass-centre) each with some speed $v$ at an orbital radius $r$ (i.e.,\hbar =_{\text{def.}} mvr$). However, it is pointless to try and think of these fundamental masses as moving at those speeds, in the manner of stars and planets because, for the same reason that $\hbar$ is indivisible, so are those internal orbital parameters. At the quantum level, those variables are no more than dimensional tokens - or 'terminal abstractions', as someone has called them - of our real (i.e. observational) measure-categories.

In photonic transactions, then, the atom as such, remains substantially invisible. All that is manifest at the optical level of interaction is some energy $\Delta K = h\nu$ shed by the atom on account of some adjustment in its internal parts, according to the basic spectrum-formula:

$$h\nu = N^2 hcR [(1/\hbar^2) - (1/\hbar^2)] \tag{4}$$

(This empirical, Balmer/Rydberg series, by the way, is directly deducible from (1) as, in his talk, my colleague, Dr. Osborne, will explain.)

In what way, then, do those atoms and their sub-particles exist when we are not seeing them - that is, substantially, 'behind' the photonic interchange? Our answer is that those particles, when optically dormant are active in other ways, namely, in those sorts of ways we customarily refer to as 'gravitational' and/or 'inertial'. When I jump, everything else jumps - at least to the extent that this action can be shared-out without splitting quanta. Like photonic action, that energy-itransaction with the environment is unilaterally instantaneous and reciprocal. That is why, in moving, we immediately feel the momentum and inertia of our own bodies - because (pace Mach) in that same instant one's muscles are moving not only one's own body but also, to some extent, the balance of the remainder of other bodies, including the 'fixed stars'. Gravitation

This other physical manifestation of these optically dormant bits of matter is what we commonly refer to as the 'weight', 'momentum', 'inertia' and so on, of bodies. In weighing a body we are transacting energy, not with just those parts of its atoms which give us those optical energies expressed in (4) but with the mass of the atoms as a whole. What we measure on a weighing-scale is the force of that mass's reaction to being prevented, by the hardness of the earth's surface, from following the orbit it would otherwise naturally describe around the sun in accordance with its solar angular momentum. [indicate (5)]

$$J = mvr = GmM/v \tag{5}$$

When that 'force of gravity' is absent - that is, when these atom are orbiting without restriction - their presence is manifest in the way their orbits are balanced, immediately and holistically, by the orbits of other atoms. In our theory, this is due, not to any 'in vacuo gravitational force' but to the automatic and instantaneous sympathetic motion of masses under the law of conservation of overall angular momentum - according to which it would be a miracle if one body could move without immediately affecting all those others. By the same token, the presence of objects which may be invisible to us, even at great distances, is implicit in the way our own and other bodies move relatively to one-another. This, of course, was how the planet Neptune was discovered, not optically but by studying its effects in purturbing the orbital motions of the known planets.

This orbital angular momentum of bodies, we must remember, is, quantised in the same way that photonic interaction is quantised. So, whereas in optical contact with an atom we are altering the angular momentum of that atom's internal parts, in weighing it we are altering (interfering with) its angular momentum as a whole. Quantum potential

We must remember, however, that in the latter case, unlike the motions of the earth and sun, those motions of m and M and associated parameters are not continuous but discrete, or quantised. So at the fundamental level we lose all that determinacy of position and motion of material particles which was so characteristic a feature of classical physics. In its place we gain a concept of the world, at the ultimate microphenomenal level, as a purely information-statistical quantum-potential in which it makes no sense to wonder where a particle is or what it is doing between one interaction and another. These elements therefore cease to be 'particles' in the old sense and become events, with purely token dimensions of position, momentum, and so on, and their motions between one place and another cease to be mechanical and become, as we say, purely sequential, or cinematographic.

It is plain, then, why photons appear to be 'telepathic' in their selections of landing-sites in light-interference experiments of the type carried out by Thomas Young. (Ref.) This is no mystery when we reflect that in 'Eleatic' terms, in its own proper-time, the photon is 'there' as soon as it starts out. In this sense the photon, in observer-extrapolated distance-time, is more suitably conceived as a probe than a projectile - which is the way Descartes' thought of it, incidentally.

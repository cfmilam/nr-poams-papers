Faster than radar, precis - Sept 2010

N. Vivian Pope — 2010

Faster than Radar N. V. (Viv) Pope

Abstract Since the introduction of Einstein’s Special Theory of Relativity in 1905, it has been generally assumed that no physical interaction between bodies can take place faster than the ‘constant speed of light, c, in vacuo’. Speculations as to how to break that ‘light barrier’ – called ‘superluminality’ – are common. However, according to Relativity, for anything to travel ‘faster than c’, its intrinsic, or proper time, would have to run backwards, which most scientists regard as nonsense. In this paper it is shown, however, that quantum instantaneity and relativistic time-delay are not, as it seems, contradictory but are complementary components of any interaction called ‘electromagnetic’ – e.g., light, radio, radar, etc. A consequence of this is that if correct, then information about distant objects may be obtainable immediately, without any assumption of ‘superluminality’ and without any conflict whatsoever with the standard relativistic time-equation.

1. The double-ended quantum jump (action-at-a-distance) The conventional conception of RADAR is that of a pulse of transmitted electromagnetic energy travelling from a source at the speed of light, c = 2.99792459 × 108 metres per second (in vacuo) and then reflected by the target back to the source at the same speed, to signal the position of a target in relation to the source.

Thus, the travelling-time of the signal, from the source to the target and back is twice the distance of the source in metres, divided by c. Radar is, of course, a low-frequency form of light. Light is never transmitted ad lib into the void. It is an interaction that takes place specifically between the atoms of bodies separated by distances that are sometimes very large – astronomical in most cases.

Radio, also, is not something that is transmitted ad lib. It is an interaction that takes place between bodies which are whole collections of atoms such as, for example, ordinary radio transmitters and receivers, also those deep-space radio sources known to astronomers.

These differently named interactions are, of course, all of the same nature as light. The fact to be noted is that for all frequencies of the light-spectrum, including radio and radar, these interactions are ultimately quantised in irreducible units of h, where h is the product of energy and time known as Planck’s constant, or quantum of action.

Now, because each quantum of this radiant energy is irreducible, no quantum of light-interaction has any energy to share with anything between where it leaves an atom in one place and ends in another atom, somewhere else. There can be no question, then, as to what the quantum does, how it ‘travels’, etc., in the space between those two interacting atoms.

At the quantum level, therefore, the two atoms are in a direct and instantly reciprocal inter-resonance relation in accordance with Newton’s law of equal and opposite action-and-reaction. That is to say, the interaction has to be a completely consummate, double-ended event, taking place between the distance-separated atoms of bodies instantly – in effect, being virtually the same event at the two separate places.

This proper-time instantaneity of a light-signal in standard orthodox Relativity, is well known. For instance, when the relative, or observer time tR of a light-signal is tR = [(s/c)2 + tP2]½ (1), the intrinsic, or ‘proper’ time tP of the interaction itself, according to that equation is, plainly, zero [1]. Contrary to what is generally assumed [2], there is no contradiction implied by the fact that light is both instantaneous – at the quantum level – and time-delayed on the ordinary observational level, since those two ‘velocities’, the instantaneous and the delayed, are complementary aspects of the same equation. With the distant receiver R1 and transmitter, T2 coupled together, as soon as the transmission from T1 is received by R1, it is immediately re-transmitted by T2 back to the source receiver R2. This situation matches that of a radar signal being sent out from a source and bounced (reflected) back, with the usual overall timedelay 2s/c, from which, of course the distance and direction of the object are determined by the radar operator at the source.

Now from the requirement that every quantum of distant interaction has to be immediately consummated, it follows that for a light pulse of just one quantum, unless there is a billet instantly assured for its reception at some distant point, it cannot be transmitted.

In other words, the condition for that irreducible quantum-interaction to take place is that both the distance and the disposition of the absorbing atom are resonant in terms of numbers of half-lengths λ/2, where λ is the wavelength of the quantum, h divided by c and the emission energy e.

This is manifest in the well-known Thomas Young two-slit experiment, where it is plain that the quantum interactions between the source and the final screen take place only at points on that screen where the tracks to the end-screen through both slits in the intermediate screen are geometrically in synch.

Otherwise, at those places where those tracks are geometrically out of synch by ninety degrees, no energy is transferred from the source – which, of course, is the cause of the typical dark and light fringe-pattern of illumination on that far-end screen.

In that situation, any receiver in the dark bit of the reception-area is, as it were, ‘blind’ to the transmission from the source. This has been called ‘spooky action-at-adistance’, because it seems, naively, that the quanta, conceived as travelling ‘photons’, are somehow ‘telepathic’ in choosing their destinations prior to their arrival.

In principle, then, it should be possible, by manipulating the conditions for the reception of quanta at the distant end to inhibit or modulate their transmission at the source. A useful analogy might be the case of a garden hose with a weak spot, or rubber bulb at the point where it is connected to the tap.

In this case, one way of signalling to someone at the far end of the hose would be, when the water is flowing steadily, for the person at the tap-end to inject a dye into the bulb or the pipe at that entry point. The flow then carries the dye to the far end of the hose where, in due time, it emerges as a signal to the far-end observer.

Another way of signalling, in that same situation, would be, when the water is flowing, for the far-end observer to stamp on, or clamp the hose at that distant point. Water being incompressible, that retrograde signal would then be received immediately as the swelling of the bulb at the near end.

Needless to say, this would be a far quicker way for a pair of observers to communicate than in the usual way. Now, in this same ideal situation, let us imagine that these special transmitters and receivers depicted in Fig. 1 are the only ones in the universe operating on that very narrow and altogether unique frequency.

In this ideal case, it should be possible for an operator at the distant end, B, simply by switching off the receiver R1, to block or regulate the signal (the flow of energy) from the source, in the same manner as clamping the far end of the hose to stop the flow of water in the hosepipe analogy.

In other words, without the receiver R1 switched on and tuned to that signal, the transmitter T1 wouldn’t be able to transmit. So, by modulating the receiver at the distant point with, say, a sequence of Morse-codelike on and off switchings, it should be possible for that far-end operator R1 and the source transmitter-operator T1 to switch roles, with the information (the signal) transferred instantly from the distant receiver R1 to the source transmitter T1, in a direction opposite to that of the normal flow of energy from that source.

Let this be called ‘retrograde (or backwards) signalling’. This means, of course, that whichever one of these observers, say T1, acts as the transmitter of the energy, will have instantaneous, on-the-spot information about the distant receiver as to whether it is on or off.

By that same token, if any such ideal transmitter were tuned to some specific frequency in the spectrum of an element, the operator, by scanning the heavens, should be able to obtain an instantaneous on-the-spot map of the distribution of that element throughout the stars and galaxies.

Unlike radar, which would take millennia to do the same, this retrograde signalling technique would be absolutely direct and immediate. That is to say, as opposed to the transmission and reflection of a radar beam taking a time of tR = 2s/c according to the equation (1), the time tP for this retrograde signal, according to that same equation would be zero.

Such, at any rate, is the ideal situation. The question remains as to whether or to what extent, these principles may pertain in practice. Where, for instance, may we find, occurring in nature, any evidence of this kind of retrograde information-flow?

One answer is provided by the above-mentioned Thomas Young two-slit experiment. In that well-known situation, energies transmitted from the source at the wavelengths λ = h/mv appear to ‘know’, ahead of time, which areas of the far-end screen to ‘steer for’ and which to avoid, so as to consummate the source-screen quantum interaction [3].

This may be taken as direct empirical evidence of the existence, in nature, of this instantaneous retrograde information flow. In practice, however, it is impossible – at least in terms of our present technology – to determine, at the source, the directions ‘steered’ by the quanta towards their destinations [4].

The only way, then, in which this retrograde, teleblocking of the quanta could be registered at the source would be for all possible alternative outlets for the source-energy, other than the one in question, to be completely eliminated. This would, in effect, enclose those quantum interactions within a perfect tubular ‘vacuum flask’, as it were, in which the source energy would have nowhere to go except to receiver at the far-end.

The principle, here, remains the same, that where energy has nowhere to go, it cannot leave. That, at least, is logically undeniable. Is there, then, any possibility of confining a beam of energy along some channel in which it is impossible for the transmitter of that energy to interact with anything other than a specifically tuned receiver at the opposite end? Optical fibres and microwave conduits spring to mind. However, it seems doubtful that the refractive index of the fibre or the tuning of the microwave guide would remain constant if endblocked in that way, which makes it unlikely that this facile hosepipe analogy could be applied to a light signal. As a colleague of this author’s has put it, the conduit for such a transmission would be less like a pipe and more like a looseknit fisherman’s sock. Blocking the energy at the far end would merely succeed in squirting it out all over – that is, by interacting with those other atoms which everywhere abound in and around the ‘sock’. Yet it remains undeniably true, in principle, that there can be no such thing as a one-ended quantum jump. Regardless, then, of the fact that its relative time tR is the delayed-time s/c in the formula (1), the quantum has an intrinsic transitiontime tP of zero. In this case, the quantum is ‘there as soon as it starts out’ – or, obversely, without an assured there to accommodate it, the quantum cannot depart. And if this is true in principle, that instantaneous retrograde signalling really does take place, then, as the two-slit experiment confirms, it should somehow or other be observationally verifiable. In any case, the principle remains logically sound, that if there is nowhere for the quantum to go, then it cannot leave. So the question remains as how to apply this seemingly valid logical principle of teleblocking in practice. Perhaps it can be answered by a homely comparison with electrical circuitry.

Figure 2 Now it is common knowledge that with either one or the other, or both, of the switches open, neither of the detectors will register a current. Also, experiment reveals that in this case, with the far-end switch open, closing the near-end switch does not cause even the slightest flow of current from the battery into the circuit, capacitance notwithstanding [5]. Experiment also reveals that as soon as both switches are ON the current will be registered by both detectors. On the other hand, if one of those switches is OFF, say at B, then the signaller at the opposite end, A, will know that immediately because his detector registers nothing in response to his closing of his switch. It would be the same, of course, for B in his attempts to signal to A.

Let us, then, envisage a situation where the switches are designed to vibrate on and off at a set frequency, so that at their distance apart they vibrate either in or out of phase with each other. From what we already know, each signaller should be able to tell instantly the on/off state of the other’s switch, without having to wait for the return light-signal or radar signal, which might take years or even centuries to arrive. But we also know, from the time-formula (1), that for an intrinsic quantum time tP = 0 of the transition, the length s of the conductor divided by c is an observationally delayed time tR. So although the sender of the signal at, say, A, is instantly aware of the on or off state of the switch at B, the question of whether or not there will be a current registered by A’s detector will depend on whether or not the distance-time s/c and the vibration time of the opening and closing frequency are in-phase or out-of-phase over the length of the circuit. This, of course, is precisely what we find in the two-slit light-interference experiment, where the only atoms in the final screen which can interact with the source are those which, in those distance-time terms are resonant with the source frequency. It seems, then, that in theory, at least, there is a possible way of circumventing the usual time-delay in the sending and receiving of signals, whether they be light-signals, radar signals, electrical signals or whatever. Whether or not this can be achieved in practice is a moot point, but it is a truism of science that whatever proves valid in theory will sooner or later be put into practice. In that case, if this theory proves to be correct, then we cannot rule out the possibility of being able to obtain, ahead of any radar-like contact, direct, onthe-spot, here-and-now information of distant objects and states, as in the electrical example and atomic resonances in the two-slit experiment.

Conclusion It appears, then, that despite the time-delay that is conventionally interposed between distant objects and our observations of them due to the ‘finite speed of light’, there is a definite possibility to be explored of obtaining immediate and instantaneous knowledge of distant events and situations independently of lightspeed. This follows on two counts: one is that the law of the conservation of energy forbids that there can be any delay between a quantum of energy leaving one atom and arriving at another, and the other is that all quantum interactions between bodies at a distance is proper-time-instantaneous and reciprocal. This means that to obtain information about distant situations and conditions due to the blocking of quantum interactions, which we have called teleblocking, is infinitely faster than can be obtained by radar.

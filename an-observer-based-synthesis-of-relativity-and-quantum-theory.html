An Observer-based Synthesis of Relativity and Quantum Theory

An Observer-based Synthesis of Relativity and Quantum Theory

by

N. Vivian Pope

The primary agent of observation in physics is light. We think of light as consisting of photons and we think of photons as little space-travelling particles whose speed relatively to all observers is a constant c, of value 300 thousand kilometres per second.

But that presents us with a mystery. How can anything 'travel' at the same speed relatively to differently moving observers? Students of relativity will say that this is because all time-standards and distance-standards are set by the speed at which these photons travel - because photon-signals are the ultimate bits of information on which we base those measurements. Well, if so, then why do we need to think of those photons as 'travelling'? How can the informational bits out of which space and time are projected travel in that same relativistically projected space and time?

Another problem with the travelling-photon hypothesis is that if photons do travel then they have to be telepathic. When a pair of photons is emitted together at a source, whatever happens to the one photon at its destination immediately affects the other photon at its destination, no matter how far apart in space and time those destinations may be. This has to be so in order to conserve the overall energy-equation, which in no way could the the photons do if they travelled at the finite speed c - unless, of course, they were somehow 'telepathic', which is an embarrassing assumption for science to have to make.

Not only that but how can we possibly tell what a photon is and what it is doing when travelling all alone in vacuo? Since the smallest amount of energy our instrument may detect is the energy the photon has, then there is no possible way of knowing what the photon is, nor where it is nor how it is, etc., in between two instrumental appearances.

To assume that a photon is a travelling particle, therefore, involves all sorts of scientifically unseemly assumptions. It is possible, however, and with no practical disadvantage whatsoever, to think of the photon in a radically

different way. We may, for instance, describe what happens in terms of the instrumental appearances alone, without assuming that anything 'travels' in between. Motion and physical phenomena generally thus become 'cinematographic', composed of observational sequences of photonic 'stills'. This fits the requirements of both relativity and quantum theory. For relativity everything has to be related to observation, and for quantum theory everything has to be 'digitalised' or quantised. The new way of thinking combines both of these theoretical requirements.

But doesn't relativity theory assume that light, consisting of photons, travels at the speed c? The answer is, for the observer, yes, but not for the photon itself. The essence of relativity lies in the fact that as bodies travel closer and closer to that observational 'speed of light c', the time they record over a given distance, hence the distance they measure in that time, gets less and less. So a 'photon', whose theoretical speed is exactly c records no time and no distance for the journey. Intrinsically, the beginning and end of a photon-interaction are one and the same event, which accords precisely with the requirements of quantum-physics. Whatever distance and time we project into that interaction are projected, not from measuring the speeds of photons, which is impossible, but from statistical numbers of these intrinsically spaceless and timeless quantum-interactions. This statistical projection, among normally distributed instruments, takes the form of an information-statistical 'probability wave' with the propagational dimensions c.

By 'instruments', here, we mean anything which takes part in photonic interactions, which includes just about anything and everything. All that is excluded is the sort of space which Newton envisaged as a self-sufficient cosmical emptiness, or 'void', residing permanently behind the scenes. The instruments themselves and the information they provide are sufficient to define both space and time relatively to an observer without having to assume the existence of this classical 'void'. Objective space - real space - is then a communicational 'video-projection' relatively to different observers, a kind of informational 'hologram', the ultimate dimensions of which are relative, not absolute, and quantised, not continuous.

To visualise the conceptions of scientists is often difficult for the man-in-the-street. The 'four-dimensional space-time continuum' of conventional relativity, for instance, is notoriously difficult to interpret on levels of commonsense, as indeed are the 'probability wave-functions' of quantum physics.. The advantage of the synthesis we have described is that it makes it possible, for modern science, to dispense with the need for such abstruse metaphysical models. All we have to do in order to understand what is being presented is to look about us, with our minds cleared of classical misconceptions. Space is then simply what appears in the different qualities of seen objects, and light is what we see, the opposite of dark, not something travelling invisibly 'in vacuo'.

A Scientist’s Personal Viewpoint on Religion

A Scientist’s Personal Viewpoint on Religion

N. Vivian (Viv) Pope

Preface

This article is written in answer to a challenge. In my book The Eye Of The Beholder $^{[1]}$ I wrote:

In fact, this (potentially) seamless connection between a rational science and a rational religion puts the two into the same logical category of anti-dogmatic natural philosophy. This should be as open-ended in its theories about nature at its microphysical boundaries as about nature on the macroscale. This philosophical open-endedness proffers the best antidote to the sort of dogmatism that presumes to know, absolutely unshakably, the mind of ‘God’ on all levels of human experience.

This was noted by some religiously-minded people who, in view of the ‘seamless connection’ between the ‘rational science’ and the ‘rational religion’ that were described in the book, have urged me, on those grounds, to extend into theology and religion the logical method I employed in my analysis of the science. At least, it was suggested that, having established that ‘seamless connection’, I had no excuse for refusing to venture further. With nothing like the confidence in religious matters that I have in science I now, with reticence, take up that challenge with the intention of pursuing the logic to wherever it might lead – or else to where it might just ‘frizzle out’ as the case may be.

In view of the sheer vastness of the known universe, it is difficult to think of our lives on this miniscule bit of rock we call Earth as having anything like the significance that our religions attribute to it. In places billions of light-years away, what can it matter whether the lives we live here are good or bad. What would ‘good’ or ‘bad’ as we know it mean, anyway, in those immensely far off regions? What could be the significance of the traditions and fortunes of a particular life-form on, say, the tiny patch of this little rock that those life-forms call Israel? As for the authority of our holy scriptures, Christian, Muslim, Hindu or whatever, what influence might they have in, say, a star-system at the very edge of our telescopic range?

And yet, for all the vastness of the universe, there is one tiny point in it which is absolutely unique. That unique point is oneself. Also, insofar as from that very small point we can comprehend so much of that universe, there is a sense in which we are as big as what we perceive. Moreover, modern relativistic physics affirms that the measures mass, length and time which are fundamental to the very existence of physical objects are measures that are not absolute but relative to each and every observer, individually. This means that the universe is not something which exists self-sufficiently in its own right but only as a community of perspectives among ‘observers’, which may be anything from atoms to stars and galaxies. This surely has a bearing on our ideas of what constitutes ‘the universe’.

Leaving that aside for the moment, let us consider the question of whether our moral evaluations here on earth have non-local or cosmical significance or are no more than locally confined? Assuming that, as the evidence so far suggests, it turns out that there are no life-forms in the universe other than ourselves, then it might be argued that our moral evaluations hold throughout the whole universe as far as we can see and comprehend. But that would make Man the measure of everything, of all moral evaluations, such as beauty, goodness, justice, etc., which would elevate these human evaluations to the level of cosmic verities. Besides, what about those inestimable regions which must lie beyond what we can see and comprehend? Should we languish in denial of all the measurements made by centuries of astronomy as to the distances involved, just to preserve the concept of ‘Man the measure’? Otherwise, considering the undoubted size of the universe, as astronomy presents it, it seems implausible, to say the least, that, despite the lack of evidence of extraterrestrial life, life as we know it here is the only intelligent life-form there is. Also, it suggests that the heavens have no significance in themselves but only relative to humanity, which takes us right back to the Old Testament view of the world as having been created and maintained for the sole benefit of Man.

Well, that was alright in the days before modern cosmology, when the sheer immensity of the universe was not realised. However, for modern thinkers that Old Testament argument smacks too much of anthropocentrism or, indeed, solipsism. Like all the other traditional man-centred cosmologies, it just doesn’t fit our modern scientific understanding of the vastness of interstellar and intergalactic space.

Logically, however, given the evidence as it stands for the lack of extraterrestrial life, this Old Testament view seems valid. But then, if any intelligent extraterrestrial life forms were to be discovered, that Old Testament view would have to be abandoned. But without any evidence for the existence of extraterrestrial life, those issues remain completely speculative, leaving the Old Testament believer to cherish his view unrefuted – up to this present point in time, at any rate.

Undoubtedly, the biggest barrier against believing in a benign deity, such as, for example, the God of the Christian religion, is the phenomenon of global suffering. In full recognition of that, how can we believe in a benign deity who is aware of all that suffering yet lets it happen? Is that God simply helpless to do anything about it, or does he (she or it) help only certain chosen individuals? That hardly seems fair! Also, since there is no objective evidence that even the most devout belief in God can prevent us from being overwhelmed by suffering, on what basis can we assume that this deity has the infinite power and will to do so?

We can, of course, close our minds to all this and just believe, doggedly, in spite of it. But that is completely to abandon rationality, since to accept the plight of all those others as real and assume that it is all the work of a loving and caring God is a complete contradiction. Should we, then, believe that the power of that God is limited, and that all that is bad in the world is the work of some evil deity, call it ‘Satan’, the ‘Devil’, ‘Beelzebub’ or whatever, whose cosmic will is opposed to that of God? But that compromises entirely any idea of God as the all-powerful creator and sustainer of the universe.

May we, then, just ignore the plight of others and regard the horrors we see happening to them as though those others were no more than figmentary, like characters in some kind of dream or nightmare? But if we really believed that, then we would be free to treat those characters as well or as badly as we wish with no fear of our actions being subject to moral evaluation. Our lives would be completely amoral, defeating the whole purpose of religion.

Then there is the notion of ‘heaven’ and ‘hell’, as the places to which our souls are supposed to depart after death. Ignoring the archaic language, this is not far from what was concluded in the addendum to ‘Light is Everything’, regarding immortality and the dimensions thereof $^{[2]}$. Shall we, then, suppose that this extra-dimensional realm is the one to which, implicitly, scriptures refer when they talk, in archaic terms, about ‘ascension’, ‘heaven’, ‘Hades’ ‘purgatory’ and so on? What seems certain is that this realm of ‘heaven’ etc., is not that of ordinary physicality. So one can think of no realm other than that extra-dimensional, extra-corporeal one as that to which this traditional terminology implicitly refers.

Shall we think, then, of ‘God’ as the overseer and judge of that extra-dimensional realm, and of our lives in the everyday corporeal world, with its trials and tribulations, as a kind of test for determining to what zone, in that region of the afterlife, our souls are to be assigned? Regardless of what we think of that suggestion, it involves no logical contradiction, hence it is a perfectly valid supposition that life is a kind of trial. Unfortunately, the kind of common language in which these thoughts are usually expressed, with its archaic references to ‘souls’, ‘salvation’ and so on, is bound to be prejudicial to any objective modern discussion. However, there may well be personal experiences, even nowadays, which are strong enough to convince those who have them, that they are in some sort of ‘exam’ situation of that kind, even though this conviction may be more intuitive than rational. In some instances, these experiences may defeat verbal description, in which case they may be left to silent sublingual contemplation. Such personal experiences may, however, profoundly affect the direction of our lives from then on, in ways which, due to the exigencies of verbal communication, others may not be able to understand, far less empathise with.

But still there remains the problem of suffering, not only of oneself but also of those others who, plainly, are innocent of any moral transgression. Think of the child subjected to terminal abuse, or the teenager who, before he is barely wise, gets stabbed or tortured to death, And what about the person born mentally disadvantaged or accidentally brain-damaged? Should we, with equanimity, regard these people and their sufferings as just part of some kind of ‘shadow-show’ for our own benefit? But where can possibly be the justice in that? And what can religion mean in view of these circumstances?

It seems, then, particularly in regard to this question of suffering, that logical reason comes to a dead end in every direction of enquiry so far. So is that it, then, that all belief in religion is logically annulled? By no means! This is for two reasons. In the first place, strictly speaking, by the very fact that it is logically incoherent, this religious belief involves no contradiction $^{[3]}$. In the second place, it leaves other logical avenues to be considered. For instance, the very circumstance of logical impenetrability in itself might be regarded as significant. Take, for instance, the concept of moral virtue. What moral virtue can there be in sitting an exam in which the answers to the questions can be obtained by means other than what the exam is designed to examine – by cheating, in effect? Suppose an athlete were able to obtain precise foreknowledge by means of some supercomputer, say, of the results of a race in which he is scheduled to take part. What point would there be in his competing if he could be informed by that computer that he would definitely lose? And what virtue would there be in his participating in the race if it were absolutely certain that he would win? Again, suppose some woman found that her husband who, she was led to suppose, was a hero for risking his life for her only to find that the situation had been set up by him with paid accomplices? What kind of ‘love’ she would wonder, was that?

In all these cases, the common factor throughout is uncertainty as to the end result of some action undertaken in a moral context. What virtue could there be in living a moral life if such rewards as it might offer could be worked out, other than by actually living it, by circumventing it with armchair logic and/or mathematics, say? Thus it might be argued that the seemingly built-in impossibility of finding a logical solution to these questions of religion is evidence in itself of the inescapability of having to exercise true moral judgement and to live with its consequences. As John Humphries says: ‘If you could prove it, it wouldn’t be faith’ [4]. In that case, the very best that we can expect of logical, mathematical and scientific reasoning, far from being to establish the case for religion – in which, as we have seen, it fails completely – is simply to alert us to the dangers and disadvantages of believing in things which cannot possibly be true because they are self-contradictory. This, basically, defines the methodology that is applied in this foray, namely, that although logic cannot reveal absolute truth it can, absolutely, reveal error. Thus, the logical method employed here, throughout, is that of reaching truth by the method of logical elimination.

Let us return, now, to the issue of the dangers and disadvantages of linguistic stylisation, both verbal and behavioural. On this account, whoever might be subjected to these deep, inexpressible experiences may abstain from even attempting to express them, either privately in thought or publicly in the traditional form of prayer. This sublingual form of consciousness answers, to some extent, to what is sometimes called meditation. Unfortunately this naming invites public expression in certain stylised forms of language and behaviour, which defeats the whole point of trying to release these pristine forms of experience from the constraints of language. Another disadvantage with verbalisation and genuflection is that different forms of verbal and behavioural language, shot through, as they so often are, with vestiges of conflicting traditions, lead to the sorts of sectarian divisions and conflicts which are such a distressing feature of the human race, especially at this present time.

Apart from these issues of language, it appears that none of these inner forms of sublingual experience which may broadly be termed ‘religious’ can be dismissed, either on grounds of logic or of the sort of revised physical science that has been described. To accept these inner experiences as true is therefore, by default, as rational an approach to religion as it is to science, since they both operate – or, at any rate, should do – in much the same empirical way That is to say, the logical criteria for acceptance of natural truths are open-ended and inductive in both cases. This means that a dependence on strictly conditional logic does not signify any constraint on our freedom to believe, consistently with logical reason, in whatever one may choose. For it is well said of logic that there are truths that are beyond, logic but not contrary to it. So, considering the vastness of the universe in which we live, what else could our reasoning possibly be, for religion as for science, but open-ended, or heuristic? In both sectors it is as Shakespeare wrote: ‘There are more things in Heaven and Earth, Horatio, than are dreamed of in your philosophy.’

However, despite this empirical open-endedness of scientific and religious experience, it has to be emphasised that there are some natural checks and balances on our freedom to think and believe as we like. For instance, as for the possibility of there being truths or beliefs contrary to reason, that is a flat contradiction. Any belief that proves to be logically self-contradictory cannot possibly be true and therefore has to be abandoned in the interests of rationality $^{[5]}$. Otherwise, in keeping with that logical condition, we are free to think and believe as we may see fit – mindful, of course, of the responsibilities which that freedom places upon us for maintaining our personal and social integrity. Thus, whatever we think of the ability or otherwise of a supreme deity to relieve the sufferings of those creatures like ourselves who are so eminently prone to them, we, as individuals do, at least, have the ability – indeed, the duty – freely to exercise that ability to relieve suffering to the extent of our capacities. That, at any rate, is surely the humanitarian ideal of morality on this planet, regardless of what we think as to how far that ideal may or may not extend into the universe at large. So, regardless of the question of what ‘good and evil’ may mean universally, we are at least certain as to what it means in terms of suffering and the relief thereof in our terrestrial environment. This gives us the clearest indication of what our moral duties are towards others – towards all forms of life, that is – which is to avoid causing them suffering and to relieve them of it wherever possible. This may, of course, mean having to suffer ourselves, for the sake of others, which is the basis of the Christian faith. This is especially so according to the biblical account of the crucifixion of Christ who, as the story goes, died in that way to relieve the rest of us from the sufferings which, according to the Christian belief, we should otherwise receive at the hands of the Almighty.

For many people, however, the logic of this is, to say the least, obscure, since there seems to be no physical or other discernible connection between that kind of martyrdom and the relief of the suffering of others. Besides, despite the rationalisation that is usually offered for it, the idea of a Creator who, as a ‘loving God’, would make agonies of suffering a condition for his Creation is little short of incomprehensible.

Be that as it may, due undoubtedly to its lack of logical coherence, there is no logical contradiction in that faith in the voluntary suffering of one person for the sake of the whole of humanity. However, for some people, that lack of any clear logical coherence leaves a gap which, for many rationally-minded people, cannot be bridged. Besides, any idea of a ‘Loving God’ who would make the suffering we see on such an horrific scale nowadays a condition of his creation, definitely seems to be a flat contradiction, hence, for those thinkers an unsurmountable barrier to belief.

In itself, however, that is no valid excuse to abandon, altogether, a belief in the efficacy of religion. In fact, the teaching: ‘Love thy neighbour as thyself’, as a formula for living, is unsurpassable. This is regardless of the metaphysical account of the man Jesus’ special relationship with the ‘powers on high’. Certainly there is no logical contradiction that can be found in that ‘Love thy neighbour’ formula.

But is this formula just confined to Man or is it, possibly, a cosmical principle? This takes us back to the question of whether there are, in the nature of things, truths applicable to the world of Man that are also universal. As a matter of fact there are. For instance, the second law of thermodynamics is universal; the conservation laws of energy and angular momentum are universal. It is also universally true that all action (the product of energy and time) is quantised in units of Planck’s constant $h$, and so on. Not least is the simple fact that, universally, two and two add up to four. Evidently, then there are truths that govern our moral existence on this planet but without being confined to it.

The same goes for the rules of logic. If an object X is contained within a region Y, and if that region Y is contained within another region Z, then the object X is contained within the region Z. That is surely a universal, not merely a terrestrial principle, since it applies throughout everything, from our Earth to the farthest stars and galaxies. The same applies to many things in what the Greeks identified as logos, the universal principle by which all things, anywhere and everywhere, are governed. Roughly translated, logos is language, but not quite what we now think of as language. For the Greeks it was a universal principle, applied to everything that is true and real. It was, in other words, a perfect language, the language that underlies human language and without reference to which human language (including logic, music, maths and science) would be no more than just noise. For some, the logos was deified. For instance, in the Gospel of St John it was identified with the God of the Christian religion. Originally, however, it was a secular concept, as objective and insignificant as the fact that two plus two are four and as significant as the mass-energy formula $e = mc^2$, both of which are integral parts of the logos. For the Greeks, another integral part of logos, was that of justice, the law of the balance of things, akin to the modern second law of thermodynamics, or entropy. But justice is also a social, or humanitarian concept, whence it may be argued that what is justice for humanity is Justice throughout the cosmos. And if this is true of Justice, then might it not also be true of all the remaining verities, such as Goodness and the distinction between Good and Evil. At least, there seems, again, to be no logical reason why that should not be so.

Of course, we cannot think of the logos as equivalent to the sets of words we use in language. The words we use in the various human languages signify the attempts we make to mirror the logos, to identify and emulate it in human communication. It scarcely needs saying that what is identified as a ‘tree’ in English (‘coed’ in Welsh, ‘baum’ in German, ‘arbre’ in French and so on) is not a product of or a projection of human language. If the tree were not whatever it is in the logos, then none of the words we use to identify it would have any meaning. Far less would they be inter-translatable which, in fact, they so eminently are! Certainly, the classes and categories into which we scientifically divide things such as ‘animal’, ‘vegetable’, ‘mineral’, etc., would not be functions common to our various languages if it were not that this is more or less the way they must be divided and classified in the underlying logos.

What we may conclude, then, of the logos is that it is what underlies or substantiates our human language and experience and that the nearer we get to expressing it satisfactorily in language, by our very best methods of rational research, the truer and more realistic that language gets. In the context of the present enquiry, this is by the careful use of logic, which is the very essence of logos, in seeking out and eliminating beliefs that end in blind alleys of logical contradiction.

However, logos cannot be everything. Logic tells us there is no universal class or category X without there being a correlative non-X. For instance, there is no universal ‘up’ that makes sense without there being also a ‘down’, or a ‘right’ without there being a ‘left’. To say ‘X is everything’ is just meaningless. The same goes for logos, against which, for it to mean anything, there has to be an anti-logos. The opposite of logos, therefore – the anti-logos – is the class of things we learn to identify as illusion, delusion, deception and falsity in general. These are members of the residual class of anti-logos. The same applies, of course, to concepts of ‘God’, ‘Goodness’, ‘Order’ etc., the residual classes of which are, respectively, ‘Devil’, ‘Evil’, ‘Chaos’ and so on. Even the concept of ‘Universe’ has its logical opposite, namely ‘Pluriverse’, ‘Multiverse’ or ‘Duoverse’. So, indeed does ‘light’, the correlative of which is ‘darkness’.

Beliefs which turn out on logical analysis to be spurious are, therefore, not part of the objective and absolute logos. They are no more than our subjective misrepresentations of the same, due, perhaps, to an over-attachment to convention or being thrust upon us by such means as cunning and political chicanery – or, of course, both. (It is noted in passing that in Christianity, the Great Deceiver is Satan, the very personification of the anti-logos that has been described.)

Recognising this necessity for the existence of the logos keeps one steered on a rational course which is at least unimpeded by false beliefs and dogmas. From one’s own ‘religious’ experience, this course is to lay aside human language as far as possible so as to commune with the logos direct – that is, by refraining to employ the conventional names in either thinking of it or otherwise discussing it. What remains is the bare knowledge that whatever the logos is, at the very least it exists.

However, insofar as this subject is discussable, it seems that so far, one has marked out an avenue of scientific rationality which one is free to take without incurring logical contradiction or conflict with true science. Life is no algorithm to be solved by deductive or axiomatic methods of logic or mathematics. In both cases, the methods employed have to be essentially inductive, or open-ended, as is the method employed in this present exploration. As for impediments due to the exigencies of language, these can be avoided simply by reducing verbalisation to the barest minimum, if not to zero. This is not only to avoid the spurious conflicts created by interference from local and national traditions but also to safeguard oneself from getting befuddled by the same. In that way, one’s mind is quieted in preparation for discovering the best that might lie within oneself waiting to emerge.

But what is meant by ‘the best’ in this context? Some would say that it is to ‘listen to the voice of God (Jehovah, Allah, or whatever)’. The difficulty with that is, again, that this naming invariably involves prejudicial differences in conceptions and quarrels over what that deity is, what is its ‘true name’, what intentions it might have towards ourselves and others, all according to our conventional habits, beliefs, customs and superstitions. But this takes us right back to the dangers inherent in verbalised thought and communication. So, once again, emphasis falls on the minimising of language as an approach to religious experience. This, of course, calls for serious reservations regarding organised religion, both verbal and scriptural. The very best ‘prayer’ in this case might be, in some quiet place such as an empty church or a forest clearing, to prepare oneself, by thinking to oneself something like the following:

I am here to receive, in complete humility and in silence, without sensual distraction or prejudgment, whatever guidance I might be given so as to bring out the very best in me and suppress the worst. This is so as to place my own will at the service of whatever are the forces of light and to fight whatever forces of darkness I may, in consequence, encounter. And grant me the strength to continue this fight towards whatever might be the best or the most bitter end.

… this to be then followed by contemplative silence.

However, since it is difficult to commune with something that is invisible, it may be necessary in some instances to construct in one’s mind, some icon or other as something quasi-personal to focus upon in thought and discussion. This ‘something’ may be personified by a name like ‘God’ or whatever’, signifying an image of, say, some kind of father-figure or mother-figure. So whereas the bible says that Man is made in God’s image, in fact it is usually more like the other way around, that God is made in Men’s images. There should be no drawbacks or dangers in this, just so long as one is not fooled into thinking that, answering to that description, there is some real person, on whom we project all the human weaknesses to which we ourselves are prone. The trouble is that identifying with ‘God’ in this way, we may deal with others in terms of those same weaknesses, believing that we are doing so in God’s name. Some of the worst atrocities in history have been committed by despots and oppressors who convinced themselves that they were acting according to the ‘will of God’, when all along they were merely giving reign to their own worst impulses and passions.

In any event, no disputes over the existence of God can make sense unless it is agreed by all disputants what the word ‘God’ means. Do you believe in X? How can that question be answered unless the meaning of ‘X’ is defined, understood and agreed? The tragedy is that so many of the wars between the various religions are no more, in the end, than conflicts over some personally preferred meaning of ‘X’. This is all the more reason for minimising use of language. In this case, the only logical answer to the question of religion, in this vast, open-ended and eminently uncertain universe, would seem to be simply to believe in spite of everything. But believe in what, there’s the rub. Without use of language, how can that question be answered?

Perhaps, then, to express it with the most minimal use of language the answer to that question should be simply that what we believe in is ‘life’. All living creatures ‘believe’ in that, as evidenced by their persistence in surviving – albeit sometimes in deadly natural conflict with one another, ‘red in tooth and claw’. What inner promptings, then, drive different animals, plants, microbes, viruses and so on to live in the way they do we can scarcely even guess at, other than that it is the need to feed to stay alive. But what is important, morally speaking is, surely, to recognise and act upon those inner promptings as they occur in ourselves, having segregated them, of course, from our baser instincts and passions such as greed, the urge to kill and so on, more or less according to the biblical Ten Commandments and the declarations of many other social institutions. The existence of these institutions may be taken as clearly signifying the aims of most people to follow the moral life and suppress immorality or, as it is conventionally called, ‘sin’.

This, at any rate, is the author’s own purely personal view of the moral life which, in his case, mostly involves sorting out, by uncompromising logical reason, the true from the false and the real from the illusory. This declaration of rational scepticism is by no means intended to cause offence to any other person or group, religious or otherwise. Insofar as it may entail rational dispute of the Socratic kind, it is intended not only for one’s own benefit, in being disabused by others of mistakes in reasoning, but also, and mostly, for the rational community whose thirst for knowledge and truth, one assumes, is similar to one’s own. More than anything, however, it is to be taken as a confession, as it were, of a burgeoning religious conviction based on discoveries in logic, mathematics, and modern physics plus, it has to be said, some experiences of a purely personal nature which, for those reasons already discussed, defeat description in conventional language. As for this personal odyssey, posterity and such superhuman powers as there might be will have to judge the outcome, or if that outcome is oblivion, then let it be so. Its aim, in sum, is to explore new avenues of philosophical thought, leaving marks behind for others to follow if it should come up with anything objectively significant. As for public approval and acclaim, it is felt that with sufficient confidence in these religious experiences – if such they may be called – one may legitimately feel that one’s duty is more towards these inner promptings than to current convention. If this seems antisocial, then one’s response would have to be that any society feeling threatened by truth is surely an immoral society and scarcely deserves the respect of right-minded people.

In the last analysis, then, this particular search for truth is motivated, not by any grandiose feeling of social responsibility nor any need for public acclaim. It is driven by the sheer curiosity that motivates most explorers, who seek truths for the same reason that they explore mountains, which is simply, as it is said, ‘because they are there’.

APPENDICES TO ‘A Scientist’s Personal Viewpoint on Religion:

Appendix 1

Sample for analysis: The Great Doping Scandal

During the 1970s and 1980s the East Germans organised a clandestine state-sponsored programme of doping young athletes for excellence in their performances during the Olympic Games. This was without the knowledge or consent of the athletes involved. The drugs were known to have dangerous side-effects, as became evident with some of the women after their competition days were over, when they clearly developed masculine characteristics which their female constitutions were not designed to cope with.

The aim of the Communist regime, as it proved, was to demonstrate the physical superiority of the East Germans over the rest of humanity, in the tradition of excellence after Adolph Hitler. Even the doctors and other medical researchers knowingly went along with the deception, for considerable financial rewards, and everyone was warned, on pain of death, not to divulge the secret.

This, of course, is not the only example of its kind. There are many well-known instances of scientists who forged their results in order the enhance their professional reputations, politicians lying to the public, police planting evidence and so on and so on. Inevitably, these deceptions eventually come to light, creating a global scandal. But why? If it is physically possible to ensure superiority in this way, then why not simply get on with it? Why all the fuss? In the doping incident, what point was there in all the other nations declaring it ‘immoral’? On what physical or other basis was this action so globally condemned? Is it simply because it provided a mean advantage over all the other competing nations, for which those nations were jealous? But, then, when the secret was out, why didn’t all the others adopt that same programme in order to ‘even things up’. Why not extend that policy to ensure superiority for the whole human race?

Now who is there that doesn’t feel an automatic revulsion for that simple, very practical solution? Let us analyse this very widespread moral kind of reaction. Is it simply due to an awareness of the dangers involved? But suppose there are no such dangers, if we were assured everyone could take these drugs with no unwanted side-effects or after-effects. Would that revulsion then simply disappear? That hardly seems likely.

As it seems, then that sort of revulsion is automatic and unconditional. It is an almost reflexive feeling of disgust, which precedes and is independent of any pragmatic calculation of end-results. What is it, within us, which generates these kinds of antipathies to the mere suggestions of things that are deemed ‘immoral’?

Attempts to explain away morality by means of theories like that of the ‘selfish gene’ or the Freudian theory of repressed sexual desire, have no relevance here, since they do not attempt to cope with the phenomenon of a-sexual and selfless morality as it is actually manifest in all its humane complexity, that is, as we actually experience it. How, for instance, would these theories explain the worldwide interest in the sacrifice of Christ or some of the self-sacrifices that were made by heroes of the two World Wars?

Anyway, to return to our example of ‘the great doping scandal’, there were, of course, not just a few competitors who did secretly adopt that pragmatic solution for purely personal advantage. Nevertheless, the fact that when their deeds are uncovered, these miscreants are globally condemned testifies to the fact that most people viscerally reject these sorts of behaviour as immoral. It is difficult to explain away these reactions as having anything to do with the need for procreation, the Oedipus complex or the like, at least not without the most tortuous of convolutions and contrivances.

It seems, then, that in human society there is a built-in personal sense of morality – or of something, call it what we may, ‘conscience’, or whatever – without which, quite clearly, that society could not function, far less survive in competition with others. (Witness, for example, the decline of the Roman and British empires due to their all-attested moral decline.) Survival of one society at the expense of another, as in ‘ethnic cleansing’, is generally regarded as immoral, as testified by the burgeoning of those international organisations such as the United Nations, Amnesty International and so on.

But of course, it will be said that this kind of morality is essentially secular, hence that it has no bearing on the issue of personal morality as discussed earlier. Is that secular morality, then, no more than a product of political circumstance, or is it something deeper and more personal? Well, what about the phenomenon of individual effort where it has been found that people of exceptional initiative have been persecuted by society only for that society to find, eventually – perhaps only after that person’s death – that for its own benefit the society has to follow that individual’s lead? (People like Socrates, Copernicus, Galileo and Bruno spring to mind here!) In this case, it cannot be said that what that person achieved was entirely a social product, that it was due to suppressed sexuality, or the ‘selfish gene. Nor was it in any other way, either inspired by or directed towards society. What drove these people was something essentially asocial, namely an abiding and uncompromising search for truth. This is an inner prompting which is primary, and irreducible, not to be explained-away in terms of anything else. Morality, for these individuals was simply to continue their search for truth in the face of all the hostility that this naturally engenders among the custodians of the social norms (Recall that Socrates, Copernicus, Galileo and Bruno were all persecuted for their temerity in challenging settled social opinions) yet history reveals that a very large proportion of social improvement – perhaps most of it – has been based on contributions of that highly individual kind.

Plainly, then, in these instances, it is the individuals who had the foresight that society lacked. They led and society trailed. What motivated them was something beyond what their community had to offer. It was something intrinsic to the nature of things, which they discovered by obeying their inner promptings. This was often at the expense of social acceptance and approval, sometimes at the risk of paying for it with their very lives.

The morality, then, without which society cannot function is not a product entirely of society itself. Somehow, the morality which directs society is essentially that of its individual members, and it is on the way these individuals behave, whether morally or immorally, that the fortunes of that society depend. (Otherwise, why would there be such things as election campaigns?) This means that despite the obvious two-way interaction between those individuals and their society, all the responsibility for both individual and social behaviour devolves, ultimately, on individuals themselves, according to their lights.

In short, the global extent of the reaction against fraud and general deception, whether it be in sport, commerce, industry, science, politics or daily life, seems to signify, by its very description, that morality is endemic in the human constitution. It is a prompting within us which we can obey or disobey according to our will, and on the basis of which, according to our reasoning in the piece ‘Light Is Everything’, we are judged by our cosmic record in the indelible past.

Appendix 2

The question of ‘fate’

Some people believe in fate. ‘If it’s meant to happen,’ they say, ‘then it will’. But how does this fit with the idea of personal freedom to choose our own moral course in life? These ideas of ‘fate’ and personal freedom don’t seem to go together. Indeed, they appear to be mutually exclusive.

But are they? Think of the age of steam. When James Watt watched the lid of the kettle bobbing up and down when the water was boiling, was he fated to do that. Was it ‘written’ that he was to invent the steam engine, that he was to be followed by Richard Trevithick and George Stephenson, who made Watt’s engines run on tracks? It was propitious, to say the least, that these events came about when they did, since it is difficult to think, nowadays, how society would have managed without the railway system.

In modern science, whilst it is true that fundamental atomic processes are indeterminate, it is also true that on the macroscale, those same processes are law-governed – by laws such as the statistical law of entropy, for example. Was it only probable, then, that those historic events happened the way they did? In that case, it was probable that if those people hadn’t done what they did, then someone else would have done it instead. If society had to have that kind of transport at that stage in history, then the probability was that something like steam locomotion would have been invented, regardless of by whom. Is fate, then, a function of probability? At least it transpires that fate and determinacy are not the same. So moral freedom and fate are not logically contradictory, as first it seems.

The laws of human society are not the same, of course, as those that govern atomic processes. Unlike the laws of physics, they are laws that can be disobeyed. Nevertheless it is well known, especially by people such as insurance agents, salesmen and casino managers, that much of human activity conforms to the rules of statistics. Yet it is also true, of course, that individuals may break those rules – as we have reasoned, by obeying their own inner promptings in despite of the social norms. These are the innovators, the individuals who, typically, direct the course of history

Were these innovative individuals fated to do what they do? And if they succeeded, was it moral for them to have achieved what they did? What moral virtue was there in it if their actions were fated? Was it moral for Einstein to have discovered his formula for mass-energy which led to the destruction of Hiroshima and Nagasaki? Was all that suffering somehow necessary for launching the Nuclear Age, and was Einstein destined to do what he did? Nuclear power, like steam power, was there in the nature of things to be discovered by someone or other, so the coming of the industrial and military employment of nuclear energy was, perhaps, inevitable. Einstein was morally offended by the use to which his discovery was put, and yet it is difficult now, in view of the extent to which society has come to depend on nuclear energy, to think of how nuclear energy could not have been discovered.

It may be argued, of course, that there are also in nature, potential sources of power which are more benign than nuclear energy, and that if Einstein had not discovered nuclear energy, the world might have come to depend more on such things as biofuels, wind power and tidal power. The same goes for steam power. If James Watt had not harnessed the power of steam, then the internal combustion engine might have been discovered sooner and become the primary natural alternative.

The conclusion from this is that the actual history of these world-changing events was shaped as much by the idiosyncrasies of the innovators as by the needs of their contemporary society. Yet we may still sensibly regard these events as fated because, as we recall, ‘fated’ is not the same as ‘predetermined’. In that case, whereas predeterminacy permits of just one outcome, fate is steered by two separate influences, one being the statistics of the ambient situation, and the other by the inner promptings of the individuals involved. The only logical way, then, it seems, of reconciling fate and free will is to conclude that it is the interplay of these two ‘inner’ and ‘outer’ influences that decides the course of history.

What, then, of morality in this situation? From what has been reasoned so far, it is possible for the individuals involved either to succeed or fail in their lives, though not necessarily by the standards of society but by their own moral standards. For someone clearly perceiving that something is wrong, and aware of his capacity to put it right, if that person fails to do so or to support anyone who does, they may well be judged as ‘lacking in moral fibre’. This reflects the discussion that was written about earlier, about life being a ‘test’. Some people may rightly feel that they are directed by fate and yet at some stage or other fall short of fulfilling their destiny for lack of ‘what it takes’, in which case it is left to others to succeed where they failed.

There is also, of course, that aspect of fate in which it seems that one person is fated to survive some disaster or set of disasters which overwhelm many others. Is it sensible to assume that there are individuals who are specially favoured in this way, ‘by fate’? Some people have been known to ‘tempt fate’ by playing Russian roulette or something similar, and survived to achieve fame and glory. For example, Wolfe of Quebec is said to have climbed dangerous spires to see if he was destined to survive.

Is this fair, that one person is fated to survive where others do not? This was discussed previously, in relation to suffering, which was deemed logically impenetrable. The answer, it may be recalled, was that this logical impenetrability might, in itself, be regarded as significant insofar as otherwise there could be no meaning to concepts of ‘virtue’, heroism’ and so forth. If Wolfe had known for certain that he would survive those tests, then there would not have been any heroism in what he did, of the sort that was required in his nature in order for him to become what he was, the famous general. What ‘forces’, then, might we suppose prepared the way for him to achieve what he did? Were all these testing circumstances set out for him in the future, like questions in a prepared examination paper? But that suggests determinacy, not fate?

Are there, then, elements of determinacy in what we call fate? That seems to be a logical possibility. It could be argued that free will is the propensity to change sets of determinate tracks at suitable points. However, physics tells us that there is no such determinacy in nature, that the nearest we get to determinacy is maximum probability. Is it, then, a set of probabilities that paved the way for people like Wolfe? Again, there is no logical contradiction in that, which makes it a feasible proposition. Is it, then, strands of probability, rather than of determinacy that are set out for us ahead of time? Then how do these strands fare when entangled with the strands of as many moral agents that there are? How far can any one of them be thought to stretch intact into the future when interfered with on such a scale? In other words, how does a single track of probability interact with the probability of the whole? But so far as we have seen, there is no probability of the whole. In other words, there can be no assured fate of the whole, because it is the strands which, individually and in relation to the others, decide the future of the whole. There is no mystery about the interactions between those strands. They are the ones we observe in daily intercommunication between individuals acting according to their own particular agendas, in a tussle between external social pressures and their own inner promptings. Every such contact skews the fatalistic probabilities into improbabilities.

Suppose, then, that I am a bank teller, and that the probability of my arriving for work at nine o’clock every weekday morning approaches one (where, in information theory, a probability of one is tantamount to certainty, whereas a probability of zero implies impossibility). For years, let us say, this remains my ‘fate’, of continuing in that humdrum way. Then, suddenly, some inner prompting impels me to leave that occupation, as Gauguin did, from his bank teller’s job, and travel to the South Seas to indulge my talent for painting. The strand of ‘fate’ (probability) that directed me in my previous occupation has now switched to another strand in which, barring interference from other strands, the probabilities approach one that I indulge my superior talent for painting. Was I then, fated to become that famous painter, and were the circumstances prepared for this to happen? Since there are two sets of probabilities (‘fates’) involved, one of which lies in the possibility of indulging the talent I have for painting and the other, in the respective sets of circumstances, the answer may, quite consistently, be yes, but just so long as by ‘fated’, here, is not meant ‘predetermined’.

Are gifted people like Gauguin, then, somehow ‘protected’ by ‘fate’ against life-threatening situations to which they would otherwise succumb, just so as to ensure consummation of their potentialities? In other words, do these people have something like a ‘guardian angel’ protecting them through life? That, to say the least, is an uncomfortable suggestion since it smacks of determinism and amorality. However, morality is secured by the fact that if there are people who are ‘fated’ in that way to achieve something momentous, they themselves could not know that for certain. They might guess at it and even anticipate it, especially if, in hindsight, the numbers of ‘fateful interventions’ are stacked up. Nevertheless such a person may well feel that at any minute, some accident may end their life – like the sword that hung precariously over the head of Damocles. From that person’s point of view, then, morality is just as it is for everyone faced as we all are with the uncertainties of life that, as we have argued, are there to test us.

So morality is the same for everyone, whether ‘gifted’ or not. But what about individuals who, before they reach the age of moral responsibility, having died at birth, say, or in infancy? If life is a moral test, then how are they to be judged? The same goes, of course, for individuals who may be brain-damaged, either from birth or at some later stage, so that there can be no question of moral responsibility for them.

Might it not be, then, that morality, in that case, consists of the responsibility of others towards those disadvantaged individuals? Parents of, say, a Mongoloid or a severely crippled child, for instance, may well feel that they are being morally tested. Some people may feel that for such disadvantaged persons to be relieved in that way of moral responsibility is to be highly privileged insofar as, for them, whatever might be the ‘afterlife’ – in ‘heaven’ or whatever – is one of assured innocence.

Our logical method, then, of eliminating beliefs that are self-contradictory, as a test of feasibility supports what is understood by commonsense words and phrases such as, on the one hand ‘fate’, ‘destiny’, ‘heaven’, ‘the afterlife’ and, on the other hand, ‘moral fibre’, ‘guts’, ‘bottle’, ‘courage’ and suchlike. In this case, anything like ‘prayer’ would constitute a desire for the strength and fortitude to realise the best in ourselves and to do what is best for others. But, surely, this is no more nor less than the norm for most people, irrespective of any denominational differences of religion, politics or whatever. Even humanists and other agnostics seek to live by those very same standards, as did the Cynics, the Stoics, the Romantics and so on, in days of yore. So, what, do we conclude from this? It has to be that it is what commonsense has known, all along, throughout the ages, namely that we are morally accountable for our actions, that there are such things as conscience and courage, moral responsibility to ourselves and our community and, in most religious cases, that death does not relieve us of the burden of judgement for our actions. Indeed, from the logical point of view, it is interesting to consider the null case. This is to think of how it would be, given the built-in uncertainties of life, for a free-thinking and self-directed creature like man to be without morality altogether. Any notion of ‘community’, ‘society’, ‘nation’ or whatever would then be devoid of meaning. All that would remain would be either a widespread distribution of completely isolated individuals or a collection of the same in a state of unadulterated anarchy. It follows, then, that insofar as human society is such a global phenomenon – indeed a fact of nature – any hypothesis to the effect that a free-willed creature like Man could live a life devoid of morality is thereby refuted.

Morality, then, in these terms, is as much a phenomenon, hence a natural fact, as human society itself. No less a natural fact is the phenomenon of religion, which, in some form or another has always been a large part of society – if, by ‘religion’ is meant belief in a morality that is cosmically accountable both here and in the hereafter. According to the mechanistic/materialist tradition of Western science, the phenomenon of religion, no matter how widespread, is specious. If we have proved anything, it is that this classical assumption is false.

One’s own conviction from all this is that there is nothing specious about religion. If left to itself, without idealistic interference, its core values are logically inviolate. The only fault with religion, one might say, lies in our attempts to organise it and to impose our own idealised versions of it on others.

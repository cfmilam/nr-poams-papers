Dark Matter - revised version

N. Vivian Pope — 1973

DARK MATTER (revised version) At the bottom of Europe’s deepest mine, in Boulby, North Yorkshire, is installed a laboratory containing scientific hardware in which millions of pounds have been invested for the purpose of answering what has been described as ‘the biggest single challenge in physics today’.

Led by Professor Tim Sumner of Imperial College, London, the aim of this team of scientists has been, for sixteen years, to discover evidence for the existence of what has been called ‘dark matter’ in the form of ‘weakly interacting massive particles’, known as WIMPs.

So far, however, the search has been completely unsuccessful, so that the notion of ‘dark matter’ remains purely hypothetical. Why, then, has this expensive research so doggedly continued when all the evidence seems to be against it? The reason is that, in theory, something has to be there to make up the ninety-six percent of our universe that seems to be missing.

In the 1920s, the astronomer Jan Oort had discovered that the orbital velocities in the Milky Way do not decrease with increasing distance from the galactic centre as Newton’s laws demand. However, he did not conjecture the cause to be ‘dark matter’.

Instead, he felt the apparent anomaly would eventually be cleared up by further, more detailed observations of matter in the Milky Way. Then, in 1933, the astronomer, Fritz Zwicky, revealed as a result of his study of clusters of galaxies orbiting one another, that on the basis of the known laws of physics, the forces holding those galaxies together were insufficiently accounted for in terms of the masses of the stars and so on that can be seen.

He calculated that at least ten times more mass was required to account for the observed motions of the galaxies. In 1973, James Peebles and Jeremiah Ostriker were engaged in pioneering work with computer models of the universe based on the known data obtained from astronomical observations. Up till that time, in spite of the findings of Oort and Zwicky, scientists had been satisfied with their understanding of the cosmos.

However, the models of the universe constructed by Peebles and Ostriker, on the basis of the known data did not compute. There was not enough matter to maintain stability. But no extra matter was to be found. It was decided that this inscrutable ‘something’ had to have mass but none of the other properties of ordinary matter, which is why it can neither be seen nor – so far – detected.

Finally, the astronomer, Vera Rubin, studying whole galaxies in the 1970s, reported that Newton’s gravitational law does not hold for the rotations of these galaxies in the way it so clearly does for our solar system, where the ordinary rotation curve is an embodiment of the law of gravity.

By measuring the Doppler shift of their H-alpha emission lines, she found that the rotation curves of the spiral galaxies are mostly flat, that they rotate like disks of stars held in place by something invisible that exerts extra gravity. Riccardo Giovanelli, of Cornell university, at the huge radio telescope in Arecibo, confirmed Rubin’s observations by reporting that even at the farthest reaches of the universe, the orbiting rate of interstellar gas is no slower than that of the stars.

It seemed, from a study of spiral galaxies (by Rubin and W.K. Ford) that there is matter outside the bulge of the galaxy which has not been observed but exerts a gravitational force that increases the velocities of the gas clouds beyond what is to be expected from the known distribution of visible matter.

Subsequently, all the various theories and observations have seemed to fit together. Thus, although there remains no hard evidence in support of its existence, in both professional and popular science a belief in the underlying reality of invisible, or ‘dark’, matter has become highly fashionable.

This postulated ‘dark matter’, it was decided, is all around us, not in ordinary atoms but in some new form of matter that has mass only, enough to account for the deficit of mass that is to be expected in terms of Newton’s gravitational law. This is what led to Sumner’s hunt for new particles that are alleged to fly straight through the earth and interact, if at all, only very weakly with ordinary matter. It is assumed that this is why they have so far remained undetected, even by the best detectors in the deepest mines.

So, it was decided, some entirely new, revolutionary concept of matter was needed to explain the observations. However, some scientists regard these searches for ‘dark matter’ as altogether vain and a waste of time. This is the view of Prof. Mordechai Milgrom, of the Weizmann Institute, in Israel [1].

But, he says, the research will continue and will stop ‘only when the money stops’ [2]. According to Milgrom, the whole conception of ‘dark matter’ is a mistake. He points out that, logically, there are other ways to get the required extra gravity, such as, for instance, ‘making gravity stronger in places’.

He published his theory of varying gravity in 1983 [3]. However, at the time, any idea of varying gravity, no matter how well it fitted the data, was considered much too radical, so it went largely neglected. Other theories were regarded as much more sensible and less heretical.

Carlos Frenk, for instance, of Durham University, UK, mobilised the ‘Big Bang’ theory as an explanation. Saul Perlmutter, of the University of California, Berkeley, on the basis of his ‘galactic recession’ interpretation of the Hubble redshift, argues that while earlier theory leads us to expect that the expansion of the universe should be slowing down, it is, in fact, speeding up.

The reason for this, Perlmutter hypothesised, was due to the existence, not of ‘dark matter’. but of dark energy. So nothing is missing, he concluded. The universe is made of twenty-one percent of ‘dark matter’ and seventy-five percent of ‘dark energy’, with only the remainder being ordinary matter.

This idea, which is successfully adjusted throughout to fit all the known facts, has now become known as the ‘Standard Model’ of cosmology. One of the biggest sceptics of this so-called ‘Standard Model’ is the astronomer, Professor Michael Disney, of the University of Wales, Cardiff.

In his view, it is all ‘wildly unrealistic’, having no basis in Physics. In opposition to these sceptics, Carlos Frenk, on the basis of the Big Bang theory, defends the Standard Model by producing computer simulations of the cosmogony of the universe by feeding-in the best of the known observational and theoretical data.

These, pictorially speaking are, to say the least, very convincing. Against this, Disney offers the truism that by such means ‘you can always get something that you want to get’. As he picturesquely puts it, if you see a pink elephant, you can always generate a computer model to fit whatever you have seen, or think you have seen. But you could also do the same for a green or any other kind of elephant, real or imaginary. This negative view of the Standard Model is disputed by James Peebles. He claims to have indisputable evidence for it. So does David Spurgel, of Princeton University. In their estimation, that Standard Model has now ‘come of age’. This is despite the fact that all evidence of ‘dark matter’, ‘dark energy’, etc., remains completely elusive. Theoretically, it all fits, and that is sufficient for some. Others, however, are more in favour of Milgrom’s alternative, that – perish the thought! – Newton’s Law of Universal Gravitation might have been wrong, that the sacrosanct ‘gravitational constant’, G, is not a constant at all but a factor that in some cases may be variable. This is precisely what is maintained by the authors of this book, as recounted in these chapters. According to the PopeOsborne Angular Momentum Synthesis (POAMS), what is ‘missing’ from the traditional account of the motions of the galaxies is nothing so mysterious as ‘dark matter’ or ‘dark energy’ but what might be called ‘dark’ spin (‘dark’ only because it has been neglected). In an angular momentum nexus, as opposed to a ‘gravitational’, one, all spin angular momenta (which are every bit as visible as orbital angular momentum) are an integral part of the overall balance of conserved angular momentum, being all part and parcel of the same equation [4]. From this point of view, it is only to be expected that if this spin part of angular momentum is left out of account – as it is in our conventional reverence for Newtonian gravity – then the astronomical data, quite

obviously, cannot

compute. Far from being invisible, mysterious and inscrutable, like ‘dark matter’ and ‘dark energy’, this missing spin angular momentum is all upfront and measurable, so long as our current classical precepts will allow it. Of course, to suggest that ‘G changes’ is a heresy. But Rubin also now supports Milgrom in this, his sacrilegious suggestion. The present authors’ reasons for accepting the variability of G are even more sacrilegious, requiring a deeper revision of current physics than these theoreticians may ever have anticipated; namely, a wholesale paradigm shift in our customary ways of thinking about matter, space and time.

One is reminded, here, of a conversation that Pope once had at CERN, in Geneva, with the late John Bell (of Bell’s Inequalities fame). ‘How can you be happy,’ Pope asked him, ‘with these sorts of impossible, purely ad hoc concepts of inscrutable particles?’ ‘Well,’ Bell replied, waving his hand towards the sumptuous surroundings of CERN: ‘It’s much better, don’t you think, than working down a mine!’ Pope now thinks this conversation rather ironic in view of the fact that Tim Sumner has since extended the study of astronomical physics down to those very deepest mining levels!

It is to be noted that, earlier than this, A.D. Osborne, in his 1980 Doctoral Thesis, Gravitation and Dynamical Systems, had hypothesised that ‘G’ is not a constant.

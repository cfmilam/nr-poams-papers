The Quantum is Kids' Stuff

N. Vivian Pope — 1986
A Sequel to 'Relativity is Kids' Stuff'
(With Apologies, again, to Enid Blyton)
A condensed and simplified version of a paper circulated at the 1986 EASST Conference, at the Council of Europe, Strasbourg[2]

Noddy and Big Ears had just returned from a fishing trip and were sitting in the garden, sunning themselves. Now quite grown-up and doing well at school, Noddy was keen to satisfy his new-found scientific curiosity. 'You've explained relativity to me, Big Ears', he said. 'Could you now explain to me the quantum theory?'

'Do you know what the "quantum" means?', asked Big Ears.

'I think so,' replied Noddy. 'It means that in the physics which deals with small amounts of matter nothing takes place smoothly and continuously. Everything comes in bits and happens in jerks, but nobody really knows why.'

'Well, that will do for a start,' said Big Ears. 'But it's no mystery really, because it follows logically from what you already know about relativity.'

Noddy was interested. 'How?', he asked.

'I'll explain,' said Big Ears. 'Now you've learned that in relativity the mass of a body is a measure relative to the observer.'

'Yes,' said Noddy, attentively.

'And you know that in the same theory, all mass is energy.'

'I do,' Noddy replied. 'The mass of a body is nothing, absolutely and in itself. It is an energy relatively to some observer.'

'Well, then, Noddy, according to relativity —'

'That follows, Big Ears.'

'So, in observing a body, the energy spent in observing it has to be some portion of that body's mass-energy relative to the observer.'

'Yes, it has to be!' said Noddy.

'And, if that observation is an entirely optical one,' Big Ears continued, 'then that relative energy lost by the body and gained by you, the observer, is in the form of light. So in that case, light-energy is the pure relativistic connection between observers and physical objects.'

'That makes sense,' Noddy agreed, 'which is, I suppose, why light is so important to relativity theory.'

'Quite, Noddy. And what is relativity theory all about?' asked Big Ears.

'The relativity of length and time,' Noddy replied.

'And what is it that's measured in the light-spectrum you've done in your physics course?' urged Big Ears.

'Light?' ventured Noddy. 'No, that's a silly answer!' he decided. 'What the spectrograph measures is wavelength, or frequency.'

'Very good, Noddy!' Big Ears encouraged. 'And what is "frequency"?'

'The inverse of time,' answered Noddy.

'And time is relative, isn't it Noddy?'

'Yes, it is,' said Noddy, thoughtfully.

'So wouldn't you expect the same formula which expresses relative time also to express observational frequency?'

'I see what you mean', said Noddy. 'The formula for the frequencies of the light-spectrum should be derivable from the relativistic-time formula, directly. That would by-pass all the rigmarole of atoms and light theory. But can it be done, Big Ears?'

'Fetch your notebook and pencil,' said Big Ears, 'and I'll show you.'

'Now,' said Big Ears, when Noddy returned. 'You've seen that Pythagoras' Theorem is all you need to know in order to understand relativity?'

'Yes,' said Noddy, 'that was very interesting!'[1]

'Well,' continued Big Ears, 'it's the same with the quantum. Take that same time-formula that we had in relativity — the Pythagoras one giving the stretched time $T$, due to the distance travelled, $s$ …'

'The time-dilation formula', said Noddy, brightly.

'Clever boy!' exclaimed Big Ears. 'Well, here's that formula again,' and Big Ears wrote:

$$T = \sqrt{t^2 + (s/c)^2} \tag{1}$$

'Now,' Big Ears continued, 'all the measures in that time-formula, you remember, were in units of years. Well, those units are too large for quantum physics. For this it is best that we use ordinary seconds and metres. But metres, of course, are measures of length, so to turn them into time-measures in seconds we have to divide by a constant $c$. Every 300 million metres of distance is then the same as a second of time.'

'Yes,' said Noddy. 'I remember what you said about the constant $c$ when you were explaining relativity.'

'Good!' said Big Ears, encouragingly. 'So now your time-formula (1) looks like this:'

$$T = \sqrt{t^2 + (s/c)^2} \tag{2}$$

When he was sure that Noddy had understood, Big Ears continued: 'How do you remember, Noddy, what we said about relative velocity, $v$, being the distance $s$ travelled by the body in the stretched or dilated time $T$?'

'Yes, I do,' answered Noddy.

'So what does that suggest about $s$ in equation (2)?' asked Big Ears.

'Well, since $v$ is $s$ divided by $T$, $s$ is $v$ multiplied by $T$, so we could replace $s$ in equation (2) by $vT$,' said Noddy.

Smiling his approval, Big Ears wrote, as Noddy had suggested:

$$T = \sqrt{t^2 + (vT/c)^2} \tag{3}$$

'There,' he said. 'That is your Pythagoras' time-formula in ordinary seconds and metres, with the velocity $v$ included as in relativity.'

'Now, so far we are still talking about relativity. What leads to the quantum, Noddy, is this. You can see, especially in formula (2), that these formulae cannot produce values of $T$ less than $t$, which is when $s$, or $vT$, is nought. So whatever value you give to $t$ at the start remains, as it were, the "time-quantum" of the whole series expressed by the formula.'

'Yes, I see that,' Noddy said.

'But these are purely dimensional formulae,' continued Big Ears, 'so we aren't concerned with values at this stage, only units, like the metre, the second, the joule, and so on, in the different measure-categories — and with the various constants interrelating them, of course.'

'I'm with you so far,' Noddy encouraged.

'Now of course,' Big Ears explained, 'we could also choose any fraction $1/N$ of our conventional unit $t$ for our basic coefficient, or "time-quantum" of the series $T$, where $N$ is any one in the series of natural numbers, or integers. We could then have any number $n$ of these fractional units $t/N$, where $n$ is some other number in the natural series. So any multiple whatever of $t$, integer or non-integer, can be expressed as $n(t/N)$ where $n$ and $N$ are both integers.'

'I see,' said Noddy eagerly. 'So we can replace $T$ in these formulae by those multiples $n/N$ of $t$.'

'Correct!' said Big Ears. 'So formula (3) is now:' and he wrote:

$$n(t/N) = \sqrt{(t/N)^2 + (vn(t/N)/c)^2} \tag{4}$$

'That's getting to look complicated,' said Noddy.

'Yes,' said Big Ears, 'so let's simplify it. Can you do that, Noddy?'

'I'll try,' said Noddy bravely. 'First, square everything to get rid of the square root:'

$$n^2(t/N)^2 = (t/N)^2 + (vn(t/N)/c)^2 \tag{5}$$

'Then cancel the $(t/N)^2$ on both sides:'

$$n^2 = 1 + (vn/c)^2 \tag{6}$$

'How's that?', Noddy said, when he had finished.

'Fine!' said Big Ears, encouragingly. 'Now rearrange the formula so as to solve for $v^2$.'

Noddy thought. Then, 'Ah, I see! First you subtract 1 from both sides, like this:'

$$n^2 - 1 = (vn/c)^2 \tag{7}$$

'Then divide through by $n^2$:'

$$(n^2 - 1)/n^2 = v^2/c^2 \tag{8}$$

'Then multiply by $c^2$ and turn the formula around:'

$$v^2 = c^2\left[\frac{n^2 - 1}{n^2}\right] \tag{9}$$

'There,' said Noddy. 'I enjoyed doing that, but where does it get us?'

'I'll show you,' said Big Ears. 'Let's see if you remember your mechanics. What is $v^2$ in your mechanical formula?'

'It comes into the kinetic energy formula $K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$,' said Noddy, 'where $K$ stands for kinetic energy and $m$ for mass.'

'Correct, Noddy,' said Big Ears. 'From that we have $v^2 = 2K/m$. Replacing $v^2$ in (9) by $2K/m$ and multiplying throughout by the mass $m$ then gives us:'

$$2K = mc^2\left[\frac{n^2 - 1}{n^2}\right] \tag{10}$$

'Now I don't know whether you know this, Noddy,' he continued, 'but $2K$, in atomic physics, is an energy $h\nu$, where $\nu$ stands for frequency in units of hertz, and $h$ is a constant called Planck's constant. So our $2K$ in (10) can be replaced by $h\nu$, which energy-expression can be denoted by $\varepsilon$, giving:'

$$\varepsilon = mc^2\left[\frac{n^2 - 1}{n^2}\right] \tag{11}$$

'That looks like the famous $\varepsilon = mc^2$ formula in relativity for converting mass and energy!', said Noddy, excitedly.

'That is just what it is,' said Big Ears, 'but derived in a slightly different way. It can also be written as:'

$$\varepsilon = mc^2\left[1 - \frac{1}{n^2}\right] \tag{12}$$

'This formula, Noddy, tells us three things. First, with $n$ at infinity the formula becomes $\varepsilon = mc^2$, which means that mass and energy are inter-convertible. Second, the mass-energy $\varepsilon$ converted in this way, according to the formula, is a series of fractions (in the square brackets) whose sum is a maximum mass-energy $mc^2$. Third, since the $n$-values in the formula are integers, those fractions are discrete, or quantised. Here, for instance, are the sorts of values of $\varepsilon$ we get when we put into (11) the successive integer values of $n$:'

$n =$1;2;3;4;etc.
$\varepsilon =$0;$(3/4)mc^2$;$(8/9)mc^2$;$(15/16)mc^2$;etc.

'And nothing in between,' said Noddy. 'That's interesting!'

'Something else which might interest you, Noddy, is that multiplying together the basic physical constants; i.e., Planck's constant $h$, our familiar distance-time conversion-constant $c$, and $R$, the Rydberg-number (you can look these up in any book of physical constants), gives you a quantum measure of energy. If you make that the value of $mc^2$ in (11), then the formula immediately becomes that of the spectrum of hydrogen, the basic element. In that formula, $m$ is the maximum amount of mass the hydrogen atom may lose or gain in optical interactions with other atoms. The energies $\varepsilon$ in Table 1 are then the energies of those spectral lines which scientists take as the signatures of the atoms — in this case, basic hydrogen. You can look that up in your science encyclopaedia under what is called the "Lyman Series" for atomic hydrogen. That will give you something to study when you're back at school.'

'I see,' said Noddy. 'So not only does relativity relativise distance and time, in the way you showed me. It also turns mass into energy and quantises that energy, into the bargain!'

'Yes,' said Big Ears, 'it's all part of the same relativising process.'

Noddy thought about it for a while. Then, 'It makes good sense!' he said. ■

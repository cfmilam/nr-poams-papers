Why Are We So Uncritical of 'Scientific' Statements?

N. Vivian Pope — 2001

Voltaire (1695–1778), the famous French sceptical philosopher and writer, was notorious for his penetrative questioning of the Catholic Establishment of his time. He also, in 1737, wrote the Éléments de la Philosophie de Newton (Elements of Newton's Philosophy), explaining Newton's theory of gravitational attraction to his somewhat recalcitrant French contemporaries. But why was he so critical of the former and so uncritical of the latter when they were equally open to question? This presents something of a mystery when we consider how plainly flawed was Newton's gravitational theory.

For instance, how could anyone have accepted Newton's statement that all free-moving bodies travel in straight lines, when they so plainly don't? Why was this swallowed, without question, by generation after generation of Science graduates? And how was it possible that these same Science graduates did not see what a confidence trick it was (albeit unintentionally) to say that the reason why no free-moving bodies ever travel in those straight lines is because they are prevented from doing so by a universal and inscrutable 'force of gravity' exerted by those bodies on one another across the void.

It reminds us of the story of the man who claimed payment for keeping down the tigers in Hyde Park. 'But there are no tigers in Hyde Park,' he was told. 'Which only shows,' he replied, 'what a great job I'm doing!' And think of how much more compounded was that con by those followers of Newton who invented a whole plethora of no less inscrutable in vacuo 'field-forces', a different 'force' for each and every different kind of non-rectilinear motion, and then set it as a serious task for students, by dint of more and more 'elegant' theoretical inventions, to 'unify' that mess of purely artificial 'field-forces' that their mentors had created!

But if this propensity for uncritical acceptance of Newton's pronouncements were not sufficiently puzzling, then how much more puzzling is the fact that generations of students-become-professors have acquiesced to the completely meaningless statement, by Newton's most illustrious successor, Einstein, that light travels in the vacuum at a speed which is constant, not only relative to the vacuum itself but also to everything within it, whether relatively moving or stationary? Yet it is on these and other meaningless interpretations of nature that our modern science is literally founded, and we wonder why we have lost sight of the nature that Science was supposed to reveal to us! In the words of George Berkeley, 'We kick up a dust and complain we cannot see'.

But what possible excuse can there be, commensurable with the original ideal of scientific integrity, for allowing such a situation to develop unchecked? How did those who, like Voltaire, strained at every 'gnat' of religious orthodoxy swallow such whole 'camels' of scientistic nonsense? Were the axioms of orthodox Religion any more dogmatic and insecure than those of its 'scientistic' usurper? With modern science now in as stultified a state as was ever the dogma it is supposed to have ousted, to what benign agency may we turn to continue the search for truth that was claimed on behalf of science? As the woman said to the man who saved her from the attentions of a rapist: 'And now, Sir, who's going to save me from you?'

What redress, then, do we have against those second-millennium worthies who saved natural philosophy from its first-millennium gang-rape in the name of Religion only to gang-rape it again in the name of Science? Who shall we trust, in this third millennium, to protect our natural curiosity from further hijackings and violations by more and more fame-hungry academics? Who else but ourselves, in free and unselfconscious, rational discussion? Newton declared that he had seen further 'by standing on the shoulders of the Giants'. But with nature now virtually obliterated in every direction by so many towering intellectuals, perhaps it is time for those of us who seek the light of common understanding to crawl out between their knees. ■

The author, Viv Pope, is a retired lecturer in Logic and Philosophy of Science. He is still engaged in research in these areas, at the Department of Mathematics, Keele University and the Department of Physics at the University of Wales, Swansea.

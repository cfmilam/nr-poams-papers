Linguistic Analysis (2)

N. Vivian Pope

Linguistic Analysis The Linguistic Movement, so called, stems from Ernst Mach’s philosophical teaching that all experience, in science as in commonsense, is based on what he called ‘sense data’. This also includes the data supplied by instruments of one kind or another, and every observed object is an instrument in his sense.

This, of course, was the root of Einstein’s Theory of Relativity, which makes the very existence of physical objects, their dimensions of length, duration and even their mass – as well as other defining properties and qualities, such as density, solidity, etc., relative to a specified observer.

Mach’s forerunner in this way of thinking was Bishop George Berkeley (1685-1753) who was the first to stress this dependence of physical existence on the ‘observer’. Berkeley’s view, however, ran into trouble on account of his notorious dictum esse is percipi (to be is to be perceived), which invited the inference that the material world is no more than a figment of one’s imagination.

This is the position known as solipsism, the notion that nothing is physically real, that it is all some kind of illusion, or dream. (Indeed, some modern critics of Einstein and Relativity, despite all the many proofs of that theory’s validity, reject it out of hand simply for fear of that imaginary consequence.)

But, of course, there is no question of relativity implying solipsism because there can be no observation which is an entirely private affair. All observation, in all sectors of commonsense and science, is essentially communal, involving other observers and their perspectives on things, all communicated in spoken, written and behavioural language.

This is where the Linguistic dimension comes into play, that is, in the codified classifying, dividing and categorising of sensory and instrumental data, or phenomena. Some of the earlier thinkers in this neo-Machian phenomenalist tradition sought to codify scientific language in terms of mathematical logic, in the belief that there are, in nature, fundamental bits of data which are the same in our observations of physical objects as in those objects themselves.

All one had to do, therefore, according to this view, was to dump ordinary language as being too ‘fluffy’ and imprecise for science, and to build-up a ‘meta-language’ of exact descriptions of the actual, most elemental bits of data and then reconstruct language purely along lines of formal logic and mathematics.

In this way, it was supposed, an exact, absolutely objective scientific language could be obtained, purged of all illusion and subjectivity. The precursor of this approach to scientific language was Ludwig Wittgenstein, in his earlier ‘Logical Positivism’ phase, which gained quite a large following among Philosophy students and graduates of that time.

Unfortunately for that movement, it was soon found that as an approach to science it was completely unworkable and had to be abandoned. This led to the next phase in Linguistic philosophy, which was to recognise that all classifications and divisions of things in language, even scientific language, are contextual.

Perception theorists know that not even the most primitive of organisms respond to specific, isolated stimuli, that even the most elemental stimuli are responded to as integral parts of perceptual wholes or character-complexes, called Gestalten. The same applies to sense-data and/or instrument data in the context of scientific language.

That is to say, there can be no language of the sort imagined by the Logical Positivists, which has point-to-point correspondence with any kind of supposed elemental data. Realising this contextual nature of meaning in language is what led to the next phase in the Linguistic movement. This was to regard what its supporters called Ordinary Language as the ultimate arbiter of truth and reality. (This was, by default,

the classical ‘King’s English’ of the Oxbridge elite.) In universities where these departments of Philosophy and Science came together, they were ‘strange bedfellows’. The philosophers were anti-Science and the scientists were antiPhilosophy. The philosophers scorned any kind of technical language as ‘scientism’ and the scientists derided the philosophers as the ‘Language Police’.

The high point of this conflict was exemplified at the University College of Wales, Swansea, where Wittgenstein’s ‘wing-man’ Rush Rees presided over the Philosophy Department. So much at odds were these departments that on one occasion Rees and a member of the science department actually came into physical exchanges over it.

So far as these philosophers were concerned, this ‘Ordinary Language’ was not to be sullied by ‘jargon’ of any kind – by physics ‘jargon’ in particular. At Swansea, for instance, any talk among students about such things as, say, ‘atoms’ ‘electrons’ and the like – indeed, just about everything with which the Physics Department was concerned – was forbidden by their Philosophy mentor, Rush Rees.

In other universities, during the 1960s, this same ‘Linguistic’ movement gained momentum. At Oxford, for example it was fronted by the likes of John L. Austin and Gilbert Ryle, and also, in the U.S.A., by Norman Malcolm, of Cornell university. Any student challenging this movement, for whatever reason, good or bad, was sure to be marked down in his exams.

Plainly, however, this Ordinary Language was every bit as unworkable as its predecessor, Logical Positivism, and in due time it died a natural death. After its heyday in Swansea, during the 1960s, this Linguistic influence led to the development of Normal Realism by this present author, Viv Pope.

Basically a telecommunications engineer (for almost twenty years prior to university), Pope was out of sympathy with this scholastic anti-science movement. Finally, he signified this, by walking out, conspicuously, in the middle of one of Rush Rees’ most vigorous diatribes against what he called ‘scientism’.

For Pope, this ‘Linguistic’ movement was basically sound but as a Philosophy of Science graduate (at the University College of North Wales, Bangor) he sought to merge the interests of progressive science and commonsense. This cross-disciplinary combination of academically separated Arts and Science was neither supported nor encouraged by the university.

Like its predecessors, Pope’s version of linguistic philosophy, albeit academically unclassifiable – indeed, ‘heretical’, is dismissive of much of the high-flown kind of pure jargon enjoyed by scientists that he regards as detrimental to commonsense understanding.

He has sought to remove these jargonised over-interpretations of observational and experimental data wherever possible in favour of more logically streamlined and conceptually economical uses of ordinary commonsense language. In 1982, this merger of Science and Philosophy was continued in collaboration with Dr. Anthony Osborne, at Keele University, Staffordshire, UK.

Together they discovered that an angular momentum formula alone can explain the orbital motions of bodies, from spiral galaxies to the so-called ‘electrons and ‘protons’ without reference to any in vacuo ‘force-fields’ of ‘gravitation’, ‘electrostatics’, ‘magnetostatics’ or whatever.

This academically unclassifiable, cross-disciplinary collaboration between exponents of Arts and Science has become known as the PopeOsborne Angular Momentum Synthesis (POAMS).

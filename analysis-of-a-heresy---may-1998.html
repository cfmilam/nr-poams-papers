Analysis of a Heresy - May 1998

ANALYSIS of a HERESY

Towards A 'Journalistic' Encapsulation of the Thesis of N.V. Pope

(in Preparation for seminars at Swansea)

(May 3rd, 1998)

1. Relevant personal details

In 1954 a short correspondence took place between a young Swansea man and Albert Einstein, just before the great man's death. This was later reported in the Swansea Evening Post under the title 'Swansea Telephone Lineman's Lines from Einstein' (1961 Philosophical Glimpses. p.15b.) also in the Herald of Wales as 'A Mini-Einstein Theory' (June 13th, 1964, P.G., p. 38). The recognition for these ideas that the reporters anticipated at the time has been slow in coming. Now, however, forty years on, there have been some interesting developments following a presentation of the theory by its author, Viv Pope, at Toronto, Canada. An international group including, in particular, some Russian physicists, have invited him to collaborate in developing his central theme of relativistic action-at-a-distance, which they see as having vital implications for modern electrodynamics. One of these scientists, Prof. Andrei Chubykalo, has been invited to the University College of Wales, Swansea, for discussions on this subject.

2. Academic History

Pope's educational career began, in Swansea, at Gors Road Infants School in 1936. In 1940, he moved to Cwmhwrla Junior School, then on to Manselton Secondary School. From there he went, at the age of thirteen, to the Swansea Junior Technical School (JTS) and at sixteen, transferred to Dynefor Grammar School. Distinctly unacademic in attitude and achievement, he was very much a 'free-range' thinker who learned most of his science in scrap-yards when truant from school. In this practical, hands-on way, he became interested in electronics and radio-communication and, by a natural extension of these interests, eventually in philosophical matters concerned with how bodies interact at great distances. In the Swansea Astronomical Society, of which his father, Don Pope (also a telephone lineman), was a founder member, and for which he (Pope junior) mixed the concrete for the Society's first telescope at Tycoch, he was introduced to the subject of Einstein and Relativity and to the puzzles which that theory notoriously generates. With plenty of ideas, but being almost completely untrained in the ways of presenting them, he married a teacher, his childhood sweetheart who had been a very promising pupil (in fact, an Oxbridge-hopeful) at the Girls' High School Swansea. With her tutelage and some casual help from authorities at University College of Wales, Swansea. e.g., J. R. Jones, Rush Rhees, Howard Mounce (Philosophy), Gareth Evans and J.R. Jones (Maths), Mel Hopkins and P. M. Davidson (Physics), he developed his own commonsense- geometrical alternative to Einstein's theory. It was during visits, on a day-release basis from his lineman's duties with the GPO Engineering Department (now British Telecom), to the university Philosophical Society to which, in the 1960's, he was invited by the then H.O.D., Prof. J. R. Jones, that he learned about modern philosophy from Rush Rhees, the well-known friend of the famous exponent of linguistic analysis, Ludwig Wittgenstein.

This was at the invitation of the Head of the Bangor Philosophy Department, Prof. C.K.Mundle. After obtaining an Honours degree in the History and Philosophy of Science, his employers, the GPO, who had not been sympathetic to the efforts of the Swansea University's authorities to get Pope 'de-industrialised' (his degree was Arts not Science), constructed his dismissal on the grounds, in effect, that he was now overqualified for his previous job of telephone lineman. With no other recourse left to him, having moved his family to live with him in North Wales, Pope stayed on at college in Bangor to obtain teaching qualifications and to pursue his bid for a higher degree on the basis of his innovative central idea. On the strength of these teaching qualifications, he obtained a position at a college in the Midlands, teaching Liberal Studies (to day-release students) and History and Philosophy of Science in Adult Education. He also taught Logic and History of Science for the Open University.

3. Educational Aims and Objectives

Pope's philosophical aim, as he presents it at conferences, particularly in his capacity as Secretary to a Physics Society in Cambridge, is to end what he calls 'the intellectual apartheid' between science and commonsense. This, he says, is not by any top-down, 'crumbs from the table' condescension on the part of science towards commonsense but by actually involving the latter with the former in a fully integrated 'two-way-interactive' partnership.

4. The essence of Pope's ideas

Pope's central idea, which proved extremely unpopular when it first appeared in academic and other circles, is that a fundamental presupposition of Einstein's famous 'Special Theory of Relativity' is false, namely, that light travels at a constant speed in a vacuum. In a vacuum, Pope argues, there is by definition nothing that can serve as a datum of reference for a speed, constant or otherwise, and he demonstrates that all the practical consequences of Einstein's theory can be deduced much more simply without all those references to the 'speed of light in the vacuum' that are usually considered obligatory. When this was presented for a higher degree, it was received by Pope's advisers and examiners in much the same way that, in 1615, Galileo's support for Copernicus' theory (that the earth is not the centre of the universe) was received by the Inquisition. Pope's dissertation was, to say the least, vigorously discouraged and his thesis was rejected twice in a situation that was later described by no less a person than the philosopher Sir Karl Popper as 'scandalous'. (As someone at Bangor intimated to a mutual friend, at the time, 'The Department is having a great deal of trouble over Vivian Pope's M.A. thesis!' (P.G., p. 211)

For Pope, however, all was not totally lost. In 1985 he was invited by a member of the Maths Department of Keele University, Staffordshire, to develop his thesis under the aegis of the Department as an official research project. It was later suggested, by the Head of

5 The heretical nature of the theory

In 1981, a writer in Australia, Robert Traille, having read of Pope's work, described him to Professor Eric Laithwaite as 'another relevant heretic' (P.G. p.1713). What has damned Pope's thesis as a heresy, in the eyes of the establishment (i.e., of academics and non-academics alike) is his advocacy of a complete and fundamental switch away from thinking of space as a great empty room or receptacle in which physical events (quantum events) take place, towards thinking of space and time as having no existence except in these events themselves. 'No events, no space and no time' as Pope puts it. Natural phenomena are thus composed of quanta, similar to the way in which a TV scenario is composed of sequences of little fleeting light-dots or pixels, with nothing happening in between.

The sort of mental flipover this requires is the one that is foreshadowed, with the coming of computers, from the traditional mechanical-engineering accounts of natural phenomena to information-theoretical ones – not unlike the switch that is gradually taking place from analog to digital, as telephone and CD users now understand it. The implications of this conceptual shift, when fully worked out, are every bit as profound and far-reaching – and no doubt, disturbing – as those of Galileo's rejection of the biblical assumption that the earth is the centre of the universe. According to this flip-side way of thinking, the distance and time measures traditionally described as the 'speed of light' are dimensions projected out of the light as actually seen or instrumentally detected, not those of a primordial vacuum in which the light is supposed to travel like waves in water. This replaces the customary 'constant in a vacuum' interpretation of light with that of a constant in observation where, in projecting the distances of objects, measures in metres have the same constant ratio, c to measures in seconds for all observers in all circumstances – e.g., no matter whether they are moving or stationary with respect to one another. As Pope and his Keele colleague, Dr. Anthony Osborne, have demonstrated many times, this leads to a 'digital' (quantised) derivation of relativity which is much simpler and much less mystifying than Einstein's notoriously abstruse 'analog' version, with its well-known conflict with quantum theory concerning the ways in which bodies interact at great distances.

analysis. The fact that the mathematical consequences of the two theories are, as commentators have had to concede, mathematically equivalent (or, as someone has described it, 'isomorphic') has led, in some cases, to the Pope theory being treated as a merely 'idiosyncratic' version of Einstein's more orthodox and more familiar one, differing, as someone has said, only in the matter of 'metaphysics and/or language' and therefore trivial and surplus to physics requirements. However, that is to avert recognition of – if not deliberately to turn one's back on – the extreme difference in the conceptual implications of the two theories. Indeed, it is scarcely too much to say that the implications of the Pope theory seem to involve an element of danger. For instance, in the early part of the 18th century the gain in scientific objectivity that the Copernican revolution had achieved by removing man from his fancied 'central' role in the system of things was almost completely reversed. This was when the Irish philosopher, Bishop George Berkeley, conceived the notion that 'to be is to be perceived'. The upheaval in thinking that this presented – one might even say threatened – was to remove reality from the world of matter and relocate it in the mind of the observer. The philosophical consequences of this were, not surprisingly, deemed disastrous. This was, of course, because it seemed to imply that everything is just a dream. As the Greek philosopher Protagoras had put it in an earlier version of Berkeley's dictum, 'Such as appears to me is for me, and such as appears to you is for you.' This is an extreme form of relativism which entails, ultimately, that only oneself exists and that all other persons and things are no more than figments of one's own imagination (the view philosophers call 'solipsism').

Unfortunately, to many present-day physicists, 'philosophy' is synonymous with this theory of Berkeley's. Very few are aware that modern analytic philosophy stems from an outright rejection of

Berkeley's idealism. For instance, in the philosophical journal Mind, 1903, the Cambridge commonsense philosopher, G.E Moore, wrote a short but powerful essay called the 'Refutation of Idealism' which may fairly claim to have destroyed that form of Idealism for ever. What Moore did was to show that there is no warrant whatsoever for assuming that 'to be is to be perceived'. It obviously is not true in the way that 'A bachelor is an unmarried man' is true, nor is it true in the way that 'The earth orbits the sun' is true. On this analysis, finding no good reason to accept Berkeley's dictum, Moore rightly rejected it, from which time on, in modern philosophy, knowledge of the world reverts to whatever is decided by common consensus, as expressed in evolving common language – not excluding, of course, the language of a properly articulated and authentic science. Moore's commonsense paraphrase of Berkeley's 'To be is to be perceived' would therefore be 'To be is to be whatever, in the end, evolving and well-informed commonsense ultimately decides'.

Unfortunately, among those who are unaware of these post-Berkeleyan developments in philosophy – which includes, it seems, most modern physicists – the implications of Pope's thesis have been summarily rejected as being equivalent to those of Berkeley's dangerous idealism. For instance, as the eminent physicist Jean Paul Vigier typically expressed it, in reaction to Pope's theory: 'This exactly resembles the theory of Bishop Berkeley'. These things, he said, 'have to be settled by the experimental physicists, and no philosophical theory drawn from some philosophical heaven is going to tell us any hard facts' on the subject. (The subject was 'The Present Status of the Quantum Theory of Light', at a symposium in honour of Vigier. York University, Toronto, 1995.)

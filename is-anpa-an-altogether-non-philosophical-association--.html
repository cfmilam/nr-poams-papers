Is ANPA an Altogether Non-Philosophical Association - Spring 1997

IS ANPA an ALTOGETHER NON-PHILOSOPHICAL ASSOCIATION?

Viv Pope

Questions have been raised among ANPA members as to the suitability of the word 'Alternative' in the title of ANPA, the Alternative Natural Philosophy Association'. Some have felt that in view of changing fashions in the usage of words like 'gay' and so on, the word 'Alternative' has gathered some inappropriate, 'hippy-type' connotations. However, for different reasons, one might also object to the use of 'Natural' and 'Philosophy', in that same title since, strictly speaking, modern philosophers might well doubt that there is anything either natural or philosophical – far less coordinately so – in the ANPA approach to physical science.

That ANPA is an 'Association' cannot, at least, be doubted. However, having been charged with the task of 'digging-out and articulating the ANPA philosophy' (see Newsletter No. 15, p.6) one's findings to date are that in ANPA there simply is no common philosophy that one can chuck a stick at.

What one finds is that such arguments as are conducted, on the 'philosophical' agenda are, for the most part, hardly ever read, far less studied – and far less, again, retained. Instead, the 'numbers game' seems to be taken by most members as self-justifying and to be no more in need of a secure philosophical underpinning than a game of bingo. To say the very least, the search for a secure philosophical basis for the Association is not regarded, by the majority of ANPA members, as being anything like a priority.

Indeed, it has to be said that the physicists and mathematicians of the Association, who make up the majority of the membership seem to have almost no clue as to what philosophy is, let alone what it has to do with ANPA. Here, for example, are one or two responses at one of my own philosophical talks:

Not being a philosopher, I'd like to know, are there philosophical apparatuses around that

are sitting on the shelf waiting to be used? Are we talking about a little bit of Heraclitus here, a bit of Wittgenstein there, and so on?

No, it was explained, that's eclecticism. What one is talking about is a whole logically integrated package.

Someone else says:

There are many philosophies, and many of them are contradictory, and I'm just wondering which philosophy you favour. It's all a matter of opinion.

This sentiment was supported by the following remark:

It appears that you hold a view of philosophy which is either true or false!

Now one would have thought it unnecessary to explain to an association with the word 'Philosophy' in its title, that the very essence of Philosophy, as exemplified by its three most famous exponents, Socrates, Plato and Aristotle, was to winnow-out truth from the usual mish-mash of circumstantial ideas and opinions. The notion implied by these members that there is no such thing as truth, only opinion, is not philosophy but sophistry, which is and always was anathema to true philosophy. It was, in fact, the view promulgated by Socrates' intellectual arch enemy, Protagoras. What neither Protagoras nor any other sophist could grasp was that to claim it as true that nothing is really true is a complete logical contradiction. Far less could those sophists understand that this contradiction entails that there has to be truth for any talk of falsity to make sense. This entails, in turn, that the search for natural truth has to be empirical; That empirical search for truth by the methodical elimination of error is what Natural Philosophy is, and always was, all about.

The very fact, then, that we engage in scientific enquiry signifies an implicit accept-

ance that our existing knowledge of nature is wanting – that it is, to some extent untrue. This signifies, in turn, that we accept that there is truth to be discovered, otherwise no undertaking could be more pointless than that of scientific enquiry and research. Unfortunately, so steeped has our society become in the old sophistic way of Protagoras that, now, any suggestion that science is a search for truth seems a sort of educational blasphemy – which is strange, because it makes a complete nonsense of prestigious science. So, if it were really the view of the majority of ANPA members that there is no such thing as truth, then the purpose of the ANPA meetings would seem pointless, to say the least.

Whatever else may be going on at ANPA, then, its philosophical underpinnings remain as inarticulate as one might expect to find in, say, a motor repair workshop. One might say, therefore, that with the prevailing apathy of members towards in-depth philosophical discussion, to satisfy any intellectual 'Trades Descriptions Act', the word 'Philosophical' ought, rightly, to be purged from the title of the Association.

Something else which evidently has not impinged on ANPA members is that there has been a complete change of direction in modern

philosophy. The implications of this departure are as vital to physics as to philosophy but seem, as yet, not to have touched the former. What we refer to here is the demise, in philosophy – albeit not yet in physics – of Cartesian dualism and its logical entourage of conceptual confusions. In previous talks it was explained how the philosopher Descartes had arbitrarily split natural philosophy into two altogether unnatural, separate and discrete sciences. One of these divisions became Physics, the science of matter, and the other became Psychology, the science of mind. In those same talks it was also explained how and why this dichotomy which had presumed to sever the material world completely from our observational knowledge of it had been retracted. This was due to the realisation, by modern philosophers, that this dichotomy between 'mind' and 'matter' is not a characteristic of nature, as Descartes assumed, but is simply an edict applied to the our use of language, which made it somehow forbidden – or at least embarrassing – to talk about the reality of matter and of our observations of it in the same breath (a dichotomy which has been confounded by the discovery of relativity). However, from comments received in these talks, it was plain that this demise of the Cartesian dichotomy between the science of mind and the

science of matter remains unappreciated. For instance, more than one response made it plain that the 'modern philosophical rejection of Cartesianism' had been interpreted as a rejection of Cartesian coordinates!

What, then, one might ask, is a 'Natural Philosophy' Association with no awareness whatsoever of what is happening in modern Philosophy? I think I might be supported by a certain ex-ANPA member in saying that in the way ANPA is presently going, the only feasible way of retaining the word 'Philosophy' in its title would be to call it something like 'the 'Association of Non-Philosophical Arithmeticians' or the 'Absolutely Non Philosophical Association' – or even the 'Avoidance of Natural Philosophy Association'. One thing is sure, for anyone imbued with the true spirit of

philosophy, an organisation dedicated to achieving progress in philosophical understanding could never be regarded as an 'All Niceness and Politeness Association'.

I think, then, that a suitable way – perhaps the only legitimate way – of retaining the phrase 'Natural Philosophy' in the ANPA title might be to call it the 'Advancement of Natural Philosophy Association'. For that however, the present pure preoccupation with numbers and systems of numbers would have to be interlaced with some serious discussion of philosophy. And that would have to be modern (i.e., post Cartesian, post Logical Positivist) philosophy, the philosophy of overall systematic conceptual analysis, not just a smattering of 'opinion', with 'a little bit of Heraclitus here and a bit of Wittgenstein there ...'

What Price 'Electrodynamics'?

Some Blue Anchor 'Combinatorics' (See p. 21)

Starting with the usual relativistic time-formula:

$$t = t_0 / \sqrt{[1 - (v^2/c^2)]}$$

and rearranging, we have

$$v^2 = c^2[1 - (t_0^2/t^2)]$$

Let $t_0$ be the period of a ground-level electron-orbit and let $N$ be some whole number of these periods. With $t$ being integer multiples $n$ of $N t_0$ this gives

$$v^2 = c^2[1 - (N^2 t_0^2/n^2 N^2 t_0^2)].$$

Rearranging again:

$$N^2 v^2 = N^2 c^2[1 - (1/n^2)].$$

In terms of the $ns$, the relativistic $v$ is $1/n$ times the classical velocity $v_s$. Dimensionally, $v_s^2$ is $e_x/m_x$, where $e_x$ and $m_x$ are the classical energy and mass, respectively. Substituting gives

$$N^2 e_x/n^2 = N^2 m_x c^2[1 - (1/n^2)].$$

The term on the left of this equation is an unknown quantity with the dimensions of energy and can therefore be replaced by $e$, whence we have

$$e = N^2 m_x c^2[1 - (1/n^2)].$$

Dividing this equation by $h$ expresses it in terms of frequency. With $n_1$ and $n_2$ as fixed and running terms, respectively, we have the following series of intermediate frequencies:

$$f = N^2 f_{\text{lim}} [(1/n_1^2) - (1/n_2^2)].$$

Compare:

$$f = N^2 c R [(1/n_1^2) - (1/n_2^2)]$$

which is the textbook formula for the frequencies of simple spectra of hydrogen-like atoms of atomic number $N$. (See, e.g., 'Quantum Mechanics', Enc. Brit. 1961, vol 18, p. 816d.).

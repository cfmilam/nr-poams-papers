(The New) Quantum Touching — A Cinematic Model of Instantaneous Action-at-a-Distance

N. Vivian Pope — April, 2003

Abstract

Quantum Touching, otherwise known as Quantum Immediacy, is the concept that interacting atoms are virtually in contact regardless of how far apart they appear to be. This follows from the Special Theory of Relativity, in which the proper distance and time of light-interactions are zero, as G. N. Lewis demonstrated in 1926. In POAMS (the Pope-Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis) this leads to a Cinematic Model in which the elements of all physical interaction are, individually, instantaneous, their time-sequences being analogous to those in cinematography.

Introduction

The concept of 'Quantum Touching' was derived originally from a paper by the physical chemist, G. N. Lewis, published in 1926, in which Lewis showed that in Einstein's Special Theory of Relativity, the so-called 'proper time' and 'proper distance' of a light interaction between a source atom and a detector atom are identically zero, regardless of their distance apart in the observer's frame of reference. This means that in their own intrinsic time and space, the two atoms are at the same place at the same instant — they are, in effect, touching.

This result, startling as it may seem, is a straightforward consequence of the Lorentz transformations at the limit $v = c$. At this limit, both the time-interval and the length along the direction of propagation contract to zero. The 'photon', therefore, far from being a particle travelling through space and time, is an event common to both the source and the detector simultaneously.

1. Action-at-a-Distance

The concept of unmediated instantaneous action-at-a-distance has a long and controversial history in physics. Newton himself was uncomfortable with the idea that his 'gravitational force' could act across empty space with no intervening medium. 'That gravity should be innate, inherent, and essential to matter,' he wrote, 'so that one body may act upon another at a distance through a vacuum, without the mediation of anything else, by and through which their action and force may be conveyed from one to another, is to me so great an absurdity, that I believe no man who has in philosophical matters a competent faculty of thinking can ever fall into it.'

Yet despite Newton's own misgivings, his successors went on to introduce more and more in vacuo forces — electrostatic, magnetic, electromagnetic — each requiring its own theoretical medium or 'field' for its conveyance. The proliferation of these theoretical fields led eventually to the electromagnetic theory of Faraday and Maxwell, in which 'light' itself was conceived as a field-disturbance propagating through the vacuum at the speed $c$.

2. The Cinematic Model

In the Cinematic Model proposed by POAMS, the elements of all physical interaction are quantum events which, in their own proper time, are instantaneous. They are like the individual 'stills' in a cinematographic film — each one a complete, frozen snapshot involving no motion and no time. The appearance of motion and time-evolution arises from the sequences of these instantaneous frames, just as the appearance of motion in a movie arises from the rapid succession of still photographs.

The constant $c$, in this model, is not the 'speed' at which something travels between the frames. It is the rate at which the frames succeed one another in the observer's time — the 'projection rate' of the cosmic movie, as it were. This is a dimensional conversion constant, relating metres of spatial extension in each 'still' to seconds of temporal succession between stills.

3. Special Relativity: The Cinematic Curriculum

In the Cinematic Model, the Pythagorean space-time formula:

$$t_R = \sqrt{t^2 + (s/c)^2}$$

expresses the composite of two orthogonal time-measures: the proper-time $t$ of the individual quantum 'stills' and the distance-time $s/c$ of the spatial extension within each still. For light ($t = 0$), this gives $t_R = s/c$, the familiar time conventionally attributed to 'light travelling a distance $s$'. But in the Cinematic Model, nothing has travelled. The distance $s$ is simply the spatial extent of the instantaneous quantum event, and $s/c$ is the rate at which such events succeed one another in the observer's time.

This model reconciles the apparently contradictory requirements of Relativity (time-delay) and Quantum Theory (instantaneity) by showing them to be complementary aspects of the same composite process — just as the stillness of individual movie frames and the apparent motion of the movie are complementary aspects of the same cinematographic process.

4. Implications

The Cinematic Model, as developed in POAMS, has far-reaching implications for the interpretation of quantum phenomena. The notorious 'wave-particle duality', the 'collapse of the wave function', the 'measurement problem' and other puzzles of standard quantum mechanics all take on a different character when viewed cinematically. In particular, the non-locality confirmed by the Aspect experiments and required by Bell's theorem becomes entirely natural — it is simply the instantaneity of the individual quantum 'stills', which is the proper-time aspect of all interactions.

The Cinematic Model also provides a natural basis for understanding quantum entanglement, the EPR correlations and other phenomena that seem paradoxical in the framework of Einstein's 'locality' assumption. When $c$ is understood not as a speed-limit on physical interaction but as the projection-rate of quantum sequences, there is no barrier to instantaneous correlation at any distance. ■

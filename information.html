Information

INFORMATION

By events, in the following context, we mean any sorts of happenings or occurrences of finite duration which succeed one another in time. An event could be, for example, the turn of a playing-card, a village fête or a particle collision.

In classical physics these events were assumed to be determinate. That is to say, every event was thought to be caused or brought into being by some immediately preceding event and was the cause, in turn, of the event immediately succeeding it. Moreover, all different events-sequences were supposed to take place in parallel (simultaneously), so that they were all the same time-length. In that case, 'now' was the same for everything everywhere in three infinitely extended Euclidean dimensions.

Modern physics, however, reveals that this classical way of thinking was wrong on two counts. Relativity showed that these events-sequences which define the measure called time are not universally in parallel and therefore not all the same length - that there is no universally extended three-dimensional 'now' and therefore no universally advancing time-front - and Quantum Physics showed that none of these events-sequences is determinate - that at the ultimate microphysical (quantum) level of analysis there is no determinate causal connection whatever between one event and another.

There are two logical consequences of this. One is that from an event such as the present position of an object nothing can be known for certain about its next instantaneous position. The best that can be known is the probability of that next position, which is a matter of statistics - technically called a probability calculus. So nothing in modern physics is absolutely predictable, by maths, or by any means whatsoever - not even in principle. All predictability is stochastic, which means that it deals with probabilities, not certainties.

Now in ordinary language 'probability' seems vague. In science, however, there is nothing vague about probability. There are definite mathematical laws of probability, a measure which is calculated on a scale from nought to one. The connection between probability and information

A newspaper which reported only things that everyone already knew would obviously convey no information. That is, its capacity to inform would be nil. Similarly, a newspaper which forecast things which are entirely probable – like that the sun will rise tomorrow or that in the population as a whole there will be some births and deaths conveys little or no information. On the other hand, events that are improbable but which nevertheless occur make news. Imagine, for instance, the furore that would be created by a newspaper reporting that John Major and Margaret Thatcher are the same person in different disguises!

This provides the clue as to the inverse relation between probability and information – or 'news'. Reporting events which are improbable is news. But we have seen that probability and improbability are measured on a scale of nought to one. In that case, information, which is directly related to improbability, is measured on an opposite scale of one to nought. That is to say, to report events of probability one would be merely to report what is already common knowledge, whilst to report events of probability nought would be tantamount to reporting miracles – which would be news indeed!

It follows, then, that since probability is a measure, then so is information. But the question of probability and improbability (non-information and information, respectively) is also related to the question of order and disorder. A set of events whose occurrences are entirely random or chaotic is not likely to produce ordered informational sequences. The classically quoted example of this is the unlikelihood of monkeys randomly strumming on typewriters producing, by sheer chance, a Shakespeare sonnet. The probability of such a fortuitous occurrence, statisticians would say, approaches nought. By the same token, if such a thing did happen then it would signify, to that same extent, the sheer improbability (albeit not the certainty) that the monkeys' typewriter-strumming was random, that among them, somewhere, was a simian 'Shakespeare'.

Now order and disorder, in science, are not subjective evaluations. Like probability and improbability they are measures related to the measure energy. Moreover there is a physical law governing order and disorder - the same law, in fact, that governs distributions of energy. This is the law of entropy, which, in its simplest terms, is the tautology that in any closed system of purely random events randomness must, overall, prevail. Take, for instance, the collisions of steam-molecules with the walls of a vessel. The possibility that when the steam enters at high pressure it will remain at one end of the vessel is, obviously, extremely unlikely, for that would require an ordering, or regimenting, of the motions of the molecules towards that end, and neither in the nature of the cylinder nor the steam-molecules is there any such ordering agency present. So those molecules distribute their energies in the most probable - or least improbable - way, which is an even, or Gaussian, statistical distribution. The practical evidence that this is what occurs is the ordinary cooling of a hot body to match its cold surroundings.

Of course, not all bodies cool in this way. An Esquimaux caught in a blizzard may not cool like a kettle of boiling water but may take steps to maintain his body-temperature with those skills so typical of his race. In such cases we are witnessing the sort of behaviour which would be extremely unlikely for things like collections of steam-molecules. Such orderings or organisings of behaviour define what we call organic processes. These signify, by the law of entropy, that they are not random processes.

The same goes for those organised distributions of events we call informational. A non-informational, or most probable, sequence of events is one which meets the essential condition of the law of entropy, which is that the events under consideration should be random. The background hiss of space which is recorded by radio-telescopes, for instance, is the sort of sound which typifies this randomness or lack of order in what we call noise. Conversely, if, out of that noise-background, an ordered - that is to say, an intrinsically improbable - sequence of radio-events were to emerge, then we would be compelled to assume that some non-random, or organic, agency was responsible for it. We would recognise it as a signal, a piece of information, and the fact that we may not be able to decipher its meaning would not matter. Its

All these concepts, then, of probability and improbability, order and disorder, information and noise, etc., are logically interrelated. An organic structure, like a DNA molecule for example, is informational in its constitution and behaviour, both of which have a probability approaching zero in any system which is supposedly random. Sometimes we hear about the 'universal rule of entropy', or the entropic 'heat death of the universe', in which all order and improbability in the distributions of energy-systems evens out so as to make everything the same temperature. In that event, no engine nor organism could exist since there would be no differentiated source nor sink for their energy. Everything would 'grind to a halt' and its parts would disperse into the general background. It is by feeding, as it were, on that general dissolution that organisms, in the classical way of thinking, were supposed to evolve.

The trouble with that way of thinking, however, is that since the relativising of physics it has become impossible to define what 'universe' and 'universal' mean in that connection. So talk about the 'universal rule of entropy', the 'heat death of the universe', 'universal randomness' and so on no longer makes sense. In that case, there is no logical contradiction in accepting what is known about the world in commonsense and science, which is that insofar as it can be comprehended – that is, conceived as a whole – it is a plethora of energetic sources and sinks in perpetual sporadic interaction. And since these interaction-events can only either occur or not occur – they cannot, say half-occur or quarter-occur, or whatever – they have to be quantised, in which case, a quantum-occurrence is digital, dealing in binary digits, like a computer.

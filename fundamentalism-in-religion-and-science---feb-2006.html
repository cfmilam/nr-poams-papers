Fundamentalism in Religion and Science - Feb. 2006

N. Vivian Pope — 2006

Fundamentalism is defined as, in general, a ‘strict adherence to the fundamental principles of any set of beliefs’. In Christianity, it is ‘the belief that every word of the Bible is divinely inspired and therefore true’; and in Islam it is ‘a movement favouring strict observance of the teachings of the Koran and Islamic law’ [1]. Some scientists, such as, e.g. Richard Dawkins [2], see these fundamentalist beliefsystems of religion as based on pure faith as opposed to what scientists see, in contrast, as ‘absolute fact’. This dichotomy between ‘faith’ and ‘fact’, hence between Religion and Science, is spurious. Every so-called ‘fact’ in science is, inevitably, as much an interpretation of sensory/instrumental experience as ‘truths’ of the written word are in religion. Besides, the dependence of science on ‘the written word’ may be no less implicit than that of religious people on religious texts. In short, both in scientific and religious fundamentalism we find the same sort of definitive adherence to ‘sets of beliefs’ in which what was written by certain forerunners are taken as divinely inspired and therefore true. In the world as we know it, it can scarcely be doubted that all events and processes have beginnings and ends. For the fundamentalists, both of religion and science, there is just one universal beginning and, for some just one universal end, all that takes place in between being measured in a single, overall cosmic time. For the fundamentalists of science, the universal beginning is either the explosion of some kind of ‘primeval atom’ (the ‘Big Bang’) or else some fortuitous coincidence among randomly moving atoms (‘emergent evolution’). For the fundamentalists of religion, on the other hand, the universal beginning is an act of creation on the part of a deity (‘God’ in Christianity and ‘Allah’ in Islam). This distinction is, of course, not exclusive, since the religious ‘act of creation’ may be as much the belief in a deity as a belief in the ‘Big Bang’, religion and science being indistinguishable on that particular point.

Notes

[1] Collins English Dictionary.

[2] Richard Dawkins.

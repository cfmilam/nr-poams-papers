Why Phi?

We often hear people talk about the 'march of progress'. Progress, as we see it, however, being always from the known into the unknown, is never guaranteed to be a measured left-right-left-right forward march. Often it has to be a two-steps-forward-one-step-back affair (sometimes even a one-step-forward and a hundred steps retreat out of some unforeseen dead-end).

This kind of progress is never more characteristic, we think, than of science and philosophy, where perhaps centuries of energetic and optimistic trailblazing may end up in an intellectual impasse. We think that is what has now happened in science since it parted company with philosophy -philosophy having dozed off somewhere back along the trail.

Where science has failed, we think, is in the business of providing understanding, not only of the large bulk of things that concern mankind outside physics, but even of physics itself. Man has never been more perplexed about his physical existence than he is at present; and in this respect the conventional policy of vigorous specialisation on the part of scientists, at a time of relative dormancy on the part of philosophers, seems to have been -to say the least- abortive.

The aim of phi, then, is to try to re-unite science and philosophy in the search for understanding. As such it is a relativistic philosophy, which is as opposed to the wan subjectivism of so much of past philosophy as it is to the dogmatic absolutism of classical science. As such, also, it is directly in line with those developments upon classical science which were instituted by Albert Einstein, the philosophical significance of which, although crucial, seems nevertheless not yet to have percolated through to our professional philosophers.

The policy of phi, therefore, may be said to be that of an enlightened, or emancipated, physicalism. This implies a far more methodical or scientific attitude towards philosophy and a far more catholic approach to science than has been customary in the past. Also, in its emphasis on practicality in philosophy, phi is essentially an anti-sophistry viewspaper, stressing the need always for the prevalence of mind over 'natter'.

NORMAL REALISM

There is no philosophy worth the candle that does not, either explicitly or implicitly, claim to be the ultimately realistic view of existence. Like all the other contenders for the title of realism, therefore, the phi philosophy has to be classified in such a way as to compare it with, and distinguish it from, its competitors. Normal Realism is a development upon -and a critique of- the modern philosophy of Naive Realism, which is the name given, generally, to the views of people like G. E. Moore, Gilbert Ryle and J. L. Austin. But it also contains elements of pragmatic philosophy of the kind usually associated with A. J. Ayer and Karl Popper. Normal Realism, in other words, accepts (for its own stated reasons) Moore's refutation of that particular brand of idealism which was called 'English Empiricism'. In the style of J. L. Austin it rejects the elementistic 'sense-datum' view of Ayer (in his Logical Positivist era). In the style of Ayer and Popper it rejects the naivist a priori-ism of Austin and Ryle. And in the manner of Ryle and Austin it rejects the transcendental dualism of rationalists like Plato and Descartes.

It must not be supposed, however, that these various philosophical elements have been just thrown together, ad lib, just for the sake of scholarship or mere novelty. The ideas form a definite synthesis, or system of philosophy, which we believe to be the latest and most logical development out of the knowledge of the world that is now available in science.

Professor Ryle still thinks that we should drop the 'partisan' approach to philosophy suggested by our title, Normal Realism. However, we think that our aim should be to make our intentions as clear as possible. In a philosophical democracy, like a political democracy, you must, after all, signify what you are for and what you are against if you offer yourself for public election -and what philosopher worth his salt is not offering his convictions for public election?

Lifting The Optical 'Iron Curtain'

One of our readers reports that he found much interest in those articles in the previous issues of phi which dealt with the so-called 'speed of light'. He concluded, however, that whatever may be the philosophical justification for rejecting the 'velocity of light' terminology (namely, that it radically divides the knower from the known), and despite all the other logical reasons given for disposing of this concept, the fact remains that light somehow gets from A to B, and this fact must have some simple explanation.

This seems to have been the view of most readers of the articles in question. Some scientific gentlemen have said that to suggest that we dispense with the 'velocity of light' concept was 'quite absurd'. But since it is not to be expected that so radical a suggestion would be accepted without considerable clarificatory discussion, we have taken this reader's question out of the 'Dialogue' section of phi and have formulated our answer as a reply to the more general criticism that was implied by other readers' reactions.

The question to be answered, then, is this: what simple answer would you give to the question of how a light signal gets from A to B, when A and B are points separated in space (in vacuo)? The simple answer, according to present convention, is that it gets there by 'travelling across space'. A physicist, when pressed for further explanation, will say that any electrical disturbance (e.g., the mechanical oscillation of an electrically charged body) gives rise, in accordance with certain well-known laws of induction, to a surrounding magnetic field-effect. This magnetic disturbance then gives rise, by similar laws, to a contiguous electrical field-effect which gives rise, in turn, to another magnetic effect; and so on, ad infinitum.

In this way, any kinetic energy imparted to a charged body will radiate itself away in a series of ever-expanding spheres (waves) which are then received in diminishing strength at points extending radially from the source towards infinity. The ratio of units of electrical energy to units of magnetic energy in the process (that is, the number of electrostatic units of charge per electromagnetic unit) then gives the velocity of propagation of the electromagnetic disturbance, which is conceived somewhat after the fashion of waves in a medium such as air or water. This is the present Physical picture of how light-signals (or any other electromagnetic signals) get from A to B. Physicists will tell you that this is very easy to understand. Philosophers will tell you that it is virtually impossible to understand. Let us see why.

In the first place, the source of this 'electromagnetic propagation' is conceived to be an 'electric displacement current'. This consists of a movement of electrically charged particles relative to some chosen point of reference. Given the movement of a charged particle at the source of the signal we therefore have, by definition, an electric displacement-current, which, so far, is all well and good. But we are considering the propagation of displacement-currents in vacuo. All subsequent disturbances, electrically and magnetically induced, have therefore to be conceived as taking place in a void, that is to say, without charged particles, or, indeed, any other kind of entity that might be considered. In other words we are called upon to conceive of 'displacement-currents' as currents of, quite literally, nothing. We thus have, in effect, the concept of 'waves in a medium' without a medium, which makes no sense outside the realms of mathematical abstraction.

In the second place, if somehow we allow the conundrum of waves of nothing travelling in nothing (in vacuo), the velocity of these waves, for the concept to make any sense, has to be conceived as referred to something. One cannot sensibly think of a constant velocity relative to nothing. Yet that is precisely what one is asked to think in respect of light. Light, it is said, has a constant velocity in vacuo. How can that be? Do they mean, perhaps, that light has a constant velocity of propagation relative to the source? No, because measurements of the speed of light received from sources such as the rotating binary star-systems reveal that although the sources have different motions relative to the recipient of the light, the speed of the light as received is constant; so science concludes that the speed of light is source-independent. Whatever meaning may be given to the 'constant velocity of light', therefore, it is assuredly not that it is a constant of emission relative to the source.

Of what, then, is the so-called 'speed of light' a constant? Of space? Allowing, once more, that it could somehow be meaningful to speak in this peculiar way of a void, or vacuum, as a 'something', then if it makes sense to talk of light travelling in this nothing-something, and if light travels in this at the speed c, then, an observer travelling at speed v in a direction away from the light-source will receive the ray at an effective speed of c-v, just as an observer travelling at the same speed in the opposite direction will receive the ray at a speed of c+v. But, again, as the physicist will be the first to inform you, experiment confirms the total absence of such effects and therefore the total absence of anything conforming to the notion of 'space' as an absolute common reference for light velocity.

Logically speaking, these results constitute what logicians call the reductio ad absurdum of the 'light-velocity' hypothesis; for all the facts attest that the so-called 'velocity' of light is constant relative to nothing but the observer, or, rather, to all observers, irrespective of their motions relative to one another. But what kind of 'velocity' is that? How can such a concept of velocity square with the view of the objective transmission of light across space? As a characteristic of space it would have to be thought of as setting out and then proceeding on its way with as many different velocities as there will eventually be recipients of the signal. Manifestly, then, it cannot be the simple, objective, mechanistic process of emission and propagation that is now the standard explanation in Physics. The appearance of 'simplicity' in this explanation is entirely spurious, for it leads in the end to complications of an altogether insuperable kind.

The conventionally 'simple answer', then, is far from simple. It is the tip of a massive metaphysical iceberg, the vast and complicated bulk of which constitutes the underlying threat to present philosophical understanding. But so far as phi is concerned, what is of particular interest about this issue concerning light is that it appears, on analysis, to be a paradigm case of the need for conceptual revision: the altogether radical kind of philosophical revision which is advocated by Normal Realism as being sometimes an essential requirement of continued progress in understanding.

Well, what is it about this 'light' issue that is so important to philosophy? The answer to that is quite simple. It is, of course, that light, conceived as a kind of travelling 'representative' of physical events in space, radically separates the knower from the known object (light source). This makes a metaphysic of external reality in which matter is something always transcending actual perception, something between which and the observer there always interposes a kind of 'veil of perception', or 'iron curtain' of uncertainty.

Those who use the 'velocity of light' terminology thereby signify their, invariably unconscious and uncritical, acceptance of a way of thinking about reality which is viciously metaphysical. Let us see why this is so.

The history of the 'velocity of light' concept begins in philosophical speculations about the relationship between the observer and an object, in what is called 'knowledge of an object'. What is it, asked the fathers of present philosophy and science, that distinguishes between what we call 'knowledge' in such cases and what we call 'belief'? To Plato the perceived object, as a thing of ordinary sensation, was an illusion, whereas the real object was something known only to reason. As such it consisted of an overlapping set of Ideal or Universal qualities, manifest not to the senses but to pure intellect. This, for Plato, is what constituted the real character of a sensed object. So far as vision was concerned, Plato regarded this as something subscribed by (that is, going forth from the eye of) the perceiver to meet with the sun's illumination of the object, giving such knowledge of the object as may obtain in ordinary vision. The real object is thus, in Plato's view, something which, in one way or another, always lies behind and beyond actual sense-perception, being accessible, if at all, only to pure reason.

Allied to this 'transcendental' view of Plato was another view: the view which was the precursor of modern physics. This was the one developed by Lucippus and Democritus, in which external reality consisted of matter in the shape of tiny indivisible (infinitely hard) particles separated by a void. These particles (which they called 'atoms') were conceived as somehow casting off little images of themselves (simulacra); and constant streams of these travelling through the void, impinged upon the senses of the percipient, thus giving representational evidence (hopefully) of their sources. If this view seems to us the more plausible of the two it is only because that is the one on which the subsequent history of optical theory was based.

Well, not quite; for while this 'emission' theory of the Greek atomists was developing on the east of the Aegean, the other, Platonic, view of matter as a 'character-complex' of ideal qualities was passed on, with suitable modifications, to Plato's star pupil, Aristotle, the founder of Western Science. Aristotle was critical of the kind of transcendental metaphysic that was contained in both Plato and Democritus, since on both views man was radically severed from reality. The real object, argued Aristotle (typically of what subsequently became a tradition of empiricism in scientific philosophy) is the one that you actually perceive, not some underlying and inaccessible entity conjured up by the theorising intellect. In accordance with this view of Aristotle's, there was passed on among the successors of this peripatetic tradition, a view of light, not as a stream of atomic effluvia, or simulacra, but as the 'propagation of a species' (that is, of a complex of essential characteristics all related in a distinctive way). The object thus perceived was therefore the same, in its essentials, as the source-object: that is to say, it consisted of more or less the same overlapping of qualities or essences as characterised the source-object. It was therefore the same object in the sense that we speak of different people as having the same build, or of the same programme being shown on different television sets. This species, or character-complex conception of light-propagation, continued in the West as the standard scientific view, right down to the time of Descartes, after which time it took place alongside the Democritan 'emission' concept which was newly imported from the East in the rediscovery of the Greek (Epicurean) 'Atomic Philosophy' recorded in the works of the Roman poet-philosopher Lucretius.

These two theories, the Peripatetic and the Epicurean, each

having been given its respective measure of initial impetus, have since persisted side by side right down to modern times. In Descartes, for instance, we see the species concept combined with a kind of mechanistic view of light as pressures propagated in a fluid ether (the precursor of the wave-theory as it was subsequently developed by Huygens) and among the followers of Newton, on the other hand, we see the spread of a view of light as the emission of corpuscles, after the fashion of Democritus.

But, thinking of light as something travelling from the source to observer, whether by the 'emission of particles' or by the 'propagation of a species' (through waves in a medium), uniformly implies something happening at one point in space at one moment of time, and then happening in a secondary way along a whole series of other points in space at successively later moments in time. It is this concept of light as something 'travelling' so very easy to understand when we invoke the fiction of identifiable solid particles, on the one theory, or that of a 'medium of propagation' on the other, which is so perplexing when we are told that light consists neither of solid particles nor of any medium. There are physicists who will tell you that there is no difficulty whatsoever implied by this so long as you understand the mathematics involved. But don't believe a man who tells you that he understands, by means of symbols and incantations, mathematical or metaphysical, what it is manifestly impossible to understand; and no mathematical device whatsoever is capable of establishing the concept of waves of nothing travelling through nothing at a constant and characteristic velocity relative to nothing. There has to be something not only wrong but fundamentally wrong with this conventionally 'simple and straightforward' explanation of how light gets from A to B.

Well, what alternative explanation is there? How else could light get from one point to another in space than by travelling, either as corpuscles in a void or as waves in a medium? Perhaps there is some other, hitherto unknown and unimagined, method of travelling. But that is no more than an academic possibility so long as no other such method may be found. In the circumstances, therefore, we might as well consider the remaining possibility, namely, that light does not travel—at least, not in any ordinary sense of the word 'travel'. But then, if light does not travel—as our inquisitor insists—how does it get from A to B? Light does, in fact, manifest itself progressively at different points in space at different progressive moments in time. Does it not therefore conform by definition to the concept of something travelling? The answer to this question, of course, has to be yes. So light does travel! Well, so it may seem, but before we assert to this let us look once again, very carefully, at what we mean in this context by 'space' and 'time'. Perhaps these will furnish some clue as to the cause of all the confusion.

What, exactly, do we mean by 'space'? The answer to this, given in the context of science where all the confusion arises, is as revealing as it is simple: it is, fundamentally, what we measure by means of light. That is to say, conceiving light as 'travelling at the velocity c' we subtract from the time of actually seeing an event the length of time that it takes light to travel, which gives us, in theory, a mathematical continuum of instantaneous and simultaneous points which, according to scientific convention, we term 'space'. And what do we mean by 'time'? Well, time, in this same context, is the measure which separates the events as we move progressively (at the 'speed of light') in the direction away from the source. But isn't this separation already called 'space'? Yes it is; and it is also called 'time'. So, in terms of these conventions, what separates points in what is classically conceived as 'space' is not just space but space-time, a length that is just as appropriately expressed in seconds as in metres (in the constant ratio of units c = 3 x 10⁸ metres/second).

Now let us look again at this question of whether light manifests itself progressively at different points in space at different moments in time, which we have seen to be so essential to the concept of 'travelling'. Yes, light does manifest itself at different points in space—the light-path is, in fact the space along which the light is progressively manifest. Does it do so at progressively different moments of time? Yes, it does—at time—intervals which are defined in terms of the 'space-separation' and 'velocity' (the time is the space divided by the velocity). Does light, then, travel? No, it does not, because all travelling is in space with time. Light does not travel in space, it is space and it does not travel with time, it is time. It is, in fact, both space and time—or, more correctly space-time. What physicists call a light-ray is therefore not something travelling in space and time: it is that from which the very concepts of 'space' 'time'

and 'travel' are all abstracted. You can call miles per hour' a speed, so long as this implies a variable. You cannot, however, sensibly call c a speed because it is a constant -just like the constant that relates, say, inches and centimetres, or pounds and grammes. It is no more than a numerical ratio between arbitrary units: between metres and seconds (or electrostatic and electromagnetic units of charge) in the measure of space-time.

Light 'travels', moreover, only on the assumption that it makes sense to abstract from the time of actually seeing an event, a whole (continuous) series of other, prior, events taking place somehow or other in vacuo, and extending beyond and behind what is actually observed. Thus is perpetuated the metaphysic of Plato and Democritus which makes reality something always inaccessible to the knower. What is happening now, at this very moment on the sun? How can we ever know, since nothing travels faster than light and light will not get here till eight minutes have elapsed.

The whole thing is a philosophical conundrum. On a consistently empiricistic view of the facts there is no such thing as the 'velocity of light'. Readers of the phi article beginning on page 6 of Issue 4, will have seen how simply and easily the mathematical results which Einstein gave to modern physics can be obtained without referring to the 'velocity of light', and we need not repeat this demonstration in the present article. Suffice it to say that far from being a 'simple explanation' of physical events, far from being essential to an understanding of things like relativity theory, the 'velocity of light' is a redundant concept and serves, in the end, not to edify, but only to complicate and bewilder. No doubt, if the concept of 'light-velocity' were rejected it would lead to further revision in the direction of a new and far more systematic kind of natural philosophy. Suspecting this, those who are the deepest entrenched in the old, comfortable confusions may be expected to hang on to the now familiar concept of light-velocity, no matter what. But there are, inevitably, the more adventurous souls who might like to try out the new conceptual possibility that we have suggested and who would be prepared to give it a fair trial in the field of experimental science. If so, we hope they will keep us informed of their results.

STUDENTS' GLOSSARY

analytic: see 'synthesis'

a posteriori: see a priori'

a priori: an 'a priori' idea is one derived from reasoning alone, with no observational evidence in its support; opposed to an a posteriori idea which necessarily has observational evidence in its support.

de facto: in fact; as a matter of fact, as opposed to de jure -what ought to be the case. e.g., a de facto distinction is one which is in fact made (whether it is correct or not is another question).

dualism: see 'veil of perception'.

empiricism: the philosophy of experience; opposed to Rationalism (see Rationalism).

epistemology: the area of philosophy which deals with theories of knowledge and theories of belief, etc. Typical of its subject-matter are such questions as 'what is the difference between knowledge and belief?'. 'How can we be certain of anything?', etc.

idealism: the doctrine stemming from Berkeley, in the British tradition, that reality consists entirely of minds and their experience, or 'ideas' -more appropriately called 'idea-ism' (except that this is thought to be too awkward a term). A facet of English Empiricism (see below).

kinetic: pertaining to movement. A moving body is said to have 'kinetic energy', or energy of movement.

metaphysics: literally means 'beyond physics': the branch of philosophy dealing with general, or all-embracing, rational principles (see Rationalism)

Empiricistic philosophers may use the term 'metaphysical' as a term of disparagement (see 'veil of perception').

Naive Realism: the kind of philosophy which holds the real to be immanent in, rather than transcendental to, ordinary or commonsense perception; opposed to the view that our perceptions are no more than representations of something more fundamentally real.

ontology: the theory of being or existence.

paradigm case: a definitive example, that to which one can point, so to speak, or say 'if that is not a (so-and-so) then I don't know what is!'

peripatetic: pertaining to the philosophy of, or the philosophical tradition stemming from, Aristotle.

phenomenon: whatever can be observed -sometimes used (in Rationalist philosophy) to designate an appearance as opposed to the underlying reality, or noumenon pregmatism: the theory that the whole meaning of any conception is what expresses itself in practical consequences -that the truth is whatever works in practice. Pragmatism and science are usually allied.

Rationalism: the doctrine that reason is a source of knowledge in its own right independently of perception (contrast 'empiricism'). Acc. to Rationalists, truth can be discovered a priori (see above) -opposed to empiricism (see 'empiricism'). Historically, the three philosophers, Descartes, Spinoza and Leibnitz are the main-stream Rationalists in the Western tradition of philosophy. They were also metaphysicians (see 'metaphysics').

STUDENTS' GLOSSARY (Contd.)

scepticism: the doctrine of the rejection of absolute knowledge or truth.

sense-data: analysed 'elements of sensation' of which perception is supposed to be constituted. The concept of a 'sense-datum' is rejected by Naive Realists (q.v.) who regard perception as determined more by context than by the character of any assumed components.

sophistry: mere pretence at philosophical wisdom. The Sophists were held to scorn by Socrates and Plato for their professional belief that all truth is no more than a matter of personal opinion.

species: an appearance, or perceived relational system (phenomenon) whose character is determined by underlying objective reality rather than by the predispositions of the observer. In peripatetic philosophy (q.v.) perception was facilitated by the propagation of species. Perception of a species is perception of something which is a contextual whole, as contrasted with the receipt of punctiform and unrelated "sense-data" (q.v.)

synthesis: the putting together of items to make a related whole, as opposed to analysis (the breaking-down of a whole into constituent parts.

tautology: a statement the truth of which follows from the meanings of its constituent terms, and which therefore conveys no information.

transcendental: lying beyond or behind what is immediately manifest (see 'veil of perception').

veil of perception: a picturesque term for the 'barrier' which exists, in some philosophical doctrines, between our perceptions and their source-objects. On the 'perception' side of this supposed 'barrier', there are supposed to be only the 'representations' of what lies inexorably beyond, in some so-called 'physical' or 'metaphysical' realm. In terms of this 'veil of perception' view, there is an essential and radical duality between immediate and transcendental (q.v.) or 'metaphysical' (q.v.) entities.

The Logic of Experience

Logic, of course, has to do, by definition, with language. Now, there are philosophers who would claim that language is more or less autonomous —a law unto itself— and that the dividing up and classifying of experience proceeds in a more or less arbitrary way, being governed by nothing more than convention. Language, they would say, is something which determines, rather than something which is determined by, perception, and that there is no fundamental philosophical question of the reality or unreality of anything but only the question of which convention to adopt in asserting its existence.

Now we would agree that linguistic convention plays an important role in the establishing of what is real and unreal. We shall argue, however, that in the end the role of convention in this matter is nevertheless a subordinate one, and that what dominates our decisions about reality is neither convention nor invention, but the inherent relational integrity of phenomena considered as things in themselves.

Those who claim freedom for convention to divide and classify the world in whatever way it chooses seem to us to take far from sufficient account of the natural and inherent limitations which are imposed on our perceptions by the time-factor in experience. If our perceptions were available all at once, within a single, all-encompassing frame, like some surrealistic still-life painting, then one might well argue that its interpretation was a matter purely of an arbitrary choice of convention. But in actual experience our perceptions are never laid out for us in this artificially circumscribed and static way. They unfold themselves in a way which has scant regard for our expectations, conventional or unconventional, and in such a way as places on us the burden of having to make correct responses to things —responses which are often the result of personal insight rather than of convention.

This means, of course, that there is always in experience so much that has never, so far, been encountered, and which remains inaccessible to us —until it happens, of course, in the fulness of time. There is thus so much uncertainty which can never be ruled out —the fall of the dice, the luck of the draw, the unexpected twist of fate— that nothing is more typical of what we call 'experience' than a constant need to revise our ideas and conventions in the light of new knowledge brought by unforeseen events. A truly empirical assessment of perception, therefore, is that of perceptions, not as a closed and completed set waiting to be interpreted or systematised, but as strung out successively along the time-dimension in the form of an entirely open-ended and heuristic continuum called experience, which is never static and circumscribed and never the arbitrary selection of events that is required by the conventionalists. In such an open-ended system there is no question of providing 'once-and-for-all' logical or linguistic 'pigeonholes' in which all future perceptions will automatically come to roost. What characterises experience, more than anything else, is change and what characterises our experience of the development of understanding, in particular, is that what now fits neatly into one nice logical slot may later prove more appropriate to another. Some of the old slots may even become redundant, while in many cases new ones may have to be contrived to accommodate new information.

The world that confronts us in this way is extremely complex, and in its linguistic interpretation a natural premium always rests on that convention which best enables us to reduce the sheer complexity and unexpectedness of what is presented. Thus, not only do we have to identify (classify and categorise) objects and spatial relations between objects but we must do so always in such a way as to give direction towards the perception of future events. In other words, the main job of perception is to determine the causal characteristics of events. That convention is best in this respect which gives greatest guidance towards proper expectations, creating a sense of order and character in experience, whereas the worst is the one which creates greatest confusion —perhaps making an emergency, or even a disaster, of every passing moment.

Perception, then, taken empirically, is no static still-life painting, hung in the contemplative quiet of some academic 'picture-gallery', begging no more than whimsical interpretation by any idle passer-by. It is more like the view of a busy thoroughfare, by the driver of a trolley-bus, on whose interpretations of what he sees hangs the safety of himself and many of his fellow men. And while there may be all sorts of interesting variations on the conventions of art-interpretation, there are only just so many interesting variations on ways of driving a trolley-bus. Similarly, while there may be all sorts of ways of interpreting a given instance of perception there are only so many ways, concomitant with sanity, of interpreting the perceptual continuum called experience.

Existing conventions are, of course, always a good guide to future experience. This is because conventions represent logical classifications made on the basis of a very much larger common fund of experience than can be gained by any individual in the space of a lifetime. Nevertheless, no convention can ever be more than moderately effective against the exigencies of ongoing experience, because, of course, they are not clairvoyant, and their effectiveness is governed, as Hume has shown, by the limits on the extent to which the past can be taken as a reliable guide to the future. Indeed, it is possible that at some stage or other in the evolution of a language, a too deeply ingrained and conservative attitude towards existing conventions will militate against further necessary adaptation. On the other hand, convention might become so adaptable, so loose and ambiguous in its uses and significations as to fail to give proper guidance. In such cases the burden falls on the individual freethinker to break, if possible, with tradition in order to further his own—and, perhaps, society's best interests.

What is required, then, is some reliable and rational method of coping with change. In other words, what is needed is a means of seeking behind the more ephemeral, or illusory, appearances among phenomena, for things more permanent—more real or substantial. In this way, there, is gradually established an empirical ontology, or list of things that can be commonly referred to in language—preferably a language which will encourage rather than inhibit the further growth of experience and understanding.

Now Kant thought that there must be some a priori, or innate, precondition in the minds of men which leads them to establish a common language. We think that this must be so—but not exclusively among men. We think that such a common precondition exists among all creatures which are capable of sensible discrimination. Even a worm discriminates in its reactions, between some logical class of things A, which it signifies by avoidance, and a class B towards which it exhibits attraction. This common tendency to discriminate between things A and non-A, B and non-B, etc., etc., we take, in Normal Realism as the universal basis of reason, being as fundamental to the computer and philosopher as to the jellyfish.

However, the fact of perceptual discrimination between A and non-A can never guarantee that things labelled 'A' will thenceforward necessarily remain A; for as we see it, no actual a priori delineation of classes of experience is guaranteed to stand the test of time. The logical rule that 'A is A' is therefore no more than an essential formal precondition of meaning or understanding. As applied in experience the logical, or a priori, principle does not become a categorical affirmation of absolute empirical fact—far less a determinant of future experience—but is never more than a rule for formulating meaningful hypotheses about the same. The more developed a language becomes the less tentative and the more 'categorical', in general, become its forms of assertion But in the end, because of the inevitably open ended, heuristic, character of experience, nothing is ever absolutely, or logically, assured. 'If that is not a tree then I don't know what is!' is about as near as one can ever get to the absolute categorical statement: 'That is a tree.' In any event, in a statement of the form, 'That is A', it is never the form of the statement, as such, that makes A what it really is, but the regularity, in ongoing experience, of the presentation itself. The confidence with which we describe whatever is 'obvious' in experience should therefore not be imputed simply to logic. For we cannot justifiably suppose that something which is logically the case is really the case If I say 'That is a tree', then in all likelihood it is a tree—but then the possibility exists, however remotely, that it may not be a tree. Consider, for instance, that at one time it might quite as confidently have been said of the whale: 'That is a fish'—or 'If that is not a fish, then I don't know what is!'—whereas it was, in fact, not a fish but a mammal. Consider, also, the kind of statement which is usually regarded as 'analytically true', or 'true by definition', such as e.g., 'All unmarried men are bachelors.' By 'marriage', here, do you mean Christian marriage? If so, then if Neanderthal man was a man he was a bachelor, whether mated or not, which is absurd. The truth, therefore, even of this analytic statement, is very much dependent on the meaning of its subject-terms—in this case, on the meanings of 'man' and 'marriage'. Obviously, then, in view of the sheer arbitrari-ness of the classes contained in our empirical definitions of such things as trees, fish, men, etc., every such definition contains the possibility of having to be revised (its terms qualified and re-qualified) in accordance with growing experience.

It might seem, then, in view of this inexorable uncertainty of experience, that 'pure logic' is of somewhat limited use, for there is no possible a priori way of establishing a basic ontology—and, even supposing one to be established, there is no possible way of preserving it inviolate against the exigencies of future experience. So what ontological method can we employ? Some philosophers, following Hume, might say that the only consistent and reliable empirical method of establishing an ontology is by induction, that is, by sheer familiarity or habitual association. Others, following Kant, might say that it is all a matter of psychological predisposition (we won't argue whether innate or acquired). We would claim, in Normal Realism, however, that in spite of the discrepancies that exist between pure logic and experience a proper ontology proceeds by logical deduction, rather than by induction and predisposition, by a process which is explained as follows.

We begin, inevitably, by assuming some kind of a priori predisposition towards seeing things in certain ways. This may be the result, to some extent or other, of psychological or social factors, to personal prejudice or prior reason —or, indeed, to any predisposing factor that might be considered applicable. Let us refer to this more or less rationally-amorphous mixture of concepts, beliefs, attitudes, etc., as the empirical substrate. This conforms to what the social psychologist calls a 'culture' which, as Kluckholm and Kelly define it, is 'all those historically created designs for living, explicit and implicit, rational, irrational and non-rational, which exist at any time as potential guides for the behaviour of men'. What is then required for the purposes of a fully articulated philosophy is the systematic application of reason* to this substrate: and by reason we mean no more than that relationship between forms in language which makes some the logical (i.e., automatic or 'analytic') consequences of others.

Philosophy, then, so far as Normal Realism is concerned, is what it is to many modern philosophers —namely, a set of language-games. What distinguishes Normal Realism, however, is that its 'language-games' are always games of consequence. By that we mean that while we may never know quite what will, in fact, be the consequence of some present classification, situation or occurrence we know at least what it ought, logically, to be if we have read that experience correctly. And what it ought to be, moreover, is what it would, automatically and inevitably be in a world which really is what it seems to be. In such a world, 'A', being absolutely A, could never be non-A; and if all 'A's were 'B's then there would be no possible instance of an A which was a non-B. In such a world, everything would be perfectly ordered and determinate —a 'universal tautology' or 'logical plenum'— and the business of philosophy, like that of the sciences generally, would be that of pure description, for all speculation would be at an end.

But discussion of the characteristics of such a world, supposing it to exist, is no more than an academic matter; for, insofar as the world contains organisms which have a logical (i.e., linguistic) function, and insofar as logic can create classes of an artificial, or 'illusory', kind, then in addition to what would otherwise be this 'universal tautology' there is also a certain measure of arbitrariness or artificiality to give existence the vitality so beloved of human beings. So far as any logical (or creative) organism is concerned, therefore, there is no single and inevitable set of logical expectations: no universal 'tautology', or 'language-game', but many possible such 'games'. And between such fancied logical worlds and the world of actual ongoing experience there is the discrepancy that invariably interposes itself, bringing those dangers and inconveniences which stimulate the organism into giving its characteristic responses. The game of life, in general, thus seems to be to secure the best of all these possible worlds —that is, the one which strikes the best balance between perceptual discrimination and effective action in the maintenance of the best interests of the organism.

As we see it, then, the 'game of reason', which is the concern of the sciences in particular, is continually to adapt our logical systems (theories) to phenomena; and the aim of philosophy is to process these special systems in accordance with the rules of logic so as to remove the inevitable contradictions and redundancies, and thereby fit them all together into a simple, logically coherent and coordinate whole. Philosophy is thus, on this analysis, the science of the sciences, whose aim is to preserve that balance between particular ideas and responses to nature that we customarily refer to as understanding. Logically, then, if you reclassify 'space' and 'time' as the single entity space-time, as required in accordance with modern relativity theory, then the characteristically progressive quality of time is automatically transferred to space, in the ratio of units 186,000 miles to the second. In other words, an event that is conventionally described as occurring 186,000 miles away will not be observed until one second of time has elapsed -not because that is the 'travelling-time' of what is conventionally called 'light', but simply because space-time now has that same progressive, or evolutionary, character which was once mistakenly confined to time.

Given, then, that space-time has this universally progressive character, spatial events have all the causal character of temporal events without necessitating the notion of a travelling intermediary called 'light'. What causes Tuesday to follow Monday? Nothing, it just happens that way, and that kind of process we call 'time'. What causes a laser to have the effect it has on a distant object? Nothing, it just happens that way, and that kind of process we call 'space-time'.

All the other processes you describe are analysable in the same way into causal sequences whose temporal datum is not just time but space-time. All that is needed is to accept it as a fact of modern science that a constant ratio exists between units of space-measure and time-measure, purge your mind of all that is associated with 'the speed of light', and all the rest falls logically into place.

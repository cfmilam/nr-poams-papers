Topsy-Turvy Modern Physics

N. Vivian Pope — 2001

Epistemologically speaking, modern physics is standing on its head. What does this mean? It means that having, on the basis of ordinary experience, determined the laws that apply to mechanical phenomena, physicists have traditionally projected these laws into a theoretical underworld of micro-mechanisms which they regard as more basic and more real than the phenomena from which our ideas of these entities derive.

To set physics on its feet, proper precedence would have to be given to the phenomena over the laws. That means taking ordinary observational experience as providing the basic informational data on which all physics is founded. These data are not the theoretical 'underlying atomic mechanisms' of classical physics. They are the ultimately irreducible informational 'blips' of modern quantum physics, down to which the world as we know it ultimately analyses. This is similar to the way in which the action in a video scenario analyses down to informational patterns and sequences of the screen pixel-events which, in themselves, are completely motionless and distanceless.

In this way, the bits of information on which our perceptions of objects are based are not, as we are told, incidental cast-offs from those objects, which they just happen to emit in the form of light and which we may just happen to receive, belatedly and at second hand, if at all. In the 'flipover' view, this quantum information is the very essence of all that we perceive, in all the various categories of sensation and instrumentation that our traditions have divided and classified. In other words, in the epistemologically uprighted view of physics, those quantum data are not something which objects shoot out into the void in the form Faraday and Maxwell envisaged as 'waves' and Einstein thought of as wave-particles called 'photons'. Each single datum of sensation-cum-instrumentation is a completely consummate event, common to an atom in the object and an atom in the observer in the same instant, no matter how near or far apart those two might be. And without another atom to interact with, in that immediate and consummate way, one atom can do nothing, since there can be no such thing as a one-ended quantum jump.

Events involving these distance-separated objects in the same instance are called 'non-local' events. There can be none of these according to Einsteinian relativity, with its idiosyncratically conceived 'velocity limit $c$' on all physical interaction. This 'inverts' relativity, as it were, with respect to quantum theory, which requires non-local correlation and conservation of atomic interactions. The solution, however, is amazingly simple. Since it is not logically necessary to interpret $c$ as a 'velocity' in the way Einstein did, that interpretation can simply be ignored.[1] There is then no 'speed-limit $c$' on physical interaction, and the two theories, relativity and quantum theory, become properly aligned.

Penny’s Response to ‘The Biggest Question’

Penny's Response to 'The Biggest Question'.

Dear Viv,

I decided to respond by tackling the document according to the subjects dealt with, so I have made headings and written my comments under them. I can't claim that all has been dealt with in a wholly rational and orderly manner, so I shall have to crave your indulgence

1. SELF-IDENTITY:

It can be quite difficult and disturbing to try and identify oneself. Of course, there is the external body that is recognisably me (though perhaps less so as one gets older) and there is the inner me, which doesn't have recognisable outlines or borders and which becomes more elusive as one tries to 'capture' it, especially when one thinks of its parts. By parts, I mean what has been identified by Psychology (particularly Freudian Psychology) as the ego, the superego, the id, the conscious mind, the unconscious mind and the subconscious mind. When one is told that one's mind has all these parts, of which one is not necessarily aware, and that there are levels of one's mind influencing thought or behaviour without one's being conscious of the fact, it becomes a real mine-field and the temptation is to give up the search for oneself on the grounds that it is better for one's mental health to leave well alone. After all, on examining all these supposed parts of my mind, will I find any unifying substance or 'glue' that guarantees its systemic wholeness? Having seen some of the symptoms of those who suffer from profoundly disordered minds, and whose personalities appear fragmented, I wonder about that. In any case, what is personality? I'm by no means qualified to answer that question – but that does not stop me from reflecting upon the matter.

As you remark, what is the real me? Is it a combination of my body and mind from the point of view of my own experience of them? Or is it my body and mind from the perspective of other people? Or is it a subtle combination of the two? If the latter, then there are aspects of me of which I may be unaware (listening to someone else's opinion of oneself can suggest that) and there are aspects of me that no-one else can know. Furthermore, there are probably aspects of myself of which both others and myself are unaware and which are likely to remain a closed book for the foreseeable future. Then, of course, I have to take into account the 'accidents' of my present situation. How is it that I exist in this particular era, in this particular country and society? Would I be significantly different had I been born elsewhere at a different time and to a different family? Is there an 'I' that will remain basically the same, no matter what my outward circumstances, or am I such a product of numerous temporal, familial and societal influences that there is no way I can even imagine having existed as 'me' at another time and in another place?

Then, to look at things from another angle, is there any truth in the claims made by astrologers that personality traits are somehow fixed at the time of one's birth? I must confess that I am dubious about the idea that it is literally 'the stars' that influence personality and even destiny. However, there can be uncanny accuracy in some of the personality assessments made by astrologers, consequently, I am disinclined to dismiss astrology entirely, although I suspect the presently assumed basis is mistaken. (This is a subject to which I have given some thought and that could be enlarged upon, but not here.)

Am I, then, as some mythologies suggest, no more than a formless lump of clay until a particular shape or pattern is stamped on me? That idea can be both intriguing and disturbing, but perhaps I am barking up the wrong tree in even entertaining it for a moment. After all, I can only hope to know what I am actually now, not what I might have been at some other stage of history (bearing in mind my comments above) or could be at some stage in the future. So I have to accept that I am, for better or worse, what I am. There's no going back and changing things. It looks as though the real challenge for any human being is to learn to live with oneself as one is – whatever that may be.

So it all seems a bit hopeless. And yet, surely it is too defeatist to give up immediately, particularly when one is encouraged to 'Know thyself'. So what does one do? Maybe the answer is to take a longer perspective and say, 'I don't know who, or what I am very well now, but that may not always be the case, especially if I allow for the possibility of the existence and survival of something beyond the physical part of me'. Now, I'm well aware that in making a remark like that I am indulging in an assumption for which I have no cogent scientific evidence. Okay, so let's call it a working hypothesis, in the form of a temporary suspension of disbelief for the sake of argument.

As far as I am concerned, all this business of self-identity – knowing who or what I am – cannot begin to make sense, or be open to any useful investigation, unless seen in a much broader context. Of course it all appears pointless and hopeless for the individual if the assumption is that we have to reach our fulfillment and understanding of ourselves on the basis of our limited experience in this world. Now, I am not for one moment suggesting that we should ditch all our understanding of the physical world and its impact on us and indulge in the creation of a fantasy that bears no relation to the real world around us, simply because it is both convenient and comfortable to do so. What I am suggesting is that it is a profitable exercise to imagine that there are depths of meaning to which we may have access, but which can easily be ignored because of our tendency to lose ourselves in the concerns of the everyday material world. Anyone, like yourself, who is naturally a philosopher, will be very aware that reality does not necessarily end at the level of the physical senses; that in order to explain the rationality of Nature, for example, it is essential to posit the existence of an intelligence that is the source of such rationality. Note I write 'intelligence' without a capital letter at this point in the discussion.

2. RELATIVISM versus ABSOLUTISM

You relate our understanding of ourselves and of the world to the debate between Relativism and Absolutism. This is a massive subject and yes, it does have a bearing on the matter of personal identity. Obviously, the range of questions this raises is vast and I could not begin to tackle them all in a piece like this, even if I were capable. I shall home in, therefore, on some basic aspects that have struck me.

The potentially frightening thing about Relativity when pursued rigorously is that it can appear to isolate the individual to an alarming degree – far more than the traditional 'God's Eye View'. Everything would seem to be fragmented and dependent on the individual observer, with no universally absolute property. There is certainly nothing from which the average human being can derive comfort in such a world-view. It's simply a case of having to learn to live with it – if one is prepared to do even that. On the other hand, there's not much, if any, comfort to be derived from Angular Momentum either, although you have identified that as the truly cohesive power at work in the universe. Nevertheless, as you make clear, the Absolutist position has its problems too. In order to get a grip on this matter, I've tried to relate it to the historical account of the movement in science from an Absolutist to a Relativist understanding of Nature. To begin with, though, one could look at it from the point of view of a child's development.

In early childhood, a child's main experience is of its own family and community, which seem to it to be 'the world'. It takes its identity, its understanding of its surroundings and its standards of behaviour from them and thus sees them in an Absolutist light. Only as it grows and matures does it see things in a wider context as it becomes aware of the existence of significant others in a wider society, some of whom embrace a different understanding and have different values and a different experience of life. It is then, in adolescence, that it begins to re-orientate itself with respect to these others, more than to its own family and community. It will also start to appreciate, at this stage, the multiplicity of roles it plays in this larger grouping. Not only is it an offspring (son or daughter), a sibling (brother or sister), it will become a friend, neighbour, workmate, citizen, member of a club or other organisation and probably, in time, a spouse and parent etc. In other words, the primitive Absolutist view is gradually supplanted by a Relativist one. This happens, for most people, without their conscious reflection on the process. They will adapt and take on the various roles quite naturally. How many, though, will actually reflect on what has occurred and identify the change as one from the Absolutist view of early childhood to the Relativist one of more mature adulthood? But when I say 'Relativist' in this context, what do I mean? I mean that we all behave in different ways and see ourselves differently, depending on what role we happen to be assuming at any given time. As relatives, neighbours, friends, workmates or customers, we will adapt our behaviour to the requirements of each situation – woe betide us if we don't!

What is emerging from this is that I have reached the conclusion that a Relativist view of ourselves represents a maturational process. If this is so on the human developmental level, it is equally so on the scientific level. It would seem that scientifically, humanity en masse did not begin to reach that collective mature state until the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Maybe that's too optimistic a statement, because even in the early twenty-first century, comparatively few have recognised, and understood, the full implications of a truly Relativistic outlook. Mention the term 'Relativity' to most people and the response is one of either blank incomprehension or apology, because they haven't a clue what it is. As far as they are concerned, it's something invented by that brainy man, Einstein, so way beyond them and certainly nothing to do with them. Even many who make it their business to talk about Relativity – physicists, mathematicians and philosophers – do not always apply the term as rigorously as they should to their everyday existence, outside their own discipline. It's this problem of thinking in boxes, of failing to see the world holistically. There is also the fear-factor. As I mentioned above, Relativity can be unnerving. Nevertheless, if it's true, it always has been true and always will be true. Nothing changes except our perception of the situation. I sometimes ask myself whether the world would be a better place if the majority did understand Relativity and all its ramifications!

Does this mean that Absolutism is, or should be, a thing of the past – an outmoded way of thinking that has no relevance to a progressive individual or society? The answer to that question has to be 'yes' – but only insofar as we are talking about human beings and their social interactions. In order to clarify what I mean, I shall refer back to the Absolute that must exist to explain the rationality of Nature and its goal-centred activity. If the term 'Absolute' has any relevance at all, it can only be in terms of that intelligence. Of course, we are still left with the thorny problem of deciding the true nature of that intelligence itself. Other than its manifestation in Nature, there is little that empirical science can know or say about it. Is it, or can it be, part of the world in which it is manifest (in which case the term Absolute applied to it is nonsensical) or on the other hand, is it, or can it be, outside the world in which it is manifested? (A thoroughgoing phenomenalist or empiricist presumably would discount the possibility of any source of intelligence being outside, beyond or behind the world as pure metaphysics.) And yet, Relativism as opposed to nothing else doesn't ring true either! Experience tends to indicate that everything in the world comes in pairs, or is defined by its opposite: positive/negative, good/bad, backwards/forwards, inside/outside etc. Also, I was fascinated to read your comment, 'Or is it – perish the thought – the other way round, that the Relative view is the true reality and that the other, the Absolute view, is a fantasy?' Yes, the thought of Relativity being the only reality is unnerving, to say the least. However, as noted above, I have reached the conclusion that Relativity is the reality we have to live with in our everyday lives in this world, but that there is, indeed, and probably has to be ne nevertheless, an Absolute dimension as well - and that Absolute dimension resides in the intelligence that underpins the rationality of Nature. It might be useful (what do you think?) to see this in terms of the traditional disjuncture between Relativity and Quantum Theory. On the macroscopic level, Relativity and Relativistic time-delay reigns, but on the quantum level, all actions are instantaneous so that everything is instantaneously connected – there is 'quantum touching', as Gilbert Lewis put it. To take this analogy to its logical conclusion, whilst we exist in one sense in a highly (if I might be permitted to use the term) Relativistic situation, we are also in direct contact with, and by association partake of, an Absolute, which is that which invests the world with rationality and meaning. That we frequently fail to recognise this is part of the tragedy of the human situation and something the great world religions have sought to address, albeit ineptly in many instances.

Since writing this section, I have tried several times to knock down my argument, but so far I have failed, I intend to continue to try and undermine it, because I wonder if it is too facile. I'd be interested to know your reaction.

3. THE SIGNIFICANCE, OR OTHERWISE, OF SIZE

A topic that frequently arises in discussions about cosmology and the universe in general is size. You will recall that I have, on previous occasions, remarked that I am acutely aware of the distinction between size and complexity and anxious not to conflate the two. Like most people, I am amazed and over-awed by the immensity of space. Nevertheless, I refuse to be 'cowed' by it, as some are. Perhaps it's a sign of vanity on my part that I take this attitude. You refer to size when speaking about pictures of our planet taken from some distance away, in which it looks no more than an insignificant blue dot in vast empty space. Yes, that can be a humbling experience and can serve to emphasise our apparent puniness and unimportance; but then you go on to say 'Or is it that in perceiving, knowing and comprehending things on that scale that we are, virtually, of that scale itself?' Well, yes, I think you've hit the nail on the head by saying that. Whilst I acknowledge that there is definitely complexity and organisation in the arrangement of the stars and galaxies, it is of a different order from that of the minds that perceive those stars and galaxies. But now I find myself in a bit of a quandary. I recognise the complexity of stars and galaxies and have to admit, in the light of a Normal Realist interpretation of phenomena, that there is information transacted instantaneously between all the elements of which they are composed. Furthermore, I have to admit that there is a systemic component involved; that not all these objects are merely passive aggregations, but that they pass on information and respond to varying stimuli, so how do I distinguish them from that which does the perceiving and knowing? In other words, I am seeking for some kind of definition of life, intelligence and conscious awareness.

It seems that the best approach to take is to isolate what can be distinguished as the difference between living and non-living things. A pebble or star would certainly come into the category of non-living in that these objects, no matter how complex 6.

their chemical constituents, or to what extent there is an exchange of 'information' between them on the quantum level, show no sign of being able to exercise will. They have no choice in the way they 'act' or 'react'. Furthermore their 'actions' and 'reactions' are determined solely by statistical laws and their shape, size and internal structure likewise, are in no way governed by conscious behaviour. In the case of stars, physics and chemistry alone account for the qualities that define them. In the case of pebbles, physics, chemistry and weathering determine their size and shape. There is no 'internal' predetermined pattern, somewhat akin to a genetic code, that accounts for the size or shape of either stars or pebbles, as is the case with living organisms, and they show no sign of adapting to any particular environment, as do living organisms, both plants and animals. So plants and animals have both complex chemistry and an internal system of communication, that enable them to behave as autonomous wholes. Remove a sizeable part of a plant or animal and the chances are that it will be unable to continue functioning as a plant or animal – it will probably die. Remove a sizeable part of a star (theoretically, of course) and it will continue to function as a star, though its position in relation to objects around it will change because of the change in mass; remove a sizeable part of a pebble and it will remain a pebble, albeit a smaller one.

As we go 'up' the scale, from plants to insects to reptiles to mammals, we see an increasingly complex internal system of communication – what is identified as a nervous system. It is only when we look at the animals with a central nervous system that we find the beginnings of reflective consciousness. Obviously a star, even the smallest example of one, is far larger than a living creature, but its organisation, its complexity and the level of communication associated with it is many orders lower. A human being can determine his or her behaviour (within limits) in the way a star could never do; a human being can be aware of his existence in a way a star never can, and a human being can reflect on the world around him, ranging the universe in his mind, something a star, for all its size and brilliance, could never do. It is this ability to think and to reflect that distinguishes human beings, and, as you remark, suggests that, 'in knowing and comprehending things, we are virtually of that scale ourselves.' In that sense, we are, I suppose, 'like gods'. For me, it is mind that is the crucial element, the one that ultimately matters most. I can only feel a sense of awe at the privilege of possessing a mind lodged in a body that, on the universal scale, is so tiny, but nevertheless is able to reflect on, and investigate, the wonders of existence.

4. RELIGION and ATHEISM

Religion has been implicit in much of what I have already written above. However, bearing in mind how easy it is to misunderstand the term and the fateful consequences that can follow such misunderstanding, I think a definition is necessary. I must make it clear that my definition might not be exactly what one would find in a textbook of religion, but it is how I see it and should be sufficient to make my position clear in this discussion.

What I mean by 'religion' is an attitude of mind that accepts the belief that there is a dimension of existence that is not simply physical or material. This attitude of mind acknowledges a 'sacred' (in the sense of 'holy', or set apart, which is what 'holy' really means$^{1}$) dimension to life and thus, ideally, treats the world and other beings as of importance in themselves and worthy of respect. To me, religion also admits the existence of an intelligence that is the source of all that exists, and sustains and gives it meaning.

Those who endorse religion often, unconsciously, include in their idea of what religion is, not only a belief in an extra dimension to life but also the unquestioning acceptance of a well-established set of doctrines and dogma, ritual and ceremonial and an established hierarchical structure. Those who do not endorse the religious view will see religion as no more than another political structure designed to keep its leaders in a powerful and privileged position and everyone else in subjugation. The intellectually inclined 'non-believer' will add that the religious view puts a constraint on freedom of thought and progress and will seek to present it as both primitive and outdated. As can be gathered from what I wrote in the opening of this paragraph, I see true religion in essence as neither a power-structure with doctrine and dogma 'set in stone', nor as primitive, outdated and an affront to human understanding and progress – though one has to admit that some forms of religious belief can be just that. I see religion as the pure acknowledgement that there is more to existence than I can possibly know; that there is a source of being and meaning far superior to myself and that the only sensible attitude to take to it is one of profound respect and gratitude. I do not feel 'put down' by accepting this position. Rather the opposite – I feel excited. I am on a journey of discovery and don't know what I might discover – or become – in the process of travelling. As with all journeys, there are lows as well as highs and there are times when one takes a wrong turning, times when things go badly wrong. However, having a belief in that other dimension and a trust in an Intelligence (I've deliberately given it a capital letter this time) that provides meaning and purpose to not only my life, but also to the life of mankind in general, makes it worthwhile. I shall leave discussion of the moral aspects of religious belief to the discussion of Good and Evil.

At this point I should say something expressly about atheism. I have always found atheism an untenable position. Even when I had my greatest doubts about elements of Christian teaching, there was no way I could deny the existence of some superior intelligence, call it God or whatever, as it was obvious to me that there were things well beyond my understanding and well beyond the understanding of all human beings, which could not be accounted for unless one posited the existence of an overarching intelligence. To state that I knew without doubt that there was no God, I'd have to assume I was in possession of all possible knowledge!

$^{1}$The Theologian, Paul Tillich, adds moral perfection, purity, goodness, truth and justice

I would add here that this conclusion has helped save me from solipsism, apart from other objections to that particular belief. Anyway, it seemed to me ridiculous to imagine that everything in the whole universe exists by sheer chance. Just a moment's thought makes one realise that, in order for any trend towards organisation to take hold, a million-, billion-, or even trillion-to-one set of circumstances has to occur, and not just once, but countless times. Also, how could one explain things like love, beauty, justice, right and wrong if one assumed that everything is no more than the accidental coming-together of atoms and molecules which give no sign of being any more than the result of mechanical and statistical laws? (Where could the laws themselves come from?) As you yourself have said many times, where is the morality in a machine?

Having explained my position regarding religion versus atheism, I must now say something about what I believe to be the nature of God. Actually, there is very little I can say. No longer subscribing wholly to orthodox Christian teaching on the subject (and not being drawn to accept wholesale the teaching of any other of the great world religions), I am reduced to reverential silence. All I can say is, there is a 'Something', I know not what, that is so vastly superior to me and to all human beings, that I cannot claim to know and understand it. In fact, I sincerely believe that any attempt on my part to enter directly into its presence (whatever that may mean) would annihilate me, because I am nowhere near ready for such an experience. I suspect that there are intermediaries of some kind between It and me, but I cannot say with any certainty what form they may take and do not wish, at this juncture, to delve any further into that question.

One could say that, with my background, there is a sense in which I was probably 'brainwashed' into accepting religious (specifically theistic) belief at an early age and that I would, as a result, find it extremely problematic to jettison what had become, by default, a natural and comfortable position for me. Equally, though, I could have ended up revolting against religion because I found it to be an emotional and intellectual straitjacket. Whichever way one looks at it, the fact of the matter is that whether one is brought up in a religious household, an agnostic one or an atheistic one, there is the option for the individual of accepting or rejecting the prevailing outlook. Whilst childhood influences should never be underestimated, fortunately they need not be the final word. If they always were, there would be little scope for change or improvement, either in the individual or society.

5. RELIGIOUS DOCTRINE

I have decided to look at this separately from what I've already written above because it requires different treatment from the broader matter of the nature of religion, dealing as it does with the specifics of belief. I can speak with any degree of knowledge only from the Christian perspective. As you are aware, my father was a Theological student when I was born and later ordained a deacon and then made a priest according to Anglican rites. Consequently, my childhood was spent in a highly 'churchified' atmosphere (the first school I attended was a church school in Cardiff) and I took everything to do with the church as a matter of course. I naturally absorbed a great deal relating to the church's doctrines, dogma, liturgy and calendar and became familiar with terms such as parochial, ruridecanal, diocesan, consistory etc. from an early age – to say nothing of the Golden Number and the Epact! I was not always an enthusiastic attender of Sunday School, Matins or Evensong, but I did hone my reading skills with The Book of Common Prayer (1662 edition), something for which I have been grateful in latter years, as it gave me an appreciation of heightened and poetic language that is not easy to find outside Shakespeare. (As a matter of interest, I was given my first copy of the complete works of Shakespeare when I was about nine, though it was many years later that I began to read it.)

This grounding in the church led me on to become at various times a chorister, a Sunday-School teacher, a temporary organist and eventually, after a training course, a Lay Reader. Even so, all along I was not entirely happy with some of the church's doctrines, in particular with regard to the Trinity and the divinity of Christ, so gradually began to question much of what I had been taught. Initially, I was hampered by the age-old dictum that one should not presume to know better than generations of saints and great thinkers of the past. Nevertheless, I continued to wonder about certain things, and when given an opportunity, to question them. One instance remains in my mind. When I was seventeen, the whole diocese of Llandaff underwent what was called a 'mission'. There was a wholesale exchange of priests who spent a week or two in a different part of the diocese from where they normally worked and a number of events were arranged to take place each day of the week. On one occasion there was a discussion session in which parishioners were encouraged to ask the missionaries whatever question they wanted to. As an encouragement to those who were likely to be reticent about speaking in public, it was agreed that the questions could be written down, anonymously, on a piece of paper and placed in a box. My question was to do with the supposed efficacy of the resurrection of Christ. It had occurred to me that, as claims were being made that Christ was part of the Godhead and therefore part of the Being believed to be the creator of the universe, it would be reasonable to ask whether his death and resurrection here on this earth could be seen as effecting salvation for any other human-like beings in the whole universe. Needless to say, I did not receive a satisfactory response. In fact, what was so disappointing was that the matter was brushed aside with a comment on the lines of 'That's something we can't answer'. Whilst I realised that no-one could provide a definitive answer, what really hurt was that no attempt was made to delve into the question. I was left feeling a fool for having asked it in the first place – though at the same time relieved that I'd not been identified as the questioner!

Things finally started to come to a head some years later, after I was given a book token by a local church in appreciation of the fact that I had helped out by conducting a number of services there. The book I purchased was 'Does God Exist?' by Hans Kung, the famous German theologian. Reading that work certainly helped make me more of a free-thinker, leading me along paths I would not have discovered otherwise. Ultimately, I decided, after some thirteen years in the office of Reader, that I could no longer go on with a clear conscience – I had too many reservations and I felt I was being a hypocrite. So, after discussion with the vicar of our parish I returned my licence to the diocese, together with a letter of explanation. I received a very pleasant reply from the bishop thanking me for my service, but was a bit taken aback to read that he would be happy to re-license me if ever I wanted to return to the fold. I thought that he should have said that he would be happy to consider the possibility of re-licensing me, rather than giving me the go-ahead in advance, regardless of circumstances!

What all this is leading up to is my thoughts about doctrine. That the development of a body of doctrine, where any such organisation as a church is concerned, is unavoidable, goes without saying. But, what is unfortunate is that doctrine quickly becomes dogma and absolute conformity is usually required of a believer. Any who do not conform may be threatened with excommunication and/or damnation, and in some instances might be made to suffer imprisonment, torture, and even death. Thankfully, most of the Christian churches do not treat the backslider in such a way these days, but there are still some sects that behave in an outrageous manner, and of course, we are all too familiar with the behaviour of some extremists of the Muslim faith. There seems to be the assumption that what has achieved the status of orthodox doctrine is absolutely and irrevocably true and cannot be questioned or modified. To justify this position, the Bible, or some other holy book in the case of non-Christian religions, is quoted as being the final authority and sometimes even as ordering such punishment. My take on it is that religious doctrine is no more than a striving after the truth and that no generation can claim to have found the absolute and definitive truth. Defending a belief on the grounds that it is the infallible word of God, as written in the Bible, does not seem to me to be convincing. What is in the Bible was written by fallible human beings and has been interpreted by fallible human beings, no matter how holy or inspired they were. I don't doubt there are truths there, but there is definitely no guarantee of absolute truth. Like an hypothesis in science, doctrine is, or should be, subject to revision if some new understanding becomes available. Now, that can sound like an invitation to every Tom, Dick or Harry to come up with a new interpretation and to start a new sect, but I don't see it like that. As far as I am concerned, any honest attempt to understand the ineffable is a very serious matter and is not to be sullied by sectarianism of any kind. Frankly, it is also some thing that is doomed to fall short of our hopes and expectations, because we are presuming to apprehend and comprehend the source of all that is. This is extremely idealistic, I know, and I have to admit that what generally passes for religious belief at best must be regarded as a concession to our limited ability to understand. (This is something we have touched on in our previous Theological Discussions.)

In this context, perhaps it would be as well to refer to the priesthood, as it is a subject that tends to 'get you going'! For obvious reasons, it is a matter that interests me. I can well understand your reaction in view of some of the unfortunate experiences you and Mary have had and I know that you question the need for, and legitimacy of, the very concept of priesthood. The fact of the matter is that, as with all professions, not everyone enters the priesthood for the right reasons, and sadly, not all those who should not be entering it are 'weeded out' in the selection process. Furthermore, even those who have a sincere sense of vocation and are well-intentioned may not always be able to retain that purity of purpose and sense of commitment throughout their ministry. Many young men presented themselves for ordination after the Second World War. They had seen the atrocities and experienced the fear and horror of outright war and it had created in them a feeling of revulsion and a determination to do something to atone for what had happened, and, perhaps, help avoid the same thing happening again. With hindsight, we might be inclined to dismiss their idealism as naïve, but given the circumstances, it is understandable. However, this doesn't begin to address the matter of priesthood per se.

Both the concept and the reality of priesthood have existed, it would seem, for as long as mankind has had the religious impulse. There have always been those who have felt a 'call' to minister to others in their community. It is not surprising, then, that the concept of priesthood arose – that of a body of people who had a sense of being destined to perform the function of a minister or mediator of the 'divine' aspect of life (and I know you don't like the idea of anyone presuming to be a mediator of the 'divine' or the ineffable). The unfortunate thing is that any such 'call' is open to abuse. As I remarked above, there will be those who enter it for the wrong reasons, namely as a means of gaining personal kudos; as a means of manipulating others, to satisfy a lust for power or to gratify some perversion – the latter might end up abusing children for example. Last, but not least, there are those who are just plain lazy and assume that they'll have plenty of opportunity simply to please themselves by doing as little as possible. Add to these, the people who, for one reason or another, genuinely lose any faith they might have had at the beginning of their ministry and it is not surprising that the whole profession is brought into disrepute. It becomes easy for those outside religion and religious establishments to point the finger at all priests and say that the idea of priesthood is rotten, in spite of the fact that there are priests who are sincere and humble and who minister to their 'flocks' with a deep sense of commitment and responsibility and actually bring help and comfort to those who need it. In my experience, the better the priest, the less likely that person is to assume possession of some infallible knowledge and power not vouchsafed to 'ordinary mortals'.

6. GOOD and EVIL

Any discussion of religion is bound to bring up the question of good and evil. This is one of the most problematical things religion has to answer. If, as Christianity teaches, God is good and loves his creation, why is evil allowed in the world? Its very existence surely implies that either God is not as good and loving as most of his supporters maintain, or he is helpless to overcome evil and suffering and therefore is not really an omnipotent being. My first reaction to such comments is that when we talk of 'God', we are talking of our conception of 'God' and when we talk of good and evil, we talk only from our limited and self-absorbed experience. That means we are a bit like a doorman at a museum claiming to be an expert on all the exhibits, or a porter in a hospital making out he knows all about the techniques employed in brain surgery. Am I trying to 'dodge the issue' by putting it this way? No, I am not. I am simply stating the fact that, as I have said previously, no human being can claim to know, in the fullest sense of the word, the nature of the ultimate intelligence, let alone understand its purposes. 'But', I hear you say, 'you still haven't begun to deal with the very real matter of human suffering.'

That there is suffering in the world, I cannot, and would not want to, deny. I see it all around. Some of it is the result of human action, some the result of natural disasters – and goodness knows, there have been enough of those in recent times – and some of disease. Over the past few weeks I have been watching a series of programmes about Nazi Germany, in which I have seen film of some of the unspeakable suffering visited upon millions of innocent people by the Nazi regime. I was very struck by films of Hitler and many of the top Nazis at the Berghof, Hitler's country retreat in Bavaria. They all looked perfectly normal human beings and were acting in a perfectly normal way, chatting and laughing and playing. At play, they were no different from the rest of us, and yet...! Was the problem that they were all too human? By that I mean that they demonstrated so clearly how flawed we human beings are and how thin can be the veneer of so-called civilisation? But, if humans are so flawed, whose fault is it? After all, we did not create ourselves. Well, it is probably the case that any being who has the gifts of reflective consciousness and free-will is going to be open to the possibility of abusing those gifts. Just a few moments of reflection should make it clear why that should be.

In our most basic primitive state, we humans are very self-absorbed, we are also fearful. Put those two factors together and it is not at all surprising that we are prone to what religion calls 'sin'. Add to the mix a certain intellectual laziness and it appears almost inevitable that humans will be tempted to perpetrate foul acts that hurt other human beings, and sometimes in an unthinking way. We should not be so amazed at the horrors of the Nazi death camps when we take all these factors into consideration. But why should it be this way, and why doesn't the ultimate intelligence, call it 'God', or whatever, intervene to stop us? I have no wish to offend anyone's sensibilities by appearing to trivialise this question, or by giving a glib answer, but all I can conclude is, if we were not given the free-will that makes it possible for us to commit heinous acts as well as altruistic ones, we would be no more than robots. Robots, by definition, are machines and cannot, of themselves, have any moral imperatives or sensitivity; they can behave only as those who built and programmed them have decided in advance. Thus, any actions they perform or 'judgements' they make are implicitly determined. The result of this is that they can never be more than just clever machines. They cannot, therefore, take any actions or make any decisions which are either praiseworthy or blameworthy, and cannot be held morally responsible for what they do. Human beings, on the other hand, are obviously capable of behaving independently – of making decisions – and we recognise that fact as indicative of moral responsibility. At the same time, however, humans are finite and limited in both understanding and experience and, as noted above, frequently motivated by self-absorption and fear. Consequently, there are times when we will make wrong or bad decisions and indulge in, at best infantile, or at worst evil, behaviour. If the intelligence that is 'behind' the world were to intervene, it would be reducing humans to the level of robots and all that that implies. This is where the religions have something to say. Most religions have a strong moral aspect and recognise human weakness and frailty. They also claim to provide believers with an invisible support in times of great temptation, suffering and danger, but it is left entirely to the individual to accept or reject that support. There are many tales of remarkable people behaving in a supremely selfless and caring way in the most dire of circumstances and finding and transmitting comfort and strength, despite what is happening to them, or around them. That some, at least, show such courage and moral fibre demonstrates the potential to be able to rise above the fears and temptations which so often hamper our good intentions. No-one is suggesting that it is an easy matter, but it is possible.

The ultimate question has to be 'Why are there Good and Evil in the first place?' Shouldn't I, at this point, talk about the existence of a wholly negative Being opposed to the wholly positive Being most believers consider God to be? Such a wholly negative being is spoken of as 'The Devil', 'Satan', or sometimes 'Beelzebub' etc. Now we are in difficult territory. There are many who believe that there certainly is a Devil who is out to snare humans and who has plenty of minions at his beck and call to carry out his evil deeds for him, or to recruit humans to carry out such deeds. It takes very little imagination to understand why such an idea is so powerful and pervasive when one looks at some of the dreadful things done in the world in the name of both politics and religion. Does it not make a kind of sense to posit the personification of evil to account for those evil deeds?

As it appears that everything comes in pairs and that opposites define each other, for example positive and negative, space and matter etc., does it not, therefore, make sense to say that if we insist that there is a power for good, there has to be its opposite, a power for evil? On the face of it that would be a reasonable supposition, but it would also raise a very uncomfortable question and one that we've encountered in our previous discussions, namely, who, then, is ultimately in charge? Is it the God who is believed to be the all-loving creator, or the Devil? Whether one concludes that it is God or the Devil, the fact would remain that this Intelligence that is 'the ground of our being' ( to borrow a phrase from existentialist theology) is nothing like as all-powerful as we've been led to believe, or at least is having to wage a constant war against a formidable foe. Frankly, I give in where that is concerned. It's going too far into strictly theological territory for this kind of dialogue – and I am not a trained theologian. The only way I could approach it would be by resorting to orthodox doctrine and dogma – something I don't think is appropriate for our present purpose. I could speculate 'until I'm blue in the face', as the saying goes, but I very much doubt that I could present any useful or significant ideas.

To broaden the discussion a little, we are all aware that there are people who indulge in Devil worship, and that there are some who, it is claimed, are Devil-possessed. What does that imply? To me, it implies that, for whatever reason, such people are bent on selfishness and negativity and are deliberately working against whatever creates rather than destroys, whatever looks outwards rather than inwards, and against truth. From what I have read on the subject – and that isn't a great amount, I have to confess – the number who are truly evil and whose behaviour can only be attributed to what is called 'devil possession', is very small indeed. So often the symptomatic behaviour which is manifested can be explained in terms of straightforward mental illness (if mental illness can be described as 'straightforward') or abject fear. Yes, I'm well aware that there is much more to be said about this topic, particularly when taking into account the current mechanistic and materialist background against which both clinical and spiritual judgements are usually made. It may be that this is yet another area in which thinking will alter, and one hopes, advance, when the move to a digital-informational and theistic outlook is made – as it must be eventually.

Any discussion about Good and Evil is bound to prompt questions about the suffering caused by disease. This is a vast subject and one which I have been tackling in the other Theological Discussion document on which I have been working for some time. As my thoughts in the context of this piece are not fully formed, I shall leave a more considered response to the other document.

7. ANTHROPOMORPHISM

Again, bearing in mind our human limitations, it is not surprising that anthropomorphism is rife and has been for as long as people have been able to express themselves in language, spoken or written. It is easy to be critical of this, but in primitive times, how else could human beings begin to express an understanding of the world around them except in terms of themselves and their own experience? The sad thing is when we remain stuck in this outlook, not having learned to take a more objective view as knowledge and understanding have increased. In other words, the means to progress is there, but too often is ignored.

An area in which anthropomorphism can be very obvious is religion. Perhaps, of all the great religions of the world, the most anthropomorphic is Christianity. That statement could be taken as a deliberate criticism, but it is not meant to be. I suspect that the anthropomorphic phase is probably part of human intellectual and spiritual development. I say 'phase' intentionally, because I believe that humans should, in theory at least, be able to pass from that into a different, less anthropomorphic and less self-centred stage. It is my belief that we should heed the words of St Paul who says, 'When I was a child, I spoke like a child, I thought like a child, I reasoned like a child; when I became a man, I gave up childish ways.' (I Corinthians XIII, v.11 [RSV]) It is no exaggeration to say that we are all, in our varying ways, still childlike, if not childish. To think we are fully mature in matters of reasoning and understanding is mistaken. Back in the 'sixties, it was fashionable to say and believe that 'Man has come of age.' Although still in my 'teens, I railed against that idea from the start. I couldn't explain exactly what was wrong with the sentiment, but I felt instinctively that it was profoundly wrong - the kind of expression of overweening confidence that one might expect from an extremely self-important adolescent! There has been little evidence in recent years that 'Man has come of age'. One has only to note the celebrity culture and what too often passes for entertainment, for example the likes of 'Big Brother', to say nothing of our political and social life and the frequent repetition of past mistakes, to see that. We are still, as a culture, as a race even, in the childish anthropomorphic stage. The important thing seems to be to recognise the fact and to attempt to mitigate it as far as possible.

8. THE MIND/MATTER DICHOTOMY

In the light of what I've written above, I must now consider the traditional mind/matter dichotomy. It could be assumed that I accept that dichotomy as real when I talk of an extra dimension to life, but that is not the case. For me, Normal Realism has demonstrated that there is no dichotomy between mind and matter. Nevertheless, it is not always easy to find the right language to express exactly what one wants and needs to say. I have no doubt that thoughts and ideas are every bit as real as pebbles and stars, to refer back to my comments in section 3. In fact, it's not inconceivable that an argument could be framed to support the contention that thoughts and ideas are more real and enduring than what is usually considered to be the concrete and indubitable reality of matter. After all, it is the invisible, rational and statistical 'Laws of Nature' that both produce and sustain the physical world as we know it. We cannot see these 'Laws', we cannot apprehend them with any of the physical senses, but their effects are no less real and palpable for that. To bring the discussion up-to-date, no-one would refuse to accept the reality of mobile 'phones and all they are capable of doing, though very few of us understand fully how they work and we don't actually see the process of communication between them.

On the purely human level, no-one of a sound mind could possibly deny the primacy of thought in much of human behaviour. Perhaps one might be allowed to parody Descartes and say, instead of 'I think, therefore I am' (Cogito ergo sum), 'I think, therefore I do' (if my Latin serves me right - Cogito ergo facio). In other words, so much of human action is preceded by thought, if sometimes subliminal, that it is impossible to remove it from the equation, so to speak, without sacrificing the essence of humanity. Only a thoroughgoing mechanist and materialist would argue against that conclusion and in so doing would be obliged to explain how it is that we humans sometimes can behave in unexpected and counter-intuitive ways, ways that appear to work against self-interest and certainly very differently from those of the push-and-pull dynamics of a machine. That there is a seamless join between matter and mind I have no doubt. It is all part of the holistic understanding of the world that refuses to compartmentalise and refuses to embrace the kind of 'tunnel vision' that sees the train rushing towards one, but fails to notice that the walls of the tunnel are collapsing.

9. EVOLUTION

This is a subject that has long interested me. I have read both Darwin's writings and Maynard's famous work on evolutionary biology. Many years ago I discovered the work of Pere Teilhard de Chardin, the Jesuit palaeontologist, who wrote widely about evolution and who introduced me to terms such as geosphere, biosphere and noosphere. Although I was not happy with his Christocentric 'take' (i.e. his belief that all evolution is aiming towards Christ), I found myself attracted to his theory that evolution starts in the geosphere and moves on into the biosphere and the noosphere, which is the ultimate goal of evolution, being the sphere of the mind. Nowadays, I have to remind myself that all his science and thinking was done to the backdrop of a mechanistic and materialist world-view. It was the absorbing of his work that made me even more acutely aware of the disjuncture between science and religion, and it made me increasingly uncomfortable to see religion fighting a seemingly hopeless rearguard action. I was convinced, deep down, that there should not be such a conflict between the two; that there was nothing in essence that should force them to be seen as inevitably and constantly in opposition. The possibility of a resolution to this conflict was one of the first things I noticed when I read your paper, 'From Light in Space to Space in Light'. It was a complete revelation to me, providing the answer I had been seeking for many years.

Now, of course, the battle is usually between 'Evolutionists' and 'Creationists'. I agree with you entirely that there need be no conflict at all, as both processes can be seen at work in the biological sphere. As you point out, unless there is some intelligent impetus and purpose behind a process, it is unlikely even to get started. Why should a blind mechanical force 'favour' one set of circumstances over another? Yet, the processes themselves, once set in motion, can be seen to operate on fairly predictable mechanical lines, determined by the statistical Laws of Nature. I find it astounding to think that many who happily accept the existence of these Laws of Nature and eagerly point to examples of their operation, do not question how they came to be in the first place. Surely that is the most basic question that needs to be asked, and in order to answer it, allowance has to be made for the existence of some intelligence that has 'willed' them into being and sustains them. As you rightly say, it's no use smashing up the chess pieces to discover the rules of chess.

10. DEISM versus THEISM

This topic inevitably raises the matter of Deism versus Theism. It has been fashionably acceptable in the past for scientists (at least whose who do not call themselves outright atheists) to accept a deistic interpretation of the world. Such an interpretation sees 'God' as some sort of Divine Clockmaker who has created the mechanism and set it to run its course without any further intervention. I suppose that analogy would chime (forgive the pun) with support of the 'Big Bang Theory' of cosmology, in which it is believed that the universe began in an almighty explosion thousands of millions of years ago and will gradually 'wind down' into a 'heat death' thousands of millions of years in the future. You have already demolished that myth, very successfully I would say, by the judicious application of a combination of sheer commonsense logic, sound philosophy and rational physics, so there is no need to go over the arguments here. Of course, the implication of this new NR view is that Deism is replaced by Theism. In other words, the idea of 'God, the Great Clockmaker' is replaced by that of 'God the Great Communicator'. What Normal Realism points us towards is a world where information is being continuously transacted by all its components, including the intelligence that is the source of all rationality. This is a very different kind of world from the mechanistic, detached one of the deists. It is a much more human, or humane, world, one where love, justice and morality can actually mean something. The tragedy is that western culture in particular, appears to have undergone a collective nervous breakdown over the past few centuries by pursuing the flawed deistic model, when all the time, the evidence suggests that it is the theistic model that is much nearer the truth. It is rather like someone in a prison cell desperately trying to escape through the window although the door is wide open.

11. DEATH, the SOUL and the PARANORMAL

I have merged these three topics because they are so inter-related and will deal with death first. It goes without saying that this is a matter that concerns everyone, without exception. To begin with, in true Professor Joad manner, I have to be clear in my mind what I mean by the term 'death'. A definition I arrived at some years ago, and to which I have made reference in one of our previous discussions, is 'the degradation of the body from a systemic to an aggregative state'. In other words, I take bodily death to be a state in which the body is no longer a self-sustaining, closed entity through which information is being constantly transmitted, but merely an aggregate that is proximate, both spatially and temporally. I reached this conclusion after reading the work of Jacques Monod. I did not, by any means, agree with all he said, but I found his definition of living as opposed to non-living matter very useful. However, the definition I have just presented is entirely my own, and I am aware that it probably needs refinement.

Having disposed of my definition of physical death, I shall talk about the subject in a less detached manner. It is something that occupies one's mind more as one gets older and nearer the prospect of one's own demise. Even so, it is impossible to contemplate one's own death in that one cannot imagine being in a state of future non-existence any more than one can say what it felt like not to have been born. I have often reflected on the fact that countless millions of years passed before I came into the world - assuming there is no such thing as reincarnation. Over the last two million years and more, vast numbers of people have lived out their lives on this planet and left their mark on it, physically, culturally, linguistically, artistically, philosophically, theologically and scientifically. Where was I during all that time? As far as I know, I just wasn't. Yet a fair proportion of those people were my direct ancestors. Was my existence somehow already 'written in' to the story long ago, or was my entry into the world the result of pure chance? Furthermore, how is it I live in this particular era? Is that pure chance, or is there some reason – other than the biological fact of my conception – for that?

I think you realise by now that I am someone who believes very strongly in rationality and honesty and that I am wary about accepting anything without analysing it and testing it. That, of course, doesn't mean that I always live up to my ideals – I am, after all, only a normal, flawed human being! However, I have no desire to lay claim to an experience I've not had or to try and make something out of nothing. Equally, I cannot and will not deny it when I have had an experience that is out of the normal run of things. I certainly have had such experiences over a number of years and they have led me to the conclusion that there probably is such a thing as life after death, though I cannot be one hundred percent sure about it, not having yet experienced it for myself, and cannot say exactly what form survival might take. Also, although there are definitely charlatans in the field of spiritualism, I am convinced – again as the result of various experiences – that there are genuine spiritualists, too. They are people who seem to have an extra sense that most of us do not have. The responsible ones use their 'gift' wisely and will not deliberately mislead, or take advantage of, the emotionally vulnerable. The only person I am aware of having known with such a 'gift' was able, without any prompting from me, to tell me things she could not possibly have known about relatives of mine who had died. Furthermore, evidence later came to light that confirmed some of the things she said. Some members of my family have had spiritualist tendencies, though not all have been happy about the fact or involved themselves in seances and the like.

Personally, I have never been anxious to become involved in such things, being somewhat doubtful about what is actually happening – and what might happen. But that does not mean that I was not prepared to allow my former friend to pass on any information that was relevant to me. Whilst I have not even tried a ouija board, let alone a séance, it appears that I do have a slight tendency to mediumship as I have had some experiences that, I'm told, suggest that – and still sometimes have them. I definitely do not want to cultivate any leaning I might have in that direction, but must admit that I do seem to have something. As I have mentioned previously, my father was a hypnotist – he hypnotised my mother and me more than once. Besides this, he was able to 'conjure up' a peach perfume apparently out of nowhere. He told me it was something that some Buddhist monks could do and that it was difficult to achieve and took a lot of preparation. He also, on one occasion, levitated one of his pupils in the school where he was teaching at the time. The levitation was very little, he said, but noticeable – and that, too, was difficult and took a lot of preparation. All this is highly indicative of there being an extra 'dimension' to life that is very real, but not necessarily accessed easily. One may not be able to explain such phenomena in the context of standard science, but there is something to be explained, nonetheless. Is it the case, to quote Hamlet, that 'There are more things in heaven and earth, Horatio, than are dreamt of in your philosophy'? Some of the things I have experienced would seem to support both telepathy and psychic phenomena as well as belief in survival after death, but I am well aware that I have no absolute proof of any of this. Am I naïve and deluded, or a bit unbalanced? Am I reading more into events than is warranted? Perhaps I am, and yet... It's not that I am the only one to have had odd experiences. Other people have told me of similar things that have happened to them and these people are of normal intelligence, sound of mind and honest. Some have been very reluctant subjects of odd phenomena on times and I can find no reason to disbelieve them.

Where does all this leave us where belief in a soul is concerned? What is the 'soul' anyway? Those who investigate such matters tell us that we all have an 'aura' surrounding our physical bodies and that there are people capable of seeing auras. But what is an 'aura', come to that? Some kind of energy, it would appear. These same investigators say it is the energy that is the essence of us and which 'moves on' at death. They further assert that all we are – including our memories – is stored in an 'astral body' that is, to all intents and purposes, a clone of our physical bodies. Personally, I am not entirely happy with the term 'astral body', because of its associations with the sometimes fraudulent practice of mediumship. (As you will realise from what I have written previously, I don't reject mediumship out of hand. I simply acknowledge that there are those who are not honest practitioners, just as there are those in the priesthood who are not honest practitioners.) However, I do not have any real problem with the idea of the self being able to exist, apart from its physical manifestation, and in the form of an 'astral body'.

DIMENSIONS

There is no way I am qualified to talk with any certainty about the technicalities of dimensions. Even so, I must make my opinion clear - if I haven't done so in what I've written already. I cannot see how anyone could begin to refuse to accept that there are four dimensions to our ordinary everyday existence – three spatial dimensions plus time. That there are potentially many more dimensions seems obvious when one reflects seriously about the past, for example. When one 'factors in' the future as well, it is almost impossible not to conclude that there must be a tremendous amount that we don't understand.

Inevitably, any talk about the possible survival of the individual after physical death requires there to be at least one more dimension beyond those of our present life. However, thinking the matter through has caused me to suspect that even what we call 'the next life' might not be all there is. In a rather naïve and simplistic manner, the teaching of some of the world religions has given the impression to the average person that we die and are just despatched to Hell or Heaven, according to our behaviour here, for eternity. Surely, if it is possible to appreciate in this life that there are many layers to existence and that we all differ as individuals and societies with regard to intellectual and moral, to say nothing of scientific, understanding, then it seems strange to assume that at the point of physical death we are going to be divided into just two 'camps' and remain in one or the other of them, in our original state, for ever. Surely, the fact of physical death is not going to solve, instantaneously, all the difficulties we have experienced in our lives hitherto, endow us with all we lack and, furthermore, reconcile us with others from a totally different background and with a wholly different conception of existence. How could the likes of me cope with that? If the only choice is being made instantly perfect and omniscient and spending the rest of eternity playing a harp on a cloud or, on the other hand, spending the rest of eternity in torment, I would rather be annihilated! If I have the potential, and am intended, to achieve anything like perfection, I would prefer to do it incrementally and in free co-operation than have it 'imposed' on me. No doubt this is symptomatic of my possession of the kind of free-will to which I have alluded.

Of course, the implication of such a view is that there have to be further dimensions beyond that to which it might be assumed we go immediately after death. After all, no-one wants to remain in the infants' school for ever – a retort that could be made equally to those who desire eternal life in the flesh. If there is to be any true progress, the conditions for that progress have to be right and I can only assume that the conditions would not be right if we simply found ourselves in a place (for want of a better word) where all who had died over the millennia were together in different stages of advancement. Now that would be a species of Hell! I suppose the conclusion that there is a 'moving on' from one dimension to another after death, according to one's state of being, could find support in the Parable of the Wheat and the Tares. I realise that in quoting that, I am taking for granted that Jesus Christ had reliable knowledge of the situation, but I don't intend pursuing that at present.

FINALE

In my opinion, your admission that you can find comfort in certain poetry, hymns and biblical quotations – especially the psalms – does nothing to undermine your position as a rational thinker. The fact is that there always have been (and I hope always will be) people who have a deeper insight than most into the human condition and its perennial problems and who have managed to express their thoughts and conclusions in such a way that they leave a powerful legacy to generations who follow. There are times when one is searching for the words to express a particular idea or feeling and cannot find them, then one chances upon a piece of literature, whether secular or religious, that sums it up beautifully. This can gave the additional effect of making one feel connected in some strange way, not only to the writer of the work, but also to the unknown generations who have derived the same satisfaction, understanding, and yes, comfort from reading the words. Let's face it, we are all little more than children under the skin, no matter how grown-up and in control we like to appear to the world. We all need support and guidance from time-to-time and there is no shame in admitting it. Furthermore, whilst the rational, logical element in the personality is extremely important if humanity is to make progress, we are not only rational beings, we are also beings with feelings, for whom the love of family and friends and the beauty of nature and art in all its forms, are as important as philosophy, logic, science and mathematics.

Despite everything, basically I am an optimist. When all is said and done, life can be very good. If one looks, there is a redeeming feature to be found in almost every situation. After all, there can be no deep valley without high hills, no dark without light. On balance one should trust – in oneself and whatever intelligence is responsible for our being here. There is a verse from Psalm 37 that has often sustained me in trying times:

'Trust in the Lord and be doing good; dwell in the land and verily thou shalt be fed: Delight thou in the Lord and he shall give thee thy heart's desire.'

The first accredited modern relativist was George Berkeley (1685-1753). In his rejection of classical materialism he made the first radical move in this philosophical direction.

What Berkeley did, presaging the relativism l of Einstein, was to invert this Democritan way of thinking, giving precedence, in our scientific knowledge of the world, to the observational information-data - the phenomena - over Democritus' unseen 'atoms'. In other words, for Berkeley, the ieality of anything consisted, first and foremost, in being observed, or 'perceived' in the language he used. Anything else we may think or talk about, space, time and even the existence of atoms and the passage of light between them, is projected from information supplied by the senses. Berkeley's choice of the word 'perceived', however, was unfortunate because it made it seem that, in order to exist, a material object has to be actually observedby a human observer, which made the object's unobserved existence as a thing in itself indescribable. The problem Berkeley thus bequeathed to his followers was that of how we can ever bq assured of the reality of anything other than our own subjective impressions - or 'ideas', as he called them.

This was the problem which was handed on via Berkeley's successor, David Hume (1711-1776) to Immanuel Kant (1724-1804). By taking Berkeley's system of ideas (idealism) to its logical conclusion, Hume had shown that if these 'ideas' are all there are, then there can be no 'system' that can hold them together other than one's own pure 'habits' of perceiving. This reduction to absurdity, by Hume, of Berkeley's 'way by ideas' galvanised Kant. He could not see how it was possible, if what Hume had deduced was true, that the science of Isaac Newton (1642-1727) could have been so spectacularly successful?

To reconcile Berkeley's phenomenalism with Newtonian science, Kant dispensed with Berkeley's 'ideas' and replaced them wtth'concepts'. These he sorted into a system of logical classes and categorles, designed to paraphrase, in his phenomenological rft*r, the science of Galileo andNewton.

Unfortunately, Kant's 'concepts' were no improvement on either Berkeley's 'ideas' or Hume's 'habits', because they were still rooted in the human psyche. As viewed from the standpoint of objective science, therefore, Kant's version of phenomenalism was no more than a form of 'subjective idsalism'.

The third stage in the move towards modern relativism was made by Ernst Mach (1833-1916), the philosophical mentor of Albert Einstein (1879-1955). Mach replaced Kant's psychological system of 'concepts' with a system of objective relations between objects as viewed in different observational frameworks, or perspectives. This, in effect, was the system that became the famous Special Theory of Relativity presented by Alberl Einstein in 1905. To Mach's consternation, however, Einstein took the backwards step of basing his relativism on the atomic materialism of Democritus. As Mach wrote:

I gather from publicafions which have reached me, and especially from my correspondence that I am gradually becoming regarded as the forerunner of relativity. ... I must, however, as assuredly disclaim to be a forerunnsr of the relativists as I withhold from the atomistic belief of the present day.[11

In this way,light, which for Mach was the observational information (the fundamental sensory/instrumental data) from which the phenomena of space, time and motion are communally projected, was presented by Einstein, not as an integral part of that phenomenal scenario but as a sub-phenomenal conveyor of its constituent bits of information, in the form of esoterically conceived wave-particles, called 'photons', from the world of atoms that Democritus had supposed was their underlying root source.

This, for Mach, meant that the transition that Berkeley had initiated from Democritan materialism to modem relativism had stalled. Einstein's version of 'relativity' had ended-up in a 'hung state' in which it was neither the one thing nor the other. In Mach's view, therefore, it was no more than a'transitory inspiration in the history of science'.[2] fnis opinion of Mach's is supported by the growing number of conferences, nowadays, to accommodate the increasing dissatisfactions of scientists with Einstein's version of Machian relativism, not least on account of paradoxes the theory generates when employed in contexts of quantum physics. This offers testimony to the fact that, despite the purely practical scientific advances made by Einstein, the philosophical transition from metaphysical materialism to phenomenalism - that is. from a classical matter-based to a truly empirical information-based science remains to be completed.

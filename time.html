TIME Dear Clive, Thanks for your review of the Butterfield book ‘The arguments of time’. Sorry to have been so long in responding but I found it difficult to give my full attention to anything much apart from the second Swansea Workshop, which took place last month. Developments in this direction have been interesting, to say the least, and I hope to have the opportunity of reporting it at the next ANPA meeting. I am also hoping to be given a slot to disabuse our President of his very strange ideas of what ‘Philosophy’ means in the context of physical science – or, indeed, in any context. Anyway, as to your views on time, my trouble with an emergence theory of time is a philosophical one, of linguistic analysis in particular. To ‘emerge’ takes time. So what can ‘time emerges’ possibly mean? Anyway, to borrow one of your own favourite phrases, Why all the fuss (about time)? From the point of view of plain scientific and commonsense empiricism I can see no problem whatsoever with time. What else can time be but what is measured by clocks? And what else but some arbitrarily chosen clock can serve as a standard of time for all clocks? Similar clocks measure similar times; dissimilar ones measure dissimilar times. And how can there be any time in between the ultimate clocks – that is, in vacuo’? How can the rate of one clock be compared with another, other than visually; that is, in the light they exchange. And since the distances between clocks are times in the ratio of units c, then how else can changes in distance between clocks visually change their readings with respect to one another other than in the way that was first noted by Römer in his observations of the Jovian ‘pendulum’ Io. The ultimate clocks are, of course, atoms, and the ultimate instrument for reading them is the spectroscope. And, given that distance is time, how can there be any simultaneity between the readings of clocks other than when the distance between them is theoretically zero. In all other cases the distance and changes in distance break the synchrony, so that time (proper-time tP) can never be non-local or ‘universal’. All there can be, so far as any finite observer is concerned. is relative time (tR) which is the resultant, by Pythagoras, of the two orthogonal (i.e., dependent but co-exclusive) time-components, the space-time component s/c and the proper-time component tP, viz.: $t_R = \sqrt{(s/c)^2 + t_P^2}$  (1). This resultant produces, as you have seen many times in my ANPA talks, what relativists call time-dilation, and that if we substitute s, in (1) with the equivalent relativistic expression vtR and simplify we get tR = tP/  [1 – (v2/c2)] (2) which is, of course, the familiar (for some, notorious) time-dilation formula of Special Relativity. In the scale of time-dilation, like all other scales, there has to be a most and a least. Those clocks which are least time-dilated are therefore the ones that move slowest relatively to one another, and vice versa. The nearest we get, then, to a non-local ‘GMT’ is the statistical norm of all the various motions relative to this datum-zero of the system as a whole, which forms the universally preferred reference datum not only for time but also for motion, in accordance with ‘Mach’s principle’. Philosophical mysteries are created. We can ask strange questions like ‘What is red in and of itself as opposed to our different private perceptions of it?’ But what can it matter so long as we stop when a traffic-light shows red, or so long as we can concur that a spectral line shifts towards the red rather than the blue? The same with time. What is time, in and of itself, when nothing is registering it? Where does the smile go when no-one is smiling? Does it linger in the sky, like the smile of Lewis Carroll’s Cheshire Cat? Carroll was a writer of entertaining nonsense. He would have done well with some of the philosophical questions about time. (Diogenes, the ‘laughing philosopher’ would have been in stitches!) As I see it, changes don’t take place in time, they are time. As I say, what time can there be but that by which a change takes place compared with some other change taken as an arbitrary standard. Otherwise, all changes, and therefore all times, are different. By the way, re your mention of the distinction between ontology, the study of what is, and epistemology, the study of our knowledge of what there is, this is philosophically passé (since the 1920s, as I hope to demonstrate in my next talk at ANPA). This was when the Cartesian/Lockean radical division between the world as it is and the world as we perceive it was logically removed by Wittgenstein, et al. Along with this bogus distinction departs any radical distinction between time in the world and time in the mind. Best wishes to you and Peggy,

Viv

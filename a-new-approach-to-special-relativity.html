A new approach to Special Relativity

by N. VIVIAN POPE

Department of Mathematics, University of Keele, Keele, Staffordshire ST5 5BG, England

We present a new commonsense approach to Special Relativity based on direct observation instead of from the usual rationale conceived in terms of light travelling invisibly in vacuo. This approach permits the construction of a model of the time dilation effect.

1. The case for a new interpretation of Special Relativity

Most interest in Special Relativity over the years has focused, quite rightly, on the consequences of the axioms of the theory and the experimental verification of these consequences. Notably little attention has been paid to the axioms themselves. Our aim in this paper is not to join forces in attacking the sufficiency of the theory, far less on the usual issue of the 'clock paradox', but to take a closer look at the axioms. We do not in any way challenge the empirical justification for the theory. There can be no doubt that in the physical sense the theory works. However, given that the set of equations called the Lorentz transformation works to the satisfaction of physicists, and that the Lorentz transformation can be derived from or is implied by Einstein's theory, it by no means follows, as P. W. Bridgman remarks [1], that Einstein's theory can be derived from the Lorentz transformation.

What is so surprising therefore is that Bertrand Russell of all people should declare, 'Technically, the whole of the Special Theory is contained in the Lorentz Transformation' [2]. However, if all of Einstein's theory is contained in the Lorentz transformation, replies Louis Essen, 'then why is it called Einstein's theory and not Lorentz's?' [3]. In so far as a distinction has to be maintained between these two theories, then some significance must be attached not only to the consequences as such, but also to the postulates from which these consequences follow.

Of course, there are all sorts of other ways in which the Lorentz transformation may be derived using other sets of postulates entirely different from Einstein's. If the difference between Einstein's postulates and Lorentz's is not negligible then neither, necessarily, is the difference between Einstein's and any of these others. In any sense in which Einstein's theory (in the sense of the postulates) is judged to be better than Lorentz's, therefore, there may be others, some perhaps as yet undiscovered, which are possibly better in this same sense than Einstein's.

In any balanced view of relativity, then, the question of whether one of these different interpretations, all equally consistent, will turn out to be uniquely 'the best' must be left empirically open. While different theories might all be equally viable

from a testable point of view, there are other practical issues to be considered. There is, for instance, the need for conserving nervous energy in coming to grips with such theories, the question of mnemonic facility in retaining them, their congruity with other ideas in other areas and so on. In short, there is the question of their sociological value in creating and maintaining a developing body of common understanding. None of these criteria can be denied except in the very narrowest conception of 'practicality'.

The search, then, for a theory of relativity which satisfies these more general needs must be allowed to continue. The very possibility, for instance, that Einstein's interpretation of relativity might not be the best from an educational point of view should be good enough reason in itself, one would think, for exploring easier and more amicable alternatives.

What this paper proposes, accordingly, is that relativity, sufficient though it is experimentally, may be simplified by getting rid of a particular concept that is central to the Einsteinian interpretation but which is, in fact, logically redundant. This is the concept of 'the speed of light in vacuo', the notorious stumbling block to commonsense comprehension.

2. The case against the 'Speed' interpretation of c

It is instructive to consider that when Special Relativity first appeared, the person who might most have been expected to support it rejected it. That person was Ernst Mach. Einstein's express justification for his own 'relativistic' interpretation of mass, length and time was that it was based squarely on Machian phenomenalism. Mach, however, was not happy about this [4]. For Mach, what was wrong with Einstein's theory was that it was not a consistent relativism, or phenomenalism, but a kind of barren hybrid, an eclectic compromise between antithetical ideas, which therefore made no logical or philosophical sense. Where, for instance, in any consistently positivistic outlook could there be any parallel to Einstein's absolutist assumption of 'light' travelling invisibly in vacuo?

Of course, one now tends to say that the speed of light in vacuo is one of the most well measured and well confirmed constants in modern physics. However, it must be remembered that experimental confirmation does not constitute a proof of a correct interpretation. It certainly seems as though light is propagated through space with a constant speed—until we try to construct in our minds any intuitive idea as to just how that motion takes place.

Besides philosophical objections, there are all sorts of logical and mathematical reasons against interpreting c as a 'speed'. In Special Relativity, for instance, the law of relativistic addition of speeds shows that speeds of material objects can never be combined to give a speed greater than or equal to c, that is, if $u < c$ and $v < c$ then

$$\frac{u+v}{1+(uv/c^2)} < c$$

In other words, the set of all speeds less than c is closed under relativistic addition. From a mathematical point of view, c can be thought of as the least upper bound of the set of all material speeds, but is not necessarily a speed in itself. From a purely physical point of view, this implies that we can never build a spacecraft that can attain 'speed' c, no matter how advanced our technology becomes. This suggests an entirely paradoxical situation.

These are not the only possible objections by any means. Another objection concerns the fact that light 'travelling' in vacuo is impossible to observe (which was precisely the point of Mach's objection). For light to be seen travelling in vacuo it would have to give off light; and that, to be seen travelling, would have to give off light, and so on. This involves the nonsense of a logical regress to infinity.

All of these apparent paradoxes can be resolved if one simply thinks of c as a conversion factor, that is, our physical dimensions of distance and time are related in the constant ratio of arbitrary units c. In astronomy, for instance, we see this inseparable relationship expressed in the 'light-year', which is as much a measure of time as it is of distance. Imagine how absurd it would be to measure the 'speed' of light across this distance! That is the kind of logical circularity we inevitably encounter, in one way or another, in presuming to measure the 'speed' of light. There is no way whatever in which a distance in space and a 'speed of light-propagation' through that space can be measured independently.

Treating c simply as a conversion factor between observational distance and time leads to a positivistic reinterpretation of Special Relativity, which is as follows.

3. A positivistic reinterpretation of Special Relativity

The two fundamental axioms on which Einstein's theory is based can be written as

  • (i) The laws of nature are identical in all inertial frames;
  • (ii) Light signals in a vacuum are propagated rectilinearly with the same speed c, at all times, in all directions and in all inertial frames [5].

The axioms we shall begin with, by contrast, are those of a consistent philosophical relativism or phenomenalism, in which every measure known to science – including even the weight and solidity of matter – is a function, ultimately, of the way in which sensations, both direct and instrumental, are structured in observational space and time. Our basic axioms are therefore

  • (A1) The ultimate dimensions of all natural phenomena are observational distance and time;
  • (A2) Observational distance and time have a constant ratio of units, c for all observers sharing that same conventional choice of units.

These axioms express a purely observational, or positivistic, approach to physics, of the kind adopted by Mach. There are no classical presuppositions about 'atoms' and 'space', nor about a 'universe', in any sense which is not entirely reducible to observational data and the conventional (linguistic) forms in which these data are structured.

The consequences of these latter axioms, as in Einstein's or in any other theory of relativity, must, of course, fit the known experimental data. In this paper we concentrate on the phenomenon of time-dilation and show that it follows as a simple consequence of (A1) and (A2). We also show that it is possible to provide a model of this effect.

4. The geometrical time-effect of relative motion

In classical representations of the motions of bodies, the radial distance of a body X relative to some fixed origin O is described by a curve against two perpendicular axes, S and T, which signify distance and time, respectively. If the motion is uniform

and $X$ passes $O$ at time $t=0$, then the curve is a straight line with gradient $v_X=s_X/t_X$ and equation $s=v_X t$ as shown in figure 1, where $v_X$ is the speed of $X$ and $s_X$ is the distance travelled from $O$ in the period $t_X$, relative to $O$ or $X$. In such diagrams, the slope of a curve at any particular point gives the speed of $X$, relative to $O$, at any particular instant, but there is no special significance attached to the lengths of such curves.

With the axioms given in §3, this situation is drastically altered. If $c$ is treated simply as a conversion factor, then a fixed geometrical relationship is established between the dimensions $S$ and $T$. Thus, on grounds of plain commonsense, it is to be expected that motion will affect time in the way Special Relativity describes. This is because motions are now geometrical (or rather, geometro-dynamical) combinations of time-measures in different dimensions—analogous to the lengths of an ordinary area. Motion, that is to say, is a composite space-time extension measured in units of $3 \times 10^8$ metres/$c$ or seconds, which are numerically and dimensionally the same. It stands to reason, therefore, that an object which combines these measures of distance and time in what is commonly called 'motion' will have a duration which is dilated compared with a relatively stationary body.

We are speaking, of course, of these events taking place in a field of observational space ($O$'s observational space) not in an 'inertial space' of the conventional theory. The motion of each observer is thus always a motion as described by (i.e. relative to) some other observer. That is to say, the motion of $X$ is the motion of $X$ relative to the specified observer $O$, there being no single, universal 'God's eye view' of how motion separating $O$ and $X$ can be seen through the eyes of both observers simultaneously. This observational space is naturally spherical, with the observer at the centre of extrapolation, the whole space evolving in the time ($T$) of the observer at its centre. This situation begs no question of intrinsic anisotropy or irregularity of the kind considered by some conventional theories. The system is projected out of sensory and instrumental information and is assumed at this stage to be purely visual.

Suppose, once again, that O observes a body X travelling at a constant speed, that X carries a clock identical to O's and that on passing O, O's and X's clocks are synchronized. As observed by O, X then travels a radial distance s_X measured in metres, in a time t_X measured in seconds by the clock carried with X, relative to O. In keeping with the axioms of this paper, that radial distance travelled by X relative to O can be measured in seconds by the use of the conversion factor c. This makes figure 1 a two-dimensional time diagram, rather than a space-time diagram as in the classical case. In this time-diagram the two axes (re-labelled S/c and T) are both measured in units of a second. It follows then that if the passage of time, as recorded by O's clock, for X to travel the distance s_X is t, measured in seconds, then t will be simply the length of the hypotenuse of the triangle in figure 1, given by

$$t = \sqrt[4]{((s_X/c)^2 + t_X^2)} \tag{1}$$

The positive root is taken due to considerations of causality.

Now the speed, v, of X relative to O, that is the usual relative speed as defined in Special Relativity, is given by

$$v = s_X/t \tag{2}$$

Substituting (2) into (1) gives

$$t = \sqrt[4]{((vt/c)^2 + t_X^2)} \tag{3}$$

which simplifies to

$$t_X = \sqrt[4]{(1 - (v^2/c^2))t} \tag{4}$$

This is, of course, the standard formula for the time-dilation phenomenon, derived here purely from premises about observational dimensions and an observational definition of motion.

5. The cone model of time-dilation

This conceptually more economical way of deriving the time-dilation formula implies a model of time-dilation, which is constructed as follows. (Once again, for simplicity, let the motion be in one space dimension only.) Consider the situation involving observer O and body X as described in the previous section. If t_X is fixed and the time-measure s_X/c is plotted against the time-measure t, the result is a rectangular hyperbola, with equation

$$t^2 - (s_X/c)^2 = t_X^2 \tag{5}$$

(This follows from (1).) One half of a typical hyperbola, plotted for non-negative values of t only, is shown in figure 2. Any such hyperbola is asymptotic to the degenerate light cone, with equations s = ±ct. On the other hand, if t is fixed and the time-measure s_X/c is plotted against the time-measure t_X, the result is a circle, with centre at O and radius t. (This follows from (5).) Now a hyperbola is a conic section cut in a direction parallel to the cone's axis, whereas a circle is a conic section cut in the direction perpendicular to the cone's axis. This suggests the model of a cone, plotted against the perpendicular time axes which record s_X/c, t_X and t. The equation of this cone will be

$$t_X^2 + (s_X/c)^2 = t^2 \tag{6}$$

(where now, in contrast to (5), t_X, s_X and t are all variables). The axis of such a cone is

This 'time-cone' describes the intimate relationship between the time-measures t_X, t and s_X/c. The larger the value of s_X/c, the greater is the difference between t and t_X (and t_X < t for s_X/c > 0). Thus the cone exhibits the time-dilation effect in a simple graphical way.

6. The Lorentz-Fitzgerald contraction

So far in this paper no mention has been made of the Lorentz-Fitzgerald contraction-effect. It can be shown, however, that this relativistic length-contraction follows logically and easily from (4). We recall, for instance, that s_X is the radial distance travelled by X, as observed by O in the time t, recorded by O's clock, and is given by (2). Meanwhile, for that same distance X's clock, as O observes, registers t_X, so that the distance travelled by X, which for O is s_X = vt is, for X, only s_XX, given by

$$s_{XX} = vt_X \tag{7}$$

Replacing v using (2) gives

$$s_{XX} = (s_X/t)t_X \tag{8}$$

Substituting for $t_X/t$ the root term from (4) then produces

$$s_{XX} = \sqrt[4]{(1 - (v^2/c^2))s_X} \tag{9}$$

This is the standard formula for the length-contraction phenomenon, here made intuitively evident by the cone model.

If it seems pointless to have replicated some of Einstein's results in this way, it can seem so only from a narrow Experimental Science point of view. However, Experimental Science, as we have argued, has no monopoly on practicality; hence, from a broader and more general standpoint, it is far from trivial if the standard consequences of relativity can be deduced in this very simple way, without commonsense being confounded by having to think of a 'light' which travels unseen, in an absolute nothing, at a speed which is the same for all differently moving observers.

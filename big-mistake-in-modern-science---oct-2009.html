Big mistake in Modern Science - Oct. 2009

N. Vivian Pope — 2009

Modern Science’s Biggest mistake Viv Pope It is alleged that Relativity’s speed-limit c and Quantum Theory’s instantaneity of distant interaction are contradictory. That is simply not so. Anyone who understands Relativity knows that in its own time, anything travelling at the speed c travels instantly from A to B. The lightquantum does just that – travels between A and B, in its own proper-time. instantaneously as required by Quantum Theory. So where’s the contradiction when the two theories say the same thing? Why has this fact not been grasped? It is because we customarily think of light as something ‘travelling’, hence the name ‘photon’, which suggests that the light quantum is a space-travelling ‘particle’. However, it cannot be a ‘particle’ because at its supposed ‘speed c’ the mass of that particle would be infinite, which is just nonsense. So if that quantum is not a particle, then what is it? Well, since all such quantum interactions are intrinsically instantaneous, quite obviously there can be no space as such between its ‘beginning’ and ‘end’. In other words, at the quantum level there is no such thing as what we call ‘space’. Space is no more than an observational projection, in a manner similar to that in which the viewer of a video scenario might fancifully ascribe to the screen pixels the same qualities, dimensions and motions as those portrayed in the scenario. This classic mode of thinking is completely upside-down. What we see – ‘perceive’, ‘observe’, ‘sense’ ‘detect’, call it what you will – are always, in the very first instance, the quantum ‘pixels’ in large informational patterns and sequences, from which we observationally project space and all it contains, in the same way that the viewer projects the features and actions of objects in a movie or a video drama. The corrective to this upside-down state of affairs is to switch from thinking of light as travelling in space to thinking of the space as in the light. This sets relativistic physics squarely on the same footing as quantum physics, both standing upright alongside each other. Both are then relativistic according to the definition of relativity as ‘having meaning or significance only in relation to something else; not absolute’. These ‘somethings’ are the objects we see and sense all around us, all of which are continually and instantly interacting with one another at the quantum level. Some of these objects we see directly and others only indirectly, while some we do not see as such but whose existence we discern via other things which are instrumental to our perspectives and conceptions of them. Among these ‘somethings’ are, of course, ourselves and our fellow observers whose perceptions concur with ours via the medium of common linguistic and behavioural communication – language in the Wittgensteinian sense of the word. In all of this knowledge of the world, the ultimate elements of that knowledge are these quantum pixels, between which there is no space, no mechanical-causal connection, nor any kind of medium, such as air, ether or of any other kind on which ‘waves’ may be conducted.. Every quantum event is a single discrete, complete, immediate and instantaneous resonance between quantised electron levels in the atoms of bodies, as manifest in the barcode-like series of spectra. In this way of thinking, relativistic and quantum physics are as one. So, what is Science’s ‘Biggest mistake’? It is to think of light as something travelling in space instead of thinking of the space – and the time – as a projection from informational patterns and sequences of quantum pixels of light as we see it. In short, science’s Biggest Mistake was to put light in space instead of the space – and everything it contains – in the light [1] . October, 2009.

The Need for De-Theorising Modern Physics

N. Vivian Pope

All physics, basically, is about phenomena. But phenomena don't come readily classified, divided and named. It is we who do this with our constructions of scientific language. The tools of scientific language are logic and mathematics. Logic classifies and divides; mathematics quantifies and measures. Whichever we use, it is all language. The fact that this language works to the extent it does means that it reflects something beyond language, something that exists in nature itself. This super-language, or language of nature, is what the Greeks called logos. Logos is the system of logical interrelations among natural phenomena. The aim of all true physical science is to match its language to that natural reality.

To achieve this, physics language needs to maintain logical consistency throughout. However, our finite language offers no guaranteed way of describing and naming that is immune to the necessity for revision and radical reconstruction in the light of new knowledge. No working-description, then, of a new phenomenon can automatically ensure an absolutely reliable description of it. This unavoidable mismatch between language and reality calls for guesswork as to how the results of our experiments and observations may best be described so as to fit logically with the corpus of existing language. These guesses are our theories (postulates, hypotheses, etc.). These theories suggest further observations to make and what experiments to conduct, serving to enhance our knowledge of phenomena and thereby match our language of physics as closely as possible to that of reality (logos).

Since there is no certainty in theoretical guesswork, it is as likely that a theory will inhibit our understanding of phenomena as that it will advance it. A theory may take on a life of its own in competition with and perhaps in conflict with reality. Revered and defended for its own sake, a theory may well constitute a barrier to progress. We must, then, be as sparing as possible, not only with our theories but also with our provisional namings of what may appear to us as real entities. Names have logical connotations and these may very well create fantasies. The sheer familiarity of such names as 'electron', 'photon', 'quark', 'black holes', 'wormholes', 'superstrings', the 'Big Bang', etc. — especially when featured in the media — can make us forget that just because these entities exist in language they don't necessarily exist in nature, far less with all their connotational paraphernalia.

Plainly, then, a properly conducted physics must keep its theories to an absolute minimum — to zero, ideally. With all theoretical intervention removed, phenomena may 'speak for themselves'. In that event, the language of science and that of nature (logos) would be as one. True physics language would then be simply 'telling it as it is'. This, of course, is not physics as currently known, which has become heavily over-weighted in favour of theory. Theories are, essentially, fictions or fantasies. All true physics should therefore be aimed at removing these wherever possible, rather than conserving them for purely academic or sentimental purposes. Nothing less than a complete overhaul of current physics language can achieve this de-fantasising of modern physics. This is the aim of the group of philosopher-scientists called POAMS (see www.poams.org). Employing the language-analysis methods of Socrates and Wittgenstein, POAMS recommends a culling of physics theories, fantasies and mystifications whose removal from language is long overdue.

Viv Pope

Proof that the Constant c is Not a Speed

N. Vivian Pope & Anthony D. Osborne — 2005

Abstract

This paper argues that the constant $c$ is not a speed. The paper began as the subject for discussion at a seminar, held on June 7th 2005, in Penclawdd, Swansea, as part of the Normal Realist Interest Group series.

A common belief is that 'Electricity' is a 'flow of electrons' measured in amps, analogous to gallons of water flowing along a pipe.1 However, in electrical conduction along a wire, no electrons actually 'flow' in that way. Rather, what is ineptly called the 'flow' is a general drift of localised shifts in the orbits of electrons around their atomic nuclei in the direction of the so-called 'current' with none of the electrons travelling all the way. In this sense, the word 'current' is inappropriate, since there is no flow of electrons in the manner analogous to that of the continuous flow of water molecules in what is truly called a 'current'. What actually occurs is a series of incipient, limited local movements of electrons in which the electrons travel at very small speeds of only millimetres per second2 over distances of no more than about 0.0000007 metres (about the distance of ten atoms). In this case, what is said to 'travel' around the circuit are not electrons as such but their disembodied charge measured in coulombs. It is this charge, conveyed in an altogether disembodied way, that is alleged to 'travel' around the circuit at the 'constant speed $c$'. The driving force for these disembodied charges is supplied by the battery, and it is called the electromotive force (popularly known as e.m.f.). Measured in volts, this e.m.f. is defined as 'the characteristic of any energy source capable of driving electric charge around a circuit.' (Enc. Brit. 2001, CD) Let us assume, then, that it is this disembodied charge, in ultimate units of a 16 million billionths of a coulomb (the amount of charge ascribed to a single electron) which is said to 'travel' at the 'speed $c$'. First, however, let us examine what is commonly known as 'speed'. This, of course, is the rate of change of distance in time, a notion which explicitly involves the idea of an object travelling. A typical commonplace example of this is as follows.

The Road Circuit Analogy

[Figure 1: A road-test circuit with a swing bridge over a canal, checkpoint at A, compound at B.]

In Figure 1 is a road-test circuit along which commercial road vehicles, manufactured at A, travel from the exit at A around to the parking compound at B on the other side of the factory. At A there is a checkout recording the number of vehicles exiting the depot at short intervals. At the far end of the circuit is a swing-bridge which, when opened, allows canal barges to pass, and when closed allows passage for the vehicles from A into the compound at B.

Now it is characteristic of anything having what can truly be called a 'speed' – such as any one of those road vehicles, for instance – that it can set out from A and then travel freely as far as the swing-bridge at B, regardless of whether the bridge at that far-end of the circuit is open or closed. If the bridge is closed, then the vehicles pass over it and complete the circuit into the compound. If the bridge is open, then they cannot pass but get held up at that point. Meanwhile, at the checkout the count of vehicles exiting A into the circuit remains undiminished, regardless of whether the bridge is open or closed. Eventually, however, the steady stream of vehicles produced at A creates, at the open bridge B, a traffic hold-up which backs up to fill the road to its maximum capacity as far as the entry point A. The road is then choc-a-bloc. Nothing moves. Everything is at a standstill. The count of vehicles passing the checkout is zero.

The Electrical Circuit

But now let us replace that road circuit with a simple electrical circuit, as in Figure 2, below. This consists of a loop of electrical wire, a battery with a switch to one side of the battery and any chosen kind of electrical detector on the other.3

[Figure 2: A simple electrical circuit with battery, conductor, detector at A, and switch at B.]

Now electrical engineers know that:

i) When the switch at B is in the open ('off') position, no flow of energy whatsoever is detected at A, and that a flow of energy will be detected when and only when that switch at B is in the closed ('on') position.

It is to be noted that when the switch at B is open (off) there is no detectable flow of anything analogous to that of the vehicles passing the checkout at A and filling up the road. Regardless of whether the detector leads are connected to the negative or the positive side of the battery, absolutely nothing is recorded by the instrument. Nor, when that switch is open at B, is there anything analogous to the build-up of traffic and backing up of vehicles behind those which cannot pass at that point. Despite the capacity of the circuit to accommodate the flow, with the switch open at B, the detector at A reveals nothing passing into the conductor. The circuit remains completely empty until the switch at B is closed.

Now this is an observable fact, well known to electrical engineers, and it can be confirmed by anyone in a simple experiment with easily available bits of apparatus. However, in contrast to this fact, this next statement is no more than a postulate, namely:

ii) The speed of the conduction of the electricity (the electrical charge) around the circuit when the switch is closed is the constant speed $c$. This is approximately 300,000,000 metres per second, which implies that the conduction takes about 3.3 nanoseconds to traverse every metre of the circuit.

This is, of course, the same constant $c$ that appears in Einstein's Second Postulate of his Special Theory of Relativity, namely, that 'the speed of light in vacuo is a constant, $c$, for all observers in all states of relative motion or rest.' However, in view of the simple experiment just described, these two propositions, i) and ii) plainly contradict each other. For the notion of 'speed' to make any sense in the electrical case, the conduction must start, as in the road example, from one pole of the battery, travel around the circuit, like those vehicles, and finish at the opposite pole. However, if we assume, as in ii), that the current 'travels' from A through the detector at that point and on around the circuit at 'speed $c$' towards B, then that implies that the flow from A has to be detected at A before it reaches the switch at B. But this creates a mystery, because the current would have to 'know' that the switch at B is closed, before setting out at A. In other words, it implies that the effect, detected at A, of the closing of the switch at B would precede that event by 3.3 nanoseconds for every metre length of the conductor – bearing in mind that the circuit may be any number of metres long. This implies a reversal of the natural order of causality, and hence reveals the absurdity of the assumption that $c$ is a 'speed'.

However, things that have what are truly called 'velocity' don't work in that altogether mysterious way. But as soon as that 'velocity' interpretation of $c$ is abandoned, the situation makes perfect sense. For instance, the POAMS version of the relativistic time-formula is:4

t_R = \sqrt{(s/c)^2 + t^2} \quad \text{(1)}

in which $c$ is not a 'speed' but simply what Bondi calls a dimensional distance-time conversion factor. The formula then tells us that in the case of $c$, as soon as the switch at B is closed, every quantum of the energy that 'starts out' from A arrives instantly at B; that is, in the proper-time (quantum time) $t = 0$ in the formula. The same formula then shows that when $t = 0$, the relative time $t_R$ is the observationally delayed time $s/c$. This means that within the observer locale (see Figure 2) the detector at A gives its reading after the closing of the switch at B, by an interval which is the length $s$ of the circuit in metres divided by $c$. This puts the effect after the cause, hence avoiding the absurdity of reversed causality.

For instance, if the switch at B were used as a Morse code sender, then, depending on the length of the circuit in metres, the signal would arrive at A in the usual, time-delayed way. However, despite the signal appearing to have this 'velocity' in all experimental measurements of $c$, that 'speed' is not a speed but a pure ratio of conventional measuring-units of metres and seconds. For instance, if $c$ is interpreted as a 'velocity', then we have the absurdity that the 'velocity' of the current around the circuit is in a direction opposite to that of the signal. Otherwise, as a pure constant, this non-velocity interpretation of $c$ as expressed in the formula, satisfies perfectly the requirements of both Relativity and Quantum Theory according to which, when the proper time (quantum time) $t$ of the contact between A and B is instantaneous (i.e., $t = 0$), the observer-time (relative time) $t_R$ is the delayed time $s/c$.

What Does This Demonstration Prove?

To answer that, let us ask ourselves what it is that is measured in the circuit as 'current'. As already argued, it cannot be a continuous flow of electrons. So what else can that 'current' be but a measure of pure energy, the same as is measured in the form of light between A and B in space. This is at the constant distance-time rate $c$, which, as we have seen, cannot be the 'velocity' of anything either corporeal or quasi-corporeal. What the demonstration proves, then, is no more nor less than what logic tells us, as a consequence of both the law of energy conservation and the 2nd Law of Thermodynamics, that neither in the case of electric current or light-conduction can the energy 'start out' from a source if there is nowhere for it to be received. That, quite simply, is what the electric circuit example proves.

Philosophical Repercussions

But, surely, someone will say, the fact that the dimensions of $c$ are those of distance divided by time makes it a speed by definition. However, that is not true. The fact that 'All velocities are distances divided by time' does not logically imply 'All distances divided by time are velocities' any more than 'All rats are animals' implies 'All animals are rats'.

What, then, is the significance of this proof for the conduct of physics? It is that the interpretation of $c$, whether as a speed or a dimensional conversion factor, is philosophically vital. The difference in interpretation marks the point of departure for two altogether incommensurable paradigm approaches to physical science. In the 'speed' interpretation we can have no immediate knowledge of physical reality, due to what is called 'Einstein separation' between the object and our perceptions of it. In the 'conversion factor' interpretation of $c$ we have direct information of things as they actually are, not as they may be thought of as existing at some metaphysical remove and mediated to us by light 'travelling in space'. This restores to phenomenalism or observational realism (Normal Realism) its original status as the most fundamental approach to physical science, as opposed to the ultra, or metaphysical 'realism' of a science based on the 'Einstein separation' implied by his idiosyncratic and unnecessary interpretation of $c$ as a 'speed'.

Removal of the Einstein separation between the object and our scientific observations of it also solves the age-old, seemingly insoluble, philosophical problem of how to rejoin the split between the 'two realities', called dualism, that was introduced by Descartes. With light interpreted as the information, based on quantum elements that are common to the object as it is and as it is perceived, that schizophrenic split-mindedness between the 'two realisms' is removed, as much in Physics as in Philosophy.

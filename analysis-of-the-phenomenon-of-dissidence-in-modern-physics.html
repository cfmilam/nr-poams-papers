This, of course, is what, since Einstein, has come to be called 'relativistic time-dilation', any evidences of which hard-line Rationalists are bound to deny because, of course, it confounds any absolutistic conception of a universal space and time' There is, however, a third alternative which avoids falling into these polar extremes of Empiricism and Rationalism.

This third way is a Rational Empiricism, which has been called Normal Realism. Like Empiricism, Normal Realism relates all scientific knowledge to just one observational standpoint at a time, But also, like Rationalism, it takes natural laws and their associated equations as sgnrfying absolutely, or universally.

Such a synthesis of Empiricism and Rationalism was attempted by Kant, in his famous Critique of Pure Reason. However, whereas Kant sought to make Pure Reason an innate, or a priori set of intuitions' programmed into every human being at birth (l.e., psychologically a priori), Normal Realism ascribes that same Pure Reason to Nature in the round, of which man is seen as no more than a finite part.

This makes Reason no longer psychologically or subjectively a priori but a posteriori; tJ:rr;t is, something we discover in nature (l.e., empirically) not something we impose upon it. Normal Realism, then, restores to science the original Greek cofilmonsense view of Nature being, in itself logical, that is, in the sense of being a logas, which is the task of the sciences (the various -ologies such as'geology','biology','palaeontology' ... and so on) to uncover.

Logic, in the meaning given to this word by Aristotle, is then simply our evolved way of representing this natural logos tn our characteristically human language of words and other signs and symbols. Reason as we know it, therefore, is a priori, not in the God's-eye-view sense of the Rationalists, nor in the psychological or subjective sense of the Empiricists, but in aa a posteriori sense - in Kant's nomenclature, 's5mthetic a priori'.

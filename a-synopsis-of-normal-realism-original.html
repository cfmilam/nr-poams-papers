A Synopsis of Normal Realism (Original)

Neville Vivian Pope — July 18th, 1978

1. Perception

A common view of perception is that it is a psychological reconstruction of an external reality, built out of bits of information mediated in the form of energy (mechanical, chemical, electromagnetic etc.) which is conveyed to and throughout the nervous system of the percipient by processes of this same energetic kind.

2. The

Physical World The external reality which thus underlies perception is then conceived as physical or material, as opposed to psychological or mental. It is defined in terms basically of the dimensions length, mass and time, of which other physical quantities and qualities are derivatives (e.g. velocity is length divided by time, mass multiplied by velocity is momentum, etc.) and there is no other quantity or quality which is not fully expressible in such terms.

3. The

Psychological World In the psychological realm, on the other hand, there are all sorts of qualities and quantities which cannot be defined in these purely physical terms. This gives us the subjective, or evaluative, as opposed to the objective and factual, characteristics which mark off our experience of the physical world from that physical world itself.

4. Psycho-Physical

Dualism The physical and psychological worlds, so described, are mutually exclusive. That is to say, no physical quality as such is directly perceived, but only indirectly in terms of psychological (that is, subjective and evaluative) representations, while no subjective quality as such exists in the purely physical world. Even where the two realms are directly connected (that is, where the physical body is that of the percipient himself) the physical and psychological manifestations of the events concerned are radically different.

5. Physical

Phenomena The bits of information which are the evaluative aspects of physical energies are usually referred to as sensory stimuli. The pictures of the world which we form in perception are psychological wholes (impressions, ideas, conceptions etc.) created by a combination of physical and non-physical agencies. The physical factors include physiological considerations like that of the layout of nervepaths and the positioning of nerve-endings, things like the strength, intensity, frequency and kind of physical stimulation etc. The non-physical agencies include things like personal predispositions, social attitudes, language, logical and mathematical calculation, aesthetic and moral evaluation etc. The psychological wholes built in this way form the continuum of physical phenomena which is the world of ordinary and scientific experience – something which is always part subjective and psychological and part physical and objective.

6. Objectivity

The community, or consensus, of experience that we commonly refer to as objectivity is assured, on this view, by common factors in the underlying physical situation and also (on some views) by certain common, innate predispositions towards perception shared by members of the community. Without such common factors, either of a physical, psychological or cultural nature, it is assumed, no truly objective measure of experience is possible.

I. Science

7. Classical

Science Now the standard set for the physical aspect of this theory of perception was, originally, that of the synthesis called Newtonian or classical science. According to that science, the energies which characterise physical interactions are ultimately mechanical, consisting of contiguous interactions (collisions) between bodies like 'atoms' and other mass-corpuscles. Since Newton, however, this purely mechanical science has been supplemented by field-electrodynamics in which, in addition to mechanical energies there are also energies of a 'pure' kind, called 'electromagnetic' energies, consisting of non-contiguous interactions between electrically and magnetically charged bodies without the necessity of mediating particles. The upper limit on the speed of this kind of 'electromagnetic' interaction is thus equivalent to the value of the elegromagnetic constant in vacuo, commonly called the 'velocity of light in space'. This is approximately three hundred million metres per second.

8. The 'Universe

of Nature' Characteristic of this standard, classical, view of physical science is the conception of the physical world as an absolute space (extended void or field) which is an autonomous whole. That is to say, it is a universe which is comprehensive as such independently of the value-systems supplied by psychology (see section 5). This (tacitly or otherwise) endows the physical realm with parapsychological characteristics. Some scientists, like Albert Einstein and Sir James Jeans for instance, quite unselfconsciously ascribe to this underlying physical reality the characteristics of a God. Others, seeking to prevent science from becoming embroiled in religious and theological controversies, prefer to use the agnostic term 'nature' to signify the barest essentials of this para-psychological characteristic.

9. The 'Space'

of Nature Typical also of this standard, classical, view, is the assumption that the events of which this universe (or 'nature') consists are, in this same para-psychological sense, immediately (instantaneously) comprehended – as, for instance, by whatever ineffable spirit, god, daemon or parapsyche that is implicit in the concept of 'nature'. This leads, in classical science, to the conception of 'space' or pure physical extension (vacuum or plenum, as the case may be).

10. The 'Time'

of Nature The physical extension, or space, thus postulated is then assumed to endure, or process, in an absolute universal, or 'cosmical' time, such as might be recorded by the same ubiquitous 'nature'. The physical manifestation of this process consists in changes in the distribution of energy, which is defined in terms ultimately of the motions of material corpuscles (e.g. atoms and molecules, electrons and protons, etc.). As (cosmical) time advances, the spatial positions of these corpuscles change in relation to one another and in relation to the universal field. The distance covered in the time defines the velocity of the motion, the change in velocity per unit time defines the acceleration, and the product of this and the mass of the body concerned is the measure of force required to produce that acceleration. The product of this force and the distance through which it operates is then the energy whose release on a statistical or thermodynamical scale, and conveyed hither and thither by 'electromagnetic radiation and diffusion', gives the ageing of the universe.

11. Special

Relativity Now the transition from Newtonian, or classical, physics to the physical science of Einstein (Relativity Physics) begins with the introduction of the electromagnetic constant $c$ into physical definitions and formulae (see section 7). This constant has the dimensions of velocity (that is, length divided by time) and is commonly identified as the 'velocity of light in space'. Characteristic of the new physical science is a changed model of external reality, in which all energies (even mechanical energies) are governed by this constant $c$. All velocities thus become asymptotal at the limit $c$, the 'velocity of light', which experiment shows to be the same for all observers irrespective of their conditions of motion relative to one another. This means that in order to give a mass a uniform acceleration, a progressively greater force has to be applied. This signifies, in effect, a gradual transfer of energy from the role of producer of motion to producer of mass as the speed of a body is increased relatively to the observer. The fact that energy can be turned into mass, and vice versa, is thus one of the central discoveries of Special Relativity, in which sense it is markedly different from classical science.

12. Photons

Relativity differs also in that the limit on causal or physical interaction between bodies, across space, is thus set at c, the so-called 'velocity of light in vacuo '. The motions of all mass-corpuscles are either below or at that limit, those which travel at the limit being packets of pure (electromagnetic) energy ($E = mc^2$) called 'photons', travelling at approximately three hundred million metres per second. All energy is now obtained at the expense of mass and all matter at the expense of energy. All material particles are therefore, theoretically, 'photonic', that is, they can be 'cashed out' entirely into pure light-energy by multiplying their mass by the constant $c$².

13. General

Relativity Some of the more highly sophisticated models of 'nature', conceived under the heading of General Relativity (Einstein's later work) therefore reject the old 'corpuscles-in-a-void' interpretation of underlying nature in favour of a cosmical continuum or field. In this view, not only bodies and what we ordinarily conceive as their motions, but also the forces which cause these motions (e.g. gravitation) become localisations, or geometrical distortions, of a universal field-continuum, producing mechanical phenomena in the way that the irregularities in the groove of a gramophone record are made to produce (reproduce) a symphony. In this highly metaphysical interpretation, not only the space but also the light that travels across it – as well as the time it takes to travel – are all part and parcel of the same comprehensive whole called 'the universe'. Physical phenomena are then relative 'cross-sections' or 'slices' of this universal whole which are made and which advance in accordance with the particular orientation of the observer. Physically, (metaphysically?) however the motions of bodies and light-rays are conceived as four-dimensional 'geodesics', in the spatio-temporal aspect of which neither the beginning nor the end of the motion are distinguishable from the middle. The para-psychological implications of this 'universe of space-time' are, of course, the same as in the classical view, except that the whole, or continuum, comprehended under the term 'nature' now contains not only the whole of cosmical geography but also the whole of cosmical history and futurity.

14. Significance

of Relativity According to many writers, the changes in thinking,which Relativity has brought about, have been profound. The view which will be expressed here, however, is that these changes have not been nearly so profound as these writers would have us believe. Perception is still the same psychological reconstruction of reality, built out of bits of information supplied in the form of energy, as on the classical view. True, that energy, in all of these sense-categories – even that of touch - is uniformly and fundamentally electromagnetic, and is therefore subject to the limitations imposed by $c$. No physical interaction, then, can take place except within the limits of c, the so-called 'velocity of light in vacuo, which is the same for all observers in all conditions of relative motion.

15. Observer-Dependence

The external world which underlies perception, however, is still physical, as opposed to psychological or mental, and it is still defined in terms of the basic dimensions mass, length and time, of which all other physical quantities and qualities are derivatives. Also, there is no quality that signifies which is not fully expressible in these terms. The only difference, here, is that external space and time have now to be conceived not as separate and independent dimensions but as dimensions which are intimately related by the constant $c$. In other words, in any system of reference the ultimate standard of length is the distance that light travels in a unit of time, and the ultimate standard of time is the time taken for light to travel the standard unit of length. Since the measures length, time and mass are now dependent on the velocity of light, and since the velocity of light is constant for all observers independently of relative motion, then length, time and mass are now observer-dependent, differing from observer to observer as these move relatively to one another. This extends to mass and time the perspective variations which are familiar to the artist and photographer.

16. Insignificance

of Relativity The effect of this 'Relativistic Revolution' in the scientific conception of space, time and mass, profound though it has been in the purely practical or technological sphere, is fairly trivial, philosophically speaking, since it signifies little alteration to the central conception of the duality, in this particular theory of perception, between the physical and the psychological worlds. Those relative 'perspective variations' in the measures length, time and mass, which extend to all derivative quantities and qualities, as well as the general concept of 'observer-dependence', might suggest that Relativity provides some fundamentally new insight into the relationship of the percipient to the physical world. This suggestion, however, is illusory. Relativity is a theory conceived securely within the classical framework of psycho-physical dualism, where it is expressly and eminently a theory of physics, not psychology. For those who are not expressly concerned with physics, therefore, Einstein's highly 'revolutionary' theory seems to be something which can be easily and safely ignored. What does it matter whether physics is dominated by Einstein or Newton if you are not a physicist?

II. The Humanities

17. Conventionalism

On this 'common view' of perception, then, it scarcely signifies whether you are a classical Newtonian scientist or a sophisticated modern Relativist. The core conception is the same, namely that of psycho-dualism. And if the purely technological advantages arising out of' this traditionally split-minded approach to things have been considerable, the moral and intellectual results have been disastrous. The root of this essentially philosophical problem lies, of course, in the lurking realisation that in the ultimate analysis the 'physical world' that forms the common bond of objectivity between men's ideas (see section 6) is itself no more than just another of those ideas – or, at best, a convention. What we customarily describe as the 'physical universe' is, in other words, not something which exists apart from perception and which can be known immediately, as by some divine transaction, and then compared (or contrasted) with our perceptions of it. The question of the 'relation between the physical world and the psychological world' can be nothing more, in the end, than that of the relation between one set of ideas and another.

18. Scepticism

This becomes more obvious as old and trusted conventions fail and have to be replaced by new theories of an ad hoc and generally incoherent kind – like that, for instance, of Relativity, which has become a by-word for obscurity among the general populace. As theories and interpretations of theories proliferate the consensus as to what constitutes the 'underlying world of cold, hard, objective fact' is gradually replaced by a rash of subjectivism, which manifests itself to most people as a 'loss of commonsense'. Those who do not suffer the discomforts of this loss at first hand suffer them at second hand, third hand, and so on, so that the altruistic conception of community breaks down, by degrees, into subjectivism, scepticism, solipsism and nihilism – manifest among non-intellectuals simply as a vague and general feeling of frustration and despair.

20. The

Libertarians For some intellectuals the situation offers, on the other hand, a heaven-sent opportunity for unbridled speculation, and for indulging in pointless (but often mercenary) feats of intellectual brilliance of the kind enjoyed by the sophists of Ancient Greece. Some people therefore, inevitably, will be as happy with such a proliferation as others are desperately confused. When all ideas seem to be true, then, to the ordinary man, none seem to be true. With such a clamour of ideas on all hands the concept of knowledge and the custodianship of knowledge is brought into contempt – and with it, of course, the concept of education. Think of a growing and increasingly complex society with a dwindling concern for education and authority and there you have what is, surely, a veritable formula for social disaster.

20. The

Totalitarians In order to recover respect for knowledge and officialdom some seek, by totalitarian means of one kind or another, to 'bring the system under control' through the enforced 're-establishment of discipline'. This is done, invaraibly, by invoking (naively, of course) the old-style appeal to 'cold, hard, uncompromising fact'. Others of similar sentiment, and only marginally less naïve, borrow from the popular prestige of 'practical science' to prop up what they regard as the failing methods of education. Education may then be seen as a discipline akin to science in ministering to some fundamentally deterministic 'underlying nature' of man, who is consequently reduced to a mechanical, or else, statistical, process. All his distressing manifestations of individuality, or 'intellectuality' are then ruled out, or 'educated out', as spurious or 'deviant'.

21. Political

Polarisation None of this conditioning can be more than minimally effective, of course, because the discipline imposed is purely arbitrary and extraneous, and there are always freethinkers who will obey the selfimposed discipline of logical reason in preference to any externally imposed arbitrary regime. These will, inevitably, see all such 'education' as no more than a masquerade; and with the arrogance of knowing that they are in some definite sense wiser than their masters, but with no more to guide them than the vaguest notions of 'human freedom', these have little source of satisfaction other than in becoming rebels or 'subversives' – people who are often much clearer as to what they are 'against' than as to what they are 'for'. Society thus polarises, by mutual reaction, into an intellectual anarchy on the one hand (which may express itself as vandalism, terrorism or any other action/inaction born of some philosophy of despair or self-indulgence) and, on the other hand, the die-hard, 'antipermissive', generally non-intellectual 'fascism' of those who would seek to keep these 'anarchists' in order.

22. Need

for a New, Non-Political Approach to the Sceptical Problem Words like 'fascist', 'anarchist', etc. are so full of emotional content nowadays, unfortunately, that they shine out like battle-beacons. Used incautiously, in the darkness of so much political conflict, even from a position of neutrality, they are bound to get you shot at from some direction or another. The intentions of this analysis are, nevertheless, expressly non-political. What is required, it seems, is not some political 'tightening up of standards', or 'softening up of attitudes', nor any new sociological, educational or political theory that will simply add coals to the existing conflagration. Nor is there required some new and even more sophisticated insight into 'the universe' or the relationship between it and the mind of man. What seems to be required is some altogether radical, semantical approach to the whole question of psycho-physical dualism, the assumption of which appears to be standard in conventional science right up to and including modern times, and of which the non-philosophical person is usually no more aware than the fit of his own skin.

III. Normal Realism

23. Perception:

A New Approach All this leads us to consider the possibility of a radical alternative to the traditional or 'standard' theory of perception which includes, as we have seen, both Newtonian science and the science of Einstein. There are, perhaps, all sorts of other alternatives which anyone is, no doubt, free to explore in the interests of liberality. What defines the particular enterprise called phi, however, (see below) is its aim to explore just one of these (possibly many) radical alternatives. The name which identifies this logical alternative is Normal Realism.

24. The

Organisation Called Phi The pledge made by the members of phi, therefore, is that of commitment (at least whilst operating in the name of the Company) to the basic tenets of this particular enterprise. That is to say, the pledge is to adopt and to argue as consistently as possible in accordance with the basic axioms and established theorems of the system of Normal Realism. Only in this way, that is, by developing the system whilst preserving its logical consistency and distinctiveness, can a theory of perception be evolved that is clearly comparable with and contrastable to that of existing convention. To do anything different would be to render the exercise pointless – unless, of course, it should lead to some other, clearly divergent, alternative which would have to be classified and distingushed in a similar and equally definitive fashion.

25. Not

a Dogma Normal Realism is therefore not a dogma; but to be a Normal Realist is to submit to a discipline, which involves familiarising oneself with the full range of basic presuppositions and with their method of logical evolution. Discussion will then consist of proposing and analysing logical developments of the system so devised, in accordance with its stated terms of reference. The set of basic axioms, or presuppositions, plus any theorems logically established therefrom, is called the system of Normal Realism, and the constitution of the company called Phi Philosophical Enterprises Ltd. is aimed at developing and promoting this system whilst preserving it from dilution in terms of foreign (incompatible) precepts and prejudices. Where these may be discovered in the course of argument they are signified by being declared unconstitutional. Those who understand the essentially philosophical and non-political purpose of this exercise (see section 22) will appreciate our call for logical and mathematical stringency and will not mistake it as an appeal for political fascism.

26. Removal

of Psycho-Physical Dualism In section 17 a 'disastrous' problem was said to be presented by the dualistic distinction made by classical science (and carried through, as we have seen, into modern science) between the physical world and the psychological world. The root of this problem was said to consist in 'the lurking realisation that in the ultimate analysis the “physical world” that forms the common bond of objectivity between man's ideas is, itself, no more than just another of these ideas – or, at best, a convention.' Whereas most philosophers in the past have taken this as a sceptical conclusion which is at all costs to be avoided, Normal Realism takes it as an inevitability, and regards all attempts to avoid it as motivated by nothing more than mystical or metaphysical superstition.

27. Language

So far as Normal Realism is concerned, things like 'the physical world', 'the psychological world', 'matter', 'mind', 'self', 'non-self', etc. are all, in the last analysis, uniformly human conceptions, or conventions of the language in terms of which we customarily formulate or communicate our experience. That is the starting-point which Normal Realism shares with most other forms of modern linguistic philosophy.

28. Classical

Misconceptions about Language The fact that this standpoint will be abhorrent to most traditionally-minded philosophers – especially those connected with science – is no more than a tautology, since it signifies an acceptance of something that tradition has steadfastly avoided. Such an abhorrence however is based, we claim, on a long-established misconception of the function of language in its supposed 'relation to reality'.

29. Logos

A most common presupposition about language (born, of course, of the incipient dualism in our culture) is that as soon as people begin to neglect the 'cold hard facts' of experience and become preoccupied with 'mere language' the result can be nothing but confusion. It will be, of course, if insufficient attention is given to the logical structure of language. But most people would say that in any case, even the most grammatical, well-formed and logically consistent language is no more than noise compared with 'the logic of nature'. And here we have, in its most pristine form, the dualism, the para-psychism, that is implicit in our classical scientific tradition. The root of the word 'logic' is the Greek word logos, meaning 'word' or 'discourse'. By attributing to 'nature' a logos, therefore, we implicitly copy the Greeks,who found it impossible to conceive of a universe, as such, whose character, as a whole, was not an idea embodied in a cosmical language (logos).

30. Forms

or Wholes In what sense, then, can things like 'nature', 'universe' etc. be what they are – that is, forms, or wholes – without a language which gives them this formal character? The answer, of course, is: in no sense. The only alternatives, therefore, are either to accept the extreme Nominalist conclusion that all such terms are completely senseless – which straightaway turns all subsequent argument into gibberish – or else to accept them as significant items of language, which is the only basis on which any sensible discussion can continue.

31. Conventionality

of the Logos The next point to note is that all assumptions about a 'cosmical language', or 'language of nature' ('logic of nature', or logos) are inevitably framed first of all in human language, and are therefore subject to the rules of human convention. Being finite and human, this is something from which (barring mystical appeals to superhuman agencies) we have absolutely no means of escape. Is the consequence, then, scepticism? The answer is no, and for the follwing reasons.

32. Refutation

of Scepticism For the sceptical conclusion to be true, words as such can have no practical significance – which straightaway nullifies the 'truth' of the sceptical conclusion. The sceptical conclusion of the insignificance of language therefore refutes itself. There is no way whatever in which it can be true.

33. The

Practicality of Language So words, do, inevitably, have practical signification. To deny it involves one in logical contradiction. And it is this practical character that words have in relation to one another that provides the dimension of practical efficiency in accordance with which it is sensible to compare languages and uses of language.

34. The

Substantial Characteristics of Words in Language For words to have this practical signification it is essential, of course, that they are not abstracted of content. To abstract the formal characteristics of a word – like its sound or its status as a bare logical (classificatory) symbol – is to deprive it of its true meaning. By a word therefore, we mean a word as it occurs in language, as applied basically to experience, from which it gains whatever sustenance it requires and without which it is bare noise. Another way of putting this would be to say that words have practical meaning only as delineations of phenomena (in any of the conventional categories like physics, psychology, history, language, fiction, or whatever) – or, if not, then as delineations of delineations of phenomena (that is, second, third, fourth etc. orders of generality)

35. Uniformity

of Convention In the practical emphasis on language, no fundamental (dualistic) division occurs between such things as the 'physical' and 'psychological' uses of language. The only relation between 'physical' and 'psychological' that we can now sensibly discuss is the logical relation between two different sets of practical conventions in the use of language. Nor is this difference necessary or indelible. With the continual evolution of language (if this be allowed to continue) other, more logically efficient, ways of speaking may develop in such a way as to erase these arbitrary differences.

36. Evolution

of a Practical Ontology And the same thing goes, of course, for any other such conventional schism in language. The advantage of having ontology under the control of convention is that there are no a priori limits to the making of adjustments. The only criterion to be observed is that the result of any adjustment represents a maximum of both logical and practical efficiency – and sufficiency.

37. No 'Mere

Language' The language thus described is no 'mere language' which is to be set aside, or abstracted, from what that language signifies and in contrast with which it is 'just words'. The language and what it signifies are inseparable. Without the language you cannot speak about – far less compare and contrast with language – what that language customarily signifies. You cannot even discuss the possibilities of anything lying 'outside and beyond' customary language without the use of customary language; so even what is unknown is characterised by (conventional gaps in ) the language of convention.

38. No

Sceptical Consequences But that, as we say, by no means implies scepticism or subjectivism, nor solipsism, nor nihlism, so long as the practical dimension of language is kept firmly in mind as the criterion of meaning and efficiency. The bogey of scepticism is no more than a figment of the metaphysical imagination, having no place in practical philosophy.

39. Conventional

Physical Dimensions But what about the physical world, the world of classical and modern science, of Newton and Einstein? Is this now to be regarded as no more than a convention of language? Well, why not? What else could it be on the analysis given? What are the basic 'physical dimensions' but classificatory conventions? What are 'space', 'time', 'mass', 'charge', 'force', etc. but words – words having, of course, that specific and inseparable practical content that we have just now described? In a world (if such could be imagined) which is devoid of man or any creature of comparable awareness, are there such things as 'space', 'time', 'mass', 'force', etc., as entities in their own right? How could there be? What would 'space' be, for instance, relative to no point in particular? What would 'time' be relative to no particular clock? What would 'force' or 'energy' be relative to no particular agent? Trying to imagine such things we merely tie ourselves up in metaphysical knots.

40. A

Humane Science For those who do not either implicitly or explicitly assume that such things exist as items in the language of some ubiquitous deity, like God or Zeus, or some daemon like 'nature', the answer must be that all such things are delineations of the language of human experience. And what is it that we experience by the use of such language? The answer must be: that which language itself expresses. And there it must rest if we are to avoid metaphysical conundrums.

41. The

Question of Coherence But how, it will be asked, can a science of such conventions hang together? What can give it reality without an underlying 'physical universe', or 'world of hard and uncompromising fact', to give it substance? The answer which Normal Realism gives to this question is that what makes these conventions hang together is logic, which is the science of relations internal to language (as opposed to Physics, which purports to be the science of relations external to language). It is this same logical function which, according to Normal Realism, gives to this set of conventions their reality – so long as the logic is applied, as we have said (see section 34) to substantial uses of language (that is, uses from which the practical content has not been abstracted). A logically consistent language of practicality is then a coherent whole, answering to what is commonly called understanding.

42. The

Illogicality of Modern Physical Experience But one of the problems which modern scientists have encountered, it will be said, is that when you get far enough away from the ordinary everyday world of commonsense, you find that experience conforms less and less to such rules of logic; so that physicists, for instance, have been forced to abandon the criterion of logical coherence, or 'commonsense understanding', and adopt instead a principle of eclecticism, or 'complementarity' – which simply means the acceptance of views which do not logically hang together. Now that is undoubtedly true; but the fault, in that case, may lie with those conventions we insist on applying. Perhaps those dimensional conventions should now be revised and some new dimensional alternatives tried that might succeed in maintaining overall coherence. Could it be that our linguistic evolution has led us up a blind alley? Then perhaps it is time we looked to some other, a-historical line of development. Since it is all a matter of convention the answer to the problem presented by the incoherence of modern science is, surely to become – well, unconventional!

43. Reconstitution

of Commonsense What Normal Realism seeks to do, therefore, is to look at present scientific conventions dispassionately in the light of pure logic. Our ability to do any really useful measure of logical rethinking, in the past, has no doubt been inhibited by the customary notion that there is but one, unique and unchangeable, reality – the one we usually associate with 'physics' – and that when this logically falls apart there is no recourse but to accept confusion as a 'universal principle'. The advantage, however, of seeing 'physics' as no more than a set of conventions puts a much more liberal and less sceptical interpretation on things. Where conventions do not hang together we simply make adjustments until they do. There is no reason, indeed, why such readjustments should not be of the most radical and revolutionary kind, so long as these succeed in closing the old logical gaps without opening up too many new ones.

44. The

Root of Modern Scientific Reality Such, in effect, is the method of Normal Realism, where the 'norm' refers to the norm of commonsense logic, extended in the method to the whole of (both ordinary and scientific) experience. What we find, in applying this method, is that there happens to be a certain small group of neo-classical precepts which are responsible for the logical incoherence of modern science. These are all concerned with what is known as 'electro-magnetic theory', which is ordinarily understood as the principles governing the generation, emission and conveyance through space, of 'photons', the physical agency of light and radio transmission. The concepts underlying this technology, we find, are the ones which have perplexed commonsense and turned physical science into an esotery. These concepts, we also find, are logically redundant: they can be dispensed with, in other words, as completely unnecessary.

45. The 'Speed

of Light in Space' The mental readjustments required to accommodate the alternative that is offered in the name of Normal Realism are, it seems, quite difficult to make. This is due, apparently, to the persistence of a whole habitual syndrome of precepts that have evolved around the convention of 'light travelling in space'. So deeply established is this convention that to deny it will seem to most people – nonscientists notwithstanding – the very height of absurdity.

46. The

Dimensional Constant c But you can't make omelettes without breaking eggs; you cannot, obviously, experiment with unconventionality without entertaining 'absurdities'. The key to making the mental readjustment that Normal Realism requires lies in the question of the adequacy or otherwise of our present dimensional conventions. The essential difference between Normal Realism and extant Relativity (to which it is, in purely mathematical respects, identical) is that whereas in Relativity c signifies the 'velocity of light in physical (metaphysical) space', in Normal Realism it signifies no more than a constant relating two previously independent sets of conventional units (e.g., miles or metres of length and seconds of time). Instead of the 'velocity of light' in 'external space' (or 'space-time'), therefore, we have a constant $c$, internal to the conventional language of dimensions, which does all that is required for the interpretation of scientific experience without presenting any of the usual riddles involving 'races with light-rays' (the standard esotery of initiation for any student of Relativity).

47. Scientific

Implications The scientific implications of this 'purely dimensional' (or linguistic) approach to Physical phenomena are far from trivial, and are available to the student in the form of of the phi publication ISBN 0-9503790-2-6, which is a folded diagram, or 'flow-chart' of logical and mathematical deduction, entitled Towards a New Economy of Fundamental Physical Concepts.** This demonstrates not only how the conventional formulae for Relativity can be deduced from our purely phenomenological premises, but also certain formulae which are of central significance to Quantum Theory.

48. Non-Scientific

Implications This, however, by no means implies that Normal Realism is of relevance only to physicists or 'specialists in mathematics'. A culture which one might imagine to have developed on the basis of Normal Realism rather than in the tradition of Newton and Einstein would be very different both morally and practically, as well as philosophically, from today's industrial society. It would be no less – perhaps it would be even more – industrious and scientific. But certainly it could never be so aimless.

What is Logic?

N. Vivian Pope

Far too many people, when they argue or try to reason, use the words 'logic', or 'logical' without having a clue as to what those words mean. So, what does 'logic' mean? Look at it this way. Suppose I tell you two things:

1. The biscuits are in the tin, and
2. The tin is in the cupboard.

Have I now told you just two things? No, I've told you three things. The third is:

3. The biscuits are in the cupboard.

Now I didn't actually say that, but I told you it, all the same. The third bit of information was in the first two bits. If you can see that then you can do logic, because logic is the art of seeing what is in a statement.

Of course, any idiot can see what information is in those two statements about biscuit tins and cupboards. But in the arguments and debates we have in everyday life, extracting information in that way from statements that are not quite so simple can be extremely difficult, to the extent that even the best 'logicians' (people who make the extraction of information from statements their speciality) can sometimes fail.

How, then, do they fail? When this happens, as it invariably does in some discussion or other, it is because some bit of information has been smuggled undetected into one or the other of their statements. We all know how easy it is, in everyday conversations, to jump to conclusions, making connections between bits of information which aren't really there; assuming, for example, that because something is true in one context it must also be true in another. Our human language is notoriously full of ambiguities that can trip us up in this way. Some of these are well-known. For instance, the ambiguity between 'A implies B' and 'B implies A'. From the fact that 'If it rains the pitch will be wet' it does not follow that 'If the pitch is wet it has been raining.' Another well-known fallacy is that from the fact that two things are positively correlated (i.e., both increase, or both decrease, together) it follows that one is the cause of the other. It may be, for instance, that both are effects of some other, undiscovered common factor.

There are all sorts of fallacies of this kind and it would take forever to list them all. But, in principle, logic works by showing how information that is contained in one set of statements must be contained in all logically connected statements. This applies to statements of physics, medicine, history, law, politics, morals, theology, etc. — in fact to all statements in which language is used to describe and prescribe.

There are, of course, different kinds of logic. First, there is what is called deductive logic (or just 'deduction'), which is the one I've been describing. This goes from premises to conclusions. If the premises are true, then the conclusions have to be true also (as in the biscuit-tin example). In this, there is a 'cast-iron' guarantee that if the original premises are true then the conclusion is true also.

Second, there is inductive logic (or just 'induction'), which is the logic of probability or likelihood. It is based on the principle that what has been experienced to happen regularly in the past is likely to happen regularly in the future. It is on this principle that scientific 'laws' are based. Laws, in science, are generalisations over our past experience which, if the experience is fair and regular enough, give us a good idea of what to expect in future. It is also the principle on which legal precedent is based, making the legal process a kind of social 'science'. As the old saying goes: 'Experience is a hard school, but fools will learn in no other.' Of course, experience is not a perfect guarantee against future misadventure, so any induction, whether in science or in law, always needs to be open to revision. The only complete guarantee of truth there is, is in deduction, and then only provided that the premises are absolutely true.

Then there are the specialised forms of logic used in mathematics, computing and so on, where the 'language' is so refined that the ambiguities of ordinary language, as above mentioned, are greatly reduced. But this is at the cost of generality. That is, 'mathematical logic', for example, cannot cope with the more general and interesting matters (right and wrong, for instance) that ordinary conversational language can cope with, at least to some extent. As a car mechanic once said to me: 'Give me a micrometer for the size of a ball-bearing; but give me a bit of string if you want a rough idea of the size of the pitch!'

Logic is involved in all human discourse and debate and, of course, in all physical science. The great thing to remember, about logic, is that while it can never tell you what a right answer is, it can certainly tell you what it isn't. As Sherlock Holmes says: 'Eliminate the impossible and whatever remains, however improbable, must be the truth.' This elimination of errors (or 'falsehoods'), which brings us ever closer to truth by a process of 'successive approximation', was known to the Ancient Greeks as the Socratic Dialogue — and, in our own times, as 'the method of trial-and-error', the Scientific Method. ■

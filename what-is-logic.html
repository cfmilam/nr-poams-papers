WHAT IS LOGIC? Far too many people, when they argue or try to reason, use the words ‘logic’, or ‘logical’ without having a clue as to what those words mean. So, what does ‘logic’ it mean? Look at it this way. Suppose I tell you two things:

1. The biscuits are in the tin, and 2.

The tin is in the cupboard.

Have I now told you just two things? No, I’ve told you three things. The third is,

3. The biscuits are in the cupboard. But I never actually said that, did I? Did I say that the biscuits are in the cupboard? Certainly not! Yet that conclusion is inescapable, given the truth of those first two statements. Now suppose I were to tell you these two things: i) All electrons are fermions. and ii) All fermions are particles with spin-half, I’ll bet you don’t know what a ‘fermion’ is or what ‘spin-half’ means. But it doesn’t matter whether you do know or you don’t. From just these two things you have now been told, if you accept that I am not lying, then you will know that I have also told you a third thing, which I have not actually stated but is nevertheless true, that iii)

All electrons are particles with spin-half.

Now how do you know that? For the same reason that you know, from what I have told you, that the biscuits are in the cupboard. So you see, it doesn’t matter whether or not you know what the words mean in these arguments - for arguments is what they technically are – he fact us that if you are assured that those first two statements are true, then you know that the third statement, in both arguments, has to be true also. So, since it doesn’t matter what these words mean, you might as well just replace them with plain Xs and Y’s. It makes no difference. So both of these above arguments, as well as any similar ones, can be represented by the following: All X are Y. and Therefore

The way it works is this. First, write ‘FOOT’ in the four cells along the row at the foot of the diagram and the abbreviation FT in the two middle cells on the top row. Then, repeat these letters diagonally into the remaining empty squares in the directions shown by the arrows, as in the game of drafts. Next, write the easily remembered sequence of letters AEIO down the left-hand side of the square. These letters signify A for ‘affirmative’, E for ‘negative, I for ‘particular affirmative’, and O for ‘particular negative’. Each of these letters stands for the premise of any particular argument. Then, across the top write same sequence of letters AEIO for the conclusions of the argument. The letters T, F and O in the cells are located, down and across in the same way as with a crossword puzzle. These letters T and F stand for the truth or falsity of the conclusion and O stands for undetermined (that is, not decidedly true or false). You now have, in effect, a mnemonic calculator. A valid standard-form categorical syllogism must contain exactly three terms, each of which is used in the same sense throughout the syllogism. Furthermore, the middle term may not appear in the conclusion. The general rules governing categorical syllogisms can be divided into two sets: the first set refers to the terms of the categorical syllogism, while the second set refers to the quantity of a sentence (rules of distribution) and to the quality of a sentence (whether it is affirmative or negative). The general rules regarding terms are:    

(1) Only three terms may appear in the syllogism, each of which is used in the same sense throughout the argument. (2) Neither the major nor minor term may be a universal in the conclusion, if it was only a particular term in the premises. (3) The middle term may not appear in the conclusion. (4) The middle term must be distributed at least once in the premises, that is, used as a universal

The general rules of quantity and quality are:   



(5) If both premises are affirmative, the conclusion must also be affirmative. (6) Both premises may not be negative; one at least must be affirmative. (7) If either premise is negative, the conclusion must be negative; if one of the premises is a particular proposition, the conclusion must be a particular proposition. (8) No conclusion can be drawn from two particular premises; one at least must be a universal proposition.

An understanding of and familiarization with the above rules will enable you to judge the validity of any categorical syllogism you may encounter. The Eight General Rules are a direct product of the very idea and nature of the categorical syllogism. To facilitate understanding them and perceive their necessity as rules of correct thinking, each one will be explained and proved separately. We will use various symbols to represent the parts of the categorical syllogism: M = middle term S = minor term (subject of conclusion) P = major term (predicate of conclusion) pM = particular (undistributed) middle term uM = universal (distributed) middle term pS = particular minor term uS = universal minor term pP = particular major term uP = universal major term + = an affirmative proposition - = a negative proposition

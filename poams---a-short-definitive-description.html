POAMS - A Short Definitive Description

N. Vivian Pope

Why is modern physics so abstruse? Could it be that it got off on the wrong foot, right from the start? Incredible? Why? Physics is a creation of men, not gods. Radical mistakes have happened before. First it was the misconception that the earth is flat.

Then it was that the earth is the centre of the universe. In both cases, it took honest, academically uncommitted people like Columbus and Copernicus to set science straight. Are we due for another commonsense revolution of that kind? More than just a few people think so.

The present state of modern physics has been compared to that of the Ptolemaic academicians, whose highly mathematical account of planetary motion no-one but they could understand. But it was wrong, fundamentally wrong, on pure commonsense grounds, as Copernicus spectacularly demonstrated.

Could physics still be wrong? What logical guarantee is there against that eventuality? With this in mind, a small, multidisciplinary group of thinkers have tried to anticipate the sort of turn the next revolution might logically take. After due discussion and deliberation among the group (called POAMS, for reasons to be explained) it is offered for serious consideration that the next ‘Copernican revolution’ is to recognise that we have no logical need to think of light as something travelling in space.

It is proposed that this ancient idea (from Empedocles, who conceived light as a ‘streaming substance’) is the root source of the confusions that have remained central to physics ever since Römer discovered that all observational distances are also times in the constant ratio of units c.

Now a velocity of motion is, by definition, distance divided by time. However, this does not logically imply that all distances divided by times are velocities. To assume that the constant ratio c, of three hundred thousand kilometres to the second is necessarily a velocity is therefore a fallacy and, if held to unquestioningly, a dogma by definition [].

POAMS suggests that it was this peculiarly dogmatic interpretation of c that led physics, via the thinking of Michelson and Morley, into the confusion that now reigns in the continuing conflict between relativistic and quantum physics. The logical alternative presented by POAMS is to think of light (in all its manifestations, from deep infrared to far-ultraviolet and gamma) as simply what we see (or detect with observational instruments).

What we perceive as space and time are then in the light, not the other way around. In light we see the positions of things, their distances from each other and from ourselves. We also see those changes in distance with time that we call motion; and from the way objects move in relation to one another we derive all our basic physical measures such as speed, force, mass, momentum, energy and so on.

In fact, in light as we see (or detect) it, there is all the information, in ultimate quantum bits, that physics needs for a logically coherent account of physical substance, in all its visual, tactile and other observable aspects. This also includes the time-delay in our observations of distant matter that Römer, Michelson, et al measured, without having

to think of the informational quanta as travelling across space from those bits of matter to one another and to us as observers. In this way, POAMS dispenses with the whole rationale of in vacuo light-mediation by ‘ethers’, ‘fields’ and so on. By the same token, it also dispenses with ‘gravitation’ as an invisible, in vacuo ‘force’ in favour of a purely phenomenological description of how masses behave in overall-conserved and balanced systems of orbital and spin angular momentum.

POAMS thereby demonstrates, in detail, the advantages of a logically cleaned-up (Ockham clean-shaven) version of relativity which incorporates quanta, not as space-travelling ‘photons’ but as the ultimately irreducible bits of information into which the all-embracing, omnidirectional ‘hologram’ we know as ‘light’ – the whole world of matter, space and time, in effect – analyses.

In short, in POAMS we deal with nature ‘direct from the manufacturer’, so to speak, in the truly observer-based way that Ernst Mach pioneered, and from which his Philosophy student, Einstein, took his ‘relativistic’ inspiration. Unfortunately, in his mentor’s view, Einstein’s ‘relativism’ or relationism was flawed.

The flaw, as we have analysed it was Einstein’s unnecessary interpretation of light as an incidental mediator of information rather than being that information itself. Removing that flaw means cutting out the traditional ‘middle man’ called ‘light-velocity’ which no-one ever sees and which physicists describe in a purely pragmatic jargon of ‘electrodynamics’ with which only the initiated can claim familiarity.

With this esotery of the invisible behaviour of light in vacuo removed, what remains is simply light as we see it, an informational manifold – a Language, in effect, which is the basis of all other languages, the language of nature, known to the ancient Greeks as logos.

The mathematical, physical and philosophical implications of this ‘flip-side’ way of thinking about light have been explored in depth over nearly fifty years, following a short correspondence with Einstein just prior to his death in 1954. ‘POAMS’ is the acronym recently coined for the group, by one of its members, Jon Blay, from the Pope Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis, around which the group has formed.

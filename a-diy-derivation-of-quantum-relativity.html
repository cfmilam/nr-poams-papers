ABSTRACT

This paper explores the possibility of bypassing some recalcitrant difficulties in Einsteinian relativity by taking a new and independent look at the basic phenomena. This reveals that Einstein's interpretation of the dimensional constant c as 'the velocity of light in vacuo' is not logically obligatory. As the supposed limit on all physical interaction, this 'velocity' interpretation of c has sustained, for almost a century, the myth of 'Einstein separation', by which overall-conserved orbital angular momentum systems are separated into isolated masses moving about independently of one another in an 'inertial space'. Moreover, it conflicts with Einstein's own General Theory with its ad hoc attempt to reconnect those masses within an underlying 'space-time-continuum'. By removing the 'light-velocity' interpretation of c we automatically remove both that conflict and the mystery of how bodies interact at a distance (the chronic 'EPR' paradox).

Our alternative, which is concurrent with that of Herman Bondi, is to interpret c simply as a constant relating traditional units of metres and seconds in the measuring of motion, making the natural ratio of length-units to time-units unity. This gives to velocity-gradients an automatically geometrical significance, from which relativistic time-dilation follows without all the notorious theoretical complexity. It also reinstates the sort of instantaneous interconnections between bodies in space that are required by the conservation law which forbids any delayed action between bodies in automatically paired and balanced angular momentum relations.

In this way, not only is the undeniable essence of Einstein's time-dilation retained but so, also, is the distance-time factor c in distant observation, without these in any way contradicting the instantaneity of quantum angular momentum correlation. ■

1. Introduction

The question of whether the physical world is, in itself, as it appears to us and as we describe it in our science of physics has never been fully resolved. The nearest we get to a scientific answer is in Einstein's Theory of Relativity, which has produced practical advantages for modern science but which, in some ways, continues to mystify us. One of these mysteries is that of how the defining attributes of physical objects, such as their mass, length-dimensions and duration, can be said to exist relatively to us in a remote, secondhand way due to an absolute separation incurred by the delayed-action intermediary called 'light'. This is not the sort of problem which can be solved by purely mathematical arguments and/or experimentation, which is why these approaches continue to prove ineffectual in seeking to assuage growing worries relating to 'Einstein' and 'Relativity'.

Of course, in any reassessment of Einstein's contribution to natural philosophy, the accepted mathematical arguments and practical proofs have to be retained. But that still leaves room for much logical and conceptual readjustment. One such avenue of ahistorical reasoning is as follows.

2. Basic relativism

'When the butcher dies, we don't have to eat pork with the bristles on.'

The basic principle of relativism is that if what one person discovers about nature is true, then that truth has to be discoverable by anyone else, independently and in his own way. Otherwise, if that truth has to remain centred on just one person's experience and ideas, then it is assuredly not a natural truth but, at best, a theory.

From this it follows that if we cannot understand or cannot agree with what the experts tell us about Einstein's famous contribution to modern physics, then if there is a basis of truth in the theory of relativity we should be able to go back to basics and rediscover that truth for ourselves.

Let's begin, then, by laying aside all the usual tortuous stuff about 'Einstein's Theory of Relativity' and its associated mysteries as to how light travels at the same speed, not only relatively to everything and everyone in space but also to space (the vacuum) in itself. Instead, let us return to a completely naïve starting point in which all we know about light, let us suppose, is that it is the opposite of dark, and that in that optical-informational scenario (in astronomy, for instance), objects appear to be distributed in terms of distance from ourselves and one another, this phenomenon being based on comparative radial measures of their apparent size and brightness, and on angular measures such as perspective, parallax and so on.

Next, let us say we learn that light-intensity is not infinitely divisible but that it is a measure of numbers of irreducible twinkles, like the dot-events on a TV screen [1]. Now of course, no distance can be gauged from these individual twinkles, or light-quanta as such, because

In all other instances, the change in the position of the object, in whole statistical successions of quantum instants, is spatio-temporal in the ratio of distance-measure to time-measure s/t, which we call its velocity V. Let this, then, be our first postulate:

Postulate 1: Any change in the observed position of an object between one instant S and the next, in the dimension T, is a motion of that object relative to the observer. The measure of that motion is s/t = V, where s is the observational distance in metres in the dimension S and t is the time in seconds that the moving body registers in its own time during that change, as seen by the observer of the motion.

Time and motion, then, cannot be separated. There are no isolated and independent measures of space-as-such and time-as-such as classically imagined. There is only a naturally unified space-time, with those inseparable components of motion, distance and time. If, from the outset, by some miraculous coincidence, these had been measured in the same units, like the ordinary dimensions of length, breadth and depth, then Figure 1 would have been automatically geometrical. However, that – albeit perfectly understandable – historical oversight has now been corrected by the expedient of introducing a constant relating the two conventional sets of units. This constant, as we now know from observation and experiment, is c, which is 300,000,000 metres to the second [3].

This constant is, of course, familiar to just about everyone nowadays as the 'velocity of light in vacuo'. With a more fortunate choice of units at the start, c might have been unity, so that all current problems associated with that 'velocity' conception in the context of Einsteinian relativity would have been pre-empted. Indeed, the original discoverers of that ratio c were logically-minded men who were wary of calling it a 'velocity' because they knew that, strictly speaking, the fact that velocity is defined as distance-divided-by-time does not logically entail that all distances divided by time are velocities. Moreover, it is difficult to conceive how anything can have a velocity with respect to a vacuum. Besides, it is characteristic of a velocity that it refers to some object, whose velocity cannot be constant relative to observers in different states of relative motion, which, to our infinite confusion, the 'velocity c' is in Einstein's theory. So we shall stick to the original policy of caution followed by its discoverers, and others such as Herman Bondi, and take c simply as a constant; that is, as stating no more nor less than the plain, unembroidered fact that for all observers, every 300 million metres of distance between one body and another is also a second of time.

It must also be emphasised that since the same space-time relation pertains between observers as between any other bodies, the constant c holds not only for distances that are radial with respect to any single observer (e.g., oneself) but for all observers and all bodies

Let this, then, be our second postulate:

Postulate 2: In observational space, all distance-measures are also time-measures in the ratio, c, of 300 million metres to the second, which is the same for all observers in relative motion or rest.

From these two postulates we may now derive our DIY version of relativity. Since the distance travelled by a body, in the first postulate, is now, according to the second postulate, also a time in the ratio of units c, there is either added to or subtracted from that body's native time-processes as measured by the observer of its motion, a distance-time which is the travelling-distance of the body in metres divided by c. This is the Doppler effect, which applies to our purely dimensional, distance-time theory of light in the same way as to the standard wave-velocity theory.

3. Einstein explains the 'clock paradox'

Now this change in the time-rate of a body with motion is, it might be supposed, no more than 'apparent', because it affects only the time of the object relatively to the observer of the motion, not relatively to the object itself. This was the view which, as an amateur astronomer, the author of this paper had originally accepted. But then he received something of a shock. Like all others of his acquaintance, he had unquestioningly assumed that those distant bodies he had viewed in the telescope all endured at the same rate in a single, overall universal time. Then, one evening, with his father, he attended a lecture on Einstein's Theory of Relativity in which they were told that there is no overall real time of the sort that was classically assumed but that all real-time ageing was different for differently moving objects and observers.

This seemed to our author complete nonsense, so he wrote to Einstein expressing his puzzlement. If motion is truly relative, he said, with the motion of a twin A relative to twin B being exactly symmetrical with that of twin B relative to twin A, then how could those twins, at the end of an out-and-back motion, possibly end up time-dilated relatively to each other (i.e., being, from then on, each younger than the other)? Surely, he argued, at the end of the relative out-and-back motion the respective positive and negative Doppler-effects would exactly cancel, leaving everything, in the end, the same.

Your question concerns the wellknown"clock-paradoxon" and is very well formulated. The answer is: Two observers B₁ and B₂, each at rest in an inertial system, can only encounter each other once.

To make possible a second encounter one of the two observers, f.i. B₁, has to reverse his speed. He is at rest, first, in an inertial system S₁ and later in a (third) inertial system S₄⁺ which has another time as J₁. This reversal distinguishes observer B₁ in comparison to observer B, who is constantly at rest relative to the same coordinate system S₁. This is the cause of the dis-symmetry of the result with respect to the two observers B₁ and B₂.

I feel sure that this short remark will suffice for you.

Sincerely yours, A. Einstein.

From his acquaintance with astronomy he already knew something about spectrometers.. As he had conceived it, prior to his introduction to 'Einstein', the period of an atom in an object is signified observationally by the position of its spectral line in relation to the spectrum of the object as a whole in the way that a musical note is identified from where it occurs with respect to the others in a musical scale (or, in more modern terms, like a line in an electronic bar-code). As he had understood it, Doppler-shifts in the spectra of distant objects reveal the difference between the 'real' periods of their component atoms, as they occur in the time-frames of the objects themselves, and the 'apparent' periods of those same atoms in the time-frame of the observer, by which those bodies signal the speeds at which they are travelling away from or towards him.

Let there, then, be two bodies, twins A and B, travelling relatively to each other and let the times of the physical, biological and all other processes in their respective reference-frames, including their own living-times, be counted in periods measured by some standard line in the spectrum. From the postulates so far, we gather that as A and B travel away from one another those spectrographic periods will be slowed, or reddened, in the spectrograph of each as seen by the other; and as they move towards one another those periods will, conversely, be quickened, or blued. But now the vital question arises: when A and B move apart and then return together, is the change from the away-motion to the towards-motion the same for both bodies?

In conventional Relativity, as Einstein et al. had presented it, there is only one way in which this can be answered. This is by each observer watching the other's image change from getting smaller to getting bigger in his field of vision, accompanied by the switch from reddened to blued periods in their spectroscopic observations of each other.

Let us suppose, then, that having blasted-off from B and coasted till he judges that B is at a sufficient distance away in his (A's) telescope, A reverses his rocket motor and watches his telescope-image of B change immediately from getting smaller to getting larger, together with the corresponding change from reddened to blued period-counts in his spectroscope. Now, obviously, although he (A) will see this reversal of the relative motion between himself and B immediately, in accordance with Postulate 2, that same reversal event at A will not be seen immediately by B, but only after a lapse of time which is the distance s separating him from that event divided by the constant c. At the end, then, of the out-and-back motion, therefore, in their respective logs of the spectral periods, the proportion of reddened (slowed) to blued (quickened) periods of A counted by B can in no way be the same as that of B counted by A. That is to say, there are fewer slowed and more quickened periods of B counted by A, and more slowed than quickened periods of A counted by B.

Now, plainly, there is no way in which that relative motion can Doppler-shift the spectrum of an object's own processes, chemical, biological or whatever, relatively to itself. Nor is there any way in which, at the end of that out-and-back motion, with its one-sided reversing action,

4. Subsequent doubts about Einstein's explanation

In this author's numerous publications, in common with orthodox relativists in general, this was the standpoint that he maintained for many years. Nevertheless, he never found it completely satisfactory. He eventually came to realise that this was because the situation Einstein had projected in his letter was in a definite sense unreal. No bodies in free spatial motion ever move relatively to one another in that simple, straight-line, out-and-back ('inertial') way Einstein had described. They all move in orbits or trajectories in which every single body is automatically paired and balanced with each and every other in an overall-conserved angular momentum relation. The nearest we ever get to that straight-line 'inertial' motion of the 'twins' example is along a short length of the straightest section of some highly elongated and narrowed ellipse, like that of a comet orbiting the sun. It might therefore be supposed that in a more realistic version of the 'twins' example, since the two bodies, A and B, are reciprocally balanced (i.e., simultaneously) on the ends of their common angular momentum moment-arm, the change in motion from outward to inward, at the furthest reach of the orbit's radial component, is always symmetrical; that is, it takes place at the same instant for both. In that case, since the reversals are symmetrical, there can be no relativistic differences in ageing between bodies in free orbital motion. In other words, with everything simultaneously balanced in an angular momentum nexus there can be no time-dilation of the sort that Einsteinian relativity describes.

Must we, then, either reject the law of angular momentum conservation to preserve time-dilation or, as so many 'dissidents' prefer nowadays, retain the conservation condition and dump the time-dilation? The logical answer is, neither. Although the two bodies are simultaneously balanced at every stage in their motion around their common centre of mass, the time registered by each of the bodies between any two such instants measured at the centre of mass may differ due to the bodies having different (tangential) velocities V relative to that

5. Restoring the universal centre-of-mass rest-frame

A more realistic rendering, then, of Einstein's B₁ and B₂ out-and-back example (see Figure 2) is to consider whether, in these actual orbital angular momentum terms, the asymmetry which, as Einstein says, distinguishes the one observer in comparison with the other, is that of the motions of the two observers, not with reference to each other but with respect to their common mass-centre. Thus, the reversal of the out-and-back component of an orbital motion that decides which of the orbiting pair ends-up time-distended and which is time-contracted will be a matter of which of them, according to the difference in their masses, travels fastest and which travels slowest relative to their common centre of motion. This means that the only instance in which, at the end of their out-and-back motion, the two orbiting observers can have aged the same relatively to each other is when their masses are equal. In all other cases, they will be relativistically time-dilated or time-contracted in the way Einstein describes.

In this veritably 'Copernican' way, the datum frame of reference for distinguishing which of the observers can be considered 'at rest' is transferred from Einstein's arbitrarily chosen 'inertial observer' to the objective reference-frame of the whole angular momentum nexus, or universe. This does not affect c because in this thesis c is a dimensional constant of observation, not a motion, which is why the time-dilational consequence of observational motion remains unaffected. So without compromising, in the least, the mathematical practicality of Einstein's theory (see below), this leads us to conclude, in agreement with so many 'dissidents' (i.e., anti-relativists who are mostly of the Dingle 'London Group' persuasion [6]), that there exists, behind all the different relativistic time-variations, a basic, uniquely objective time-standard of reference. So far as our DIY thesis is concerned, this is the time of the common centre of motion, the centre of mass coordinate system (CMCS) of all bodies involved. It fits, exactly, the description of the datum time for the 'ageing-rate of the universe' as envisaged by classical commonsense and encapsulated in the science of Galileo and Newton.

A small but significant modification, without affecting in the least the accepted time-dilational consequences of Einstein's Special theory, is to assume that the events he describes in his letter are all referred to a more fundamental, fourth frame, which is that of the CMCS of all other bodies in the universe, as assumed by Mach. This is implicit, anyway, in Einstein's description of time-dilation, according to which, given a normal distribution of bodies all travelling at different speeds relatively to one another, there has to be, among them, if only as a statistic, some least-dilated time-frame of them all, the frame with respect to which all others are dilated to different extents. Indeed, it is absurd to imagine that in a universe of bodies with disparate motions, there is no such uniquely undilated time-frame forming the datum-frame for the reference of all motions and consequent ageing-rates.

6. Summary

It seems, then, that by commonsense reasoning we rediscover for ourselves, in a natural do-it-yourself way, the essence of Einsteinian special relativity but which, instead of opposing instantaneous and delayed distant interaction, combines the two without any suggestion of contradiction. This, in effect, reconciles the instantaneous-correlation requirement of quantum physics with relativistic delayed-action. This is the sort of synthesis which has long been anticipated but which has so far proved impossible due to the 'separability' requirement inherent in the Einsteinian theory.

If our reasoning is correct, the causes of that failure are twofold. One is Einstein's arbitrary and unnecessary interpretation of the constant ratio of our historical distance-units to time-units as the 'speed of light in vacuo', and the other is the resulting separation of observational from physical reality by that supposed inexorable 'Einstein separation'.[9] In his General Theory, Einstein attempted to restore that lost connectedness by the contrivance of a metaphysical 'space-time continuum'. In our DIY version of relativity this connectedness is achieved simply by not losing it in the first place and by making it the logical basis of a truly phenomenological relativism instead of an esoteric afterthought. ■

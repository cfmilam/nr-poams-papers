For the major part of the twentieth century, physical science, which is supposed to be our most basic contact with nature, has been held in a state of suspended irresolution between two completely opposite approaches to the subject. The one is what might be called the anolag approach; the other is what might be distinguished as the digital approach. Typical of the analog way of thinking, which is traditional, is to assume that on the most fundamental levels of physics everything takes place in much the same way as it does in our ordinary, mechanical-engineering types of experience, in which it seems that bodies exist and move continuously in a continuous space and time - that is to say, without fundamental breaks or jumps. By contrast, in the digttal approach there is no such physical continuity. All physical processes are quantised - that is to S&y, they consist, fundamentally, of' discrete and indeterminate events. These events take place in fundamental jumps, like those that make up the pictures on a video or cinema screen.

Plainly, then, nothing could be further apart than these two approaches to physics. The trouble is that social convention, which builds itself around long-established ideas, can suffer no sudden and radical switch from the classical, analog way of thinking into the digital mode that modern quantum physics requires. Despite the logic that points the need for such a radical conceptual shift, all sorts of compromises have had to be made between the old view and the new, in order to preserve some semblance of cohesion. The state we are in at present is therefore like that of the time when our ideas were suspended between thinking of the earth as flat to having to think of it as round, or between thinking of it as the centre of the universe to thinking of it as just one tiny rock orbiting a not particularly significant star.

I

In such situations, any systematic attempt to revise the old approach in favour of the new, stresses convention and is therefore bound to be unpopular, even when all the observational and experimental evidences point to its being necessary. On the other hand, it is plain that scientific ideas cannot be allowed to congeal forever into whatever shape they just happen to take at the time of their inception. That way, science would stultify into a mere mess of theories and ideas without overall coherence, with the result that progress in understanding would grind to a halt. There has to be, then, some provision in science for thinkers to be able to project their minds beyond the purely circumstantial here-and-now to contemplate wholesale conceptual restructuring, where necessary without being subjected to the sorts of blocking tactics that 'heretics'such as Copernicus and Galileo had to face. This kind of enterprise mustbe allowed to continue, even at the risk of creating social upheaval - or, more likely, of being smothered for appearing to threaten the same. These are very real problems, as anyone soon discovers in choosing to follow this exploratory course, of 'trafficking with the devil', as Galileo was warned. Why, then, would anyone want to embark on such a disastrous course? The only answer is, out of sheer philosophical curiosirT. If one discovers that there happens to be a some other, perfectly logical but radically different and altogether simpler way of interpreting, for instance, what we traditionally conceive as the'speed of lighf, then, surely, philosophical curiosity directs that that different way of thinking should be explored. And it has been systematically explored. In one of our British universities there is an ongoing project based on that very discovery, namely, that it is by no means logically obligatory to interpret the evidences of what we customarily call the 'speed of light' in that mechanistic, or analog sort of way, and that the interpretation can be recast into a consistently digital fotm.

But, of course, there is no making omelettes without breaking eggs. The 'eggs' in this case, are the orthodox theories of Quantum Mechanics and Special Relativity, and the 'omelette' is a synthesis of these two logically disparate theses within a new quantum-relativistic (or 'digital') paradigm. The basis of this synthesis is what might be called a 'cinematic', or movie-type conception of light-interaction between distance-separated bodies. This rejects the customary analog conception of light as conducted through the vacuum at a finite and constant speed c relative not only to the vacuum but also to everything in it (which has only to be stated to reveal how nonsensical it is). Thus, whereas the distance and time components of c are customarily conceived as signiflring a'velocity', in the paradigm they are conceived, instead, as expressing no more than a constant relation between units, such as metres and seconds, that have been traditionally allocated to the measuring of optical distances and times. The fact that c is the same for all observers in all states of relative motion and rest is then simply a tautology instead of the mystery it seemed to present in the experiments of Michelson and Morley. This leads, with no loss of practicality whatsoever, to a reconstruction of Einsteinian special relativity along lines that would have been approved by Einstein's tutor, Mach. t'l Not only does this massively simplifu the theory (see, e.g., 'The Relativrty Theory of Srthagoras', in this volume) by removing the 'Einstein separation' implied by Einstein's 'velocity of lightl interpretation of c, but also it reinstates that wholeness, or non-locality of mechanical systems that quantum physics requires and which is subsumed under the name'universe'.

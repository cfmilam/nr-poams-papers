PHILOSOPHY: AN OUTLINE OF THE DISCIPLINE AND ITS SUB-DISCIPLINES

In human society, unlike a society of social insects such as bees or ants, the responses of individuals to each other seems not to be governed by any clearly defined principle of the common good. This of course is because among men, unlike the social insects, there is no single time-proven instinctive attitude which automatically serves the best interests of the community as such and of the individuals comprising it. To a large extent this lack of a stereotyped or 'instinctive' response to environment gives a man an advantage over the other animals insofar as it enables him to come to terms with change in a way that is impossible for animals acting in terms of 'instincts'. This advantage however is offset by the extra burden that man bears in having almost constantly to think out his responses to situations of ever-increasing complexity. Unlike the bees therefore, man does not act in response to any single, unifying, natural imperative. Instead it falls to each of us individually, to a large extent, to act in such a way as we personally believe will secure our own best interests. But our freedom to act in this way also entails the ever-present possibility of failure — not only failure to achieve our chosen ends but also failure to choose wisely which ends we should set out to achieve. So whereas no question of wisdom, of praise or blame, etc., is relevant to the actions of animals like bees, who respond automatically or instinctively to circumstances, this is far from being the case with man. The concept of wisdom, of moral evaluation (praise or blame) for acting and contemplating action is something which comes with the human natural predicament. It is this peculiar necessity which man has in addition to his more basic animal drives and instincts — that is, this need to make wise decisions — which leads to things like technology, the sciences, political and moral theory, and, in the widest possible aspect, philosophy (which means, literally, the 'love of wisdom').

What distinguishes philosophy from other disciplines like technology, politics, etc., on the analysis just given, is that while each of these subjects is concerned to advance some particular human good, philosophy (traditionally at any rate) is concerned with human good as a whole. This means that philosophy maintains at all times a critical or evaluative attitude, not only towards the particular sciences and institutions but also towards itself.

In some cases the point of reference for 'the good' may be conceived in mystical terms, as some kind of divine ordinance. This mystical aspect of philosophy, however, is more properly termed religion, and it is only insofar as the concepts of 'the mystical', of 'faith', 'belief', etc., are logically analysable that they are the concern of philosophy as such. Discussions in this area are usually conducted under the heading of philosophy of religion.

Concepts of human goodness, however, are not always religious. In secular philosophy there have been many attempts to define 'the good'. Some philosophers, like Plato for instance, enchanted by their own ability to reason, have tended to think of human benefit in terms of the exaltation of reason at the expense of the ordinary sensual pleasures. Such philosophers sometimes called Rationalists, or Idealists, have often made recommendations for reforming society around a rationalistic concept of 'the good' which other men would reject as completely inhumane, whereas other philosophers, by reaction, have given just as unbalanced a consideration to the pursuit of the purely irrational or sensual pleasures, exalting to the same unjustifiable degree the concept of the 'Natural' — as though man's exercise of reason in the control of his passions and prejudices were somehow unnatural and unpleasurable. Those who, in this way, stress irrationality as the essence of the human constitution, may be called psychological naturalists. Other philosophers, again, would say that there is no such thing, on the individual level, as an essential 'human nature', rational or non-rational, that individualism is no more than a romantic ideal and that the only really natural unit, so far as man is concerned, is not the individual as such but the society of which he is a part: that a man's nature as an individual is entirely the product of social change, and that any claims that a man may make as an individual are illusory. Conflict between personal and communal interest are thus regarded as nothing but the manifestations of some kind of 'sickness' of which the individual, for his own sake and that of society, must be 'cured'. This kind of philosophy, sometimes called ethical naturalism, tends to conceive human society on an analogy with that of the social insects, like the bees or ants.

Political philosophy is thus, properly considered, a subsidiary of moral philosophy, for every political ideal is based either explicitly or implicitly upon some kind of moral assumption. In 'politics', therefore, we might expect to find the practical expression of some well-thought-out and matured moral philosophy. As often as not, however, what we call 'politics' is no more than the expression of some sentiment or dogma — or even, at its worst, pure personal self-interest or 'expediency'. In the context of philosophy, however, every political activity implies a moral standpoint which is connected in a definite and systematic way with some general body of assumptions made about individual and common good: that is, with some philosophical selection of human ends and means, considered the best (talent) of all the presented alternatives.

PHILOSOPHY OF SCIENCE

LOGIC

One of the main presuppositions underlying the pursuit of science seems to be that man in his 'natural' (i.e., primitive) state is not automatically in possession of the greatest good or benefit of which he is capable: that by systematically studying nature he can continually find ways of improving his natural physical condition. This presupposition is of course one of the things that is arguable under the heading of Moral Philosophy. Some scientists would claim, however, that science has nothing to do with morality. The only duty of the scientist, they would say, is to his particular subject: that is, to advance knowledge, not to ponder over the morality or otherwise of his discoveries. This viewpoint, however, like some of the political views mentioned in the previous section, is an unconscious moralising pronouncement, which should not be accepted without proper deliberation. Science cannot therefore claim moral absolution on such grounds without falling (as religion often does) into dogmatism. Again it might be objected that scientists do not in fact operate under this assumption of the beneficence of their discipline, that individual scientists are more often motivated not so much by any concern for 'the good of mankind' as by a concern for their own individual benefit, by natural curiosity or, perhaps, pure ambition. If so, whatever 'benefits' are bestowed upon mankind are, in general, no more than accidental; and scientific discovery, according to this view, may well prove detrimental to the good of man. The consideration of a moral control on science is thus bound to arise sooner or later even on this individualistic interpretation of the motivation of scientists.

But in any case, whether the pursuit of science be adjudged 'good' or 'evil': whether it be deemed 'moral' or 'immoral', it is evident, considering man's peculiar evolutionary predicament, that he will continually find ways of opening up doors in nature that must remain closed to the other animals, and to operate in ways which these other creatures find impossible. Man's range of perceptions thus extends at present from what he can see in the electron-microscope to what is revealed in the radio-telescope: from what he can read with a ruler to what he can read out on a computer. He can 'interfere with nature' on the levels of birth-control ('the pill'), biological engineering, spare-part surgery and living in space. All of these things require constant and careful readjustment in our philosophical understanding: of our physical and moral relationship to 'nature', to ourselves and to our fellows. Man characteristically 'plays with fire' and plucks at the forbidden 'tree of knowledge'. His consciousness of his consequent predicament is expressed in legends such as those of Prometheus and Adam. As a philosophical 'flea' on a cosmical 'griddle' he must make higher and higher leaps towards order or else expire in confusion.

What characterises philosophy, therefore, on the analysis given here, is the connectedness of ideas in general. However, not all philosophies are so systematic as to enable us to predict what will be a person's attitude in one field from statements which he makes in another. This is because human reason is not always consistent. In general, however, the business of philosophy is consistency. Philosophical discussion is therefore aimed at seeking out mistakes in reasoning: that is, testing for consistency, connexity and redundancy in any given view or concept-system. The main tool used for this purpose is called logic. This, briefly, is the science of knowing what statements have to be true for others to be false.

Often one encounters people who imagine that logic is something weird and wonderful, like some set of spells and incantations employed by magicians for turning men into robots, and therefore to be rejected by all fair-minded men in the interests of humanity. In fact, however, logic is nothing more vicious than a set of rules for making sense in language. All these rules say, basically, is that if you say that something 'A' is universally the case, then you cannot at the same time admit of any other particular instance of 'non-A' without having to retract your original universal statement. If I say to you 'All swans are white' and you show me something which I have to admit is a black (non-white) swan, you would naturally expect me to retract, or at least modify, my original statement. You would no doubt think me a very strange person if I insisted, having admitted that I had seen a black swan, that all swans are, nevertheless, white. You would think either that I was mad or that I had somehow made an improper use of the language. All that logic does is to carry this kind of consistency, which commonsense demands of those who communicate by means of the common language, into the more complex issues which are dealt with in philosophy. Logic is therefore never a mechanical substitute for human creativity, nor is it ever a barrier to the same. It is what puts the spine into creativity and imagination. It is not something malevolent or inhumane - something which is likely to 'take us over' or 'destroy us'. We have no more to fear from the rules of logic than from the rules of grammar or the principles of accountancy. Rather, we have much to fear from a fear of logic, for without logic there is little that can keep human guile under constraint.

Perhaps, then, we should expect the greatest amount of opposition to logic to come from those whose aim is to beguile us — or themselves — by their skill in the use (or, rather, misuse) of language. Not that this in itself is necessarily such a bad thing, but the most impressive display of linguistic

ability may do no more than conceal a complete lack of structure, or, at least, a structure so inherently unsound that it cannot happily bear inspection. A philosopher will be the last person to be impressed by the dazzling display of knowledge, or captivated by the high-sounding word or phrase. His aim is not to accumulate the maximum amount of educational glitter, like some intellectual magpie. His aim, rather, is to examine how various propositions relate to each other in language without giving rise to ambiguity and contradiction, and to make whatever adjustments might thus prove necessary in his own views and opinions.

Logic, therefore, simply tells us which of our ideas are compatible with each other and which are not. It never tells us what to think, but only that given that we think so-and-so then such-and-such automatically follows – or does not follow. There are philosophical problems connected with the use of logic, but for the most part the philosopher's use of logic is fairly simple and straightforward. If I say 'Philosophy should never tell people what they ought or ought not to do', then I am, quite obviously telling people what I say I 'ought not' to tell them, and the logician can tell me that I am quite straightforwardly contradicting myself. If I then persist in my view, in spite of the contradiction, the fault lies, certainly not in logic, as such, but in something else – possibly in my own personality, which seeks, perhaps, simply to be perverse. But perversity apart, in most philosophical contexts – unless we are dealing expressly with certain known problematic areas of logic – the issues are usually amenable to straightforward logical application.

Now some people might question the advantage of being so logical in one's philosophical outlook that every particular view is logically connected to every other particular view within the context of the whole. They might consider that a person with such a logical outlook would be too predictable, like some silly puppet, which can be placed under the will of anyone knowing how to pull the right strings. A far wiser and more interesting kind of outlook, they might suppose, would be one with so much 'flexibility', so much built-in deviousness and inconsistency (what is sometimes called 'animal cunning') that it can operate to its own advantage with little chance of outside anticipation and interference. Like all attitudes to life, however, this last one is based on a specific, albeit concealed, moral presupposition; and the moral presupposition in this case is the rather superficial one that one's own best interests are automatically served by seeking the immediate advantage – if necessary, placing other people at a disadvantage with respect to oneself. A deeper study of this presupposition, however (such as is made in Plato's Republic) reveals that it has scant justification, and that the confidence and trust that is born of rationality in one's dealings with one's friends and neighbours – and even enemies – may bring far greater and more permanent advantages for all than is obtainable through mere cunning. A man who thinks in this limited way, in any case, invariably succeeds in frustrating his own desired ends as well as those of his friends, and soon becomes the enemy of everyone – not least himself.

So vast is the scope of philosophy, however, that in order to fill out the full spectrum of philosophical sub-divisions one would need to mention just about every possible aspect of human intellectual activity. Many who object to philosophy as a subject, therefore, do so on the grounds that no man can be sufficiently expert in so many fields as to enable him to conform to the ideal of a philosopher; that the aims of the subject are therefore totally impracticable. Some critics will even say that to contemplate doing philosophy is, for the reasons stated, no more than the expression of a kind of megalomania.

Proper consideration reveals, however, that it is by no means necessary to know everything in order to know something, and it is certainly not necessary to know every detail about every subject in order to obtain philosophical understanding. True philosophical wisdom is manifest in a sense of proportion, to 'know what is best worth knowing and to do what is best worth doing'. To imagine that it is incumbent on the philosopher to have expert knowledge in everything is therefore sheer misunderstanding of what the subject entails. What the philosopher requires to know are not all the minutiae of the various disciplines but only those things which are philosophically significant. There may be discoveries in science, for instance, like that of the relativity of space and time, or the synthesising of viruses, etc., which have definite logical implications for philosophy. All the philosopher needs to know in such cases are the end-results of some particular field of research, as reported by the expert in that field. Philosophy thus implies a division of labour, the main factors and issues of philosophical concern being contributed by the various specialists. No-one who understands the aims of philosophy expects the philosopher nowadays to be, as it were, both head cook and bottle-washer in the kitchen of knowledge.

An allied misunderstanding, I should say, is to imagine that philosophy is – or should be – just sheer dispassionate, rational contemplation. True philosophy, like its scientific subsidiaries, involves evaluation, which is to say that it involves the wise selection of generally significant information. The aim of true philosophy is, in the end, that of the ultimate or long-term welfare of mankind, both as an individual and as a whole – which man, being a moral rather than an instinctive agent, cannot leave, like the bees, to circumstance but must secure for himself by the proper exercise of his distinguishing natural faculty, reason.

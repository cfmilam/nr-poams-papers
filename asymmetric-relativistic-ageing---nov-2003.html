Asymmetric Relativistic Ageing - Nov. 2003

N. Vivian Pope — 2003

Asymmetric Relativistic Ageing What distinguishes the ageing rates of the two observers in the standard relativistic ‘Twins’ example is the asymmetry of their respective experiences of the motion, that is, the acceleration that is felt and recorded by the one and not the other. In the example so far presented [see e-mail to Neal, et al., on the subject of ‘Variable c’, 31 October 2003 11:17, Attached files: ‘Clocks Everywhere’ and ‘Dogma of Temporal Non-Locality’], the two observers move apart in an approximately classical ‘inertial space’ – that is, in virtual straight-line trajectories relatively to each other. The two situations are perfectly symmetrical until one or the other of the observers takes some action to reverse the motion [see the Einstein letter attached to my e-mail to Jon of 05 November 2003 18:05]. It is this active observer who experiences the change in motion (i.e., by visceral and other effects) and who, as a result, when they meet up at the end of the out-and-back motion has aged less than the other. Now instead of that acceleration being felt at just that one point in an otherwise undisturbed ‘inertial’ motion, it might, conceivably, be felt continuously, as in some constrained out-and-back loop or curve. In this case, the effect should be the same as though the two legs of the motion were inertial with the acceleration concentrated to the single half-way point. It is therefore predictable that a curved out-and-back motion, in a constrained (i.e., accelerated) loop or curve, will be time-dilated relatively to an observer who experiences no such acceleration. Indeed, this asymmetric ageing is confirmed in observations of the half-lives of particles in particle-accelerators at places such as CERN. Those who observe this effect report that it takes place exactly in accordance with the relativistic time-dilation formula [see email from Tom Phipps, date and time.) It does not matter, then, that the trajectories in the ‘twins’ example are straight or curved. What matters is the distance – or, rather, the distance-time – separating the observers. It is the interposition of this distance-time, in seconds, into their respective observations of each other’s time-processes that distends, or dilates that time relatively to each other. The whole process is entirely kinematical, or geometrotemporal, the only effect of the forces of acceleration being to signal any change from one geometry to another in the manner described by Einstein (ibid.) and depicted in ‘The Clock Non-Paradox’ [see attachment to my e-mail to Jon of 05 November 2003 18:05] Now it has been pointed out by Neal [ref.] that in POAMS there is no truly straight-line motion, that all force-free motion-trajectories are automatically cyclic or orbital in accordance with an angular momentum formula which dispenses with the Newtonian concept of an in vacuo ‘gravitational force’ – also with in vacuo electrostatic and magnetostatic forces. Neal is therefore correct in observing that in POAMS all force-free motion is naturally curved. In these curves, there are no forces of acceleration involved, hence there is nothing to distinguish the asymmetries in ageing that those forces would otherwise signify. This means that in the situation Ron describes, in his e-mail of [ref.], where one clock moves freely around another in a circular orbit there can be (according to POAMS) no ageing asymmetry between

those two clocks. Hence, if such an asymmetry exists, as Ron claims it does, then it might seem that POAMS is refuted. However, Ron’s example is unreal, insofar as there can be no motion in such an isolated instance. The orbital motion Ron describes has to be with respect to something; and what else can that ‘something’ be in Ron’s example but a tacitly postulated ‘ether’, which is no part of POAMS. So far, then, as POAMS is concerned, without that postulated ‘ether’, the two clocks in that example remain relatively stationary on the ends of that fixed distance-time that Ron presents as a ‘circular radius’. And if motion is entirely relative – i.e., if there is no absolute determination of motion – without there being any change in that distance-time there can be no motion, hence no time-dilation of the sort that Ron claims for circular orbital motion. On the other hand, in POAMS there is something like an ether – or, at least a ‘preferred frame of reference’ as an ‘absolute’ reference for orbital motion. This is the frame of the overall angular momentum nexus which defines the orbital trajectories of bodies with respect to one another. (In this situation, the straight-line inertial motion of Newton’s First Law becomes an ideal case of an angular momentum $mvr$ with theoretically infinite r.) This means that in POAMS there may well be a situation in which one clock orbits another in the way Ron describes. For this to be feasible, the central clock would have to be of near-infinite mass, as compared with the orbiting clock whose mass would have to be suitably smaller and the circularity of whose orbit would be no more than approximate. Only under that condition can POAMS contemplate the situation that Ron describes in which, at the end of a fixed radius a clock orbiting a central clock can be regarded as moving, that is, with respect to the centre-of-mass of the whole angular momentum system. The question that now arises is: does this sort of ‘absolute’ motion entail timedilation? According to Ron, de facto it does. But how does that fit with POAMS? Where, in this situation is the change in, and interposition of, distance-time that causes time-dilation? Our answer is: in aberration. The earth orbits the sun almost in the manner of clocks in Ron’s example. And we know (pace Bradley) that this has the effect that Tom Phipps and I discussed in our e-mail exchanges between 12 th and 18th Oct., 2003 on the subject of ‘variable “c”.’ In that discussion it was agreed that without compromising the constancy of c, aberration entails relativistic changes in the observational distance-times of objects as seen in the telescope, depending on the angle of aberration. Now here is a bit of purely qualitative logic. (Look after the logic and the math will look after itself.) The difference between the so-called ‘Doppler’ effect, as applied to astronomical sources, and the effect discovered by Römer is one of name only. That is to say, they both deal in the same way with changes in the observational distance-times of sources in relative motion. Further, the effect of aberration, as discovered by Bradley, is, in essence, the same as that discovered by Römer, being simply an angular composition of Römer’s in-line vectors. This was proposed in an ATTACHMENT to my email to Tom, 21 Oct. 2003, subject: ‘variable “c”, to which Tom’s response, ( in an e-mail of 18 Oct 2003) was ‘I thought you would be pleased that our different viewpoints have led to the same result.’ Logically, then, wherever aberration is measured, so, implicitly, is the Doppler effect; and since it is the Doppler lengthenings and shortenings of the distance-time periods that are the basis of time-dilation, then the aberration that Bradley observed in the earth’s solar orbit must occasion time-dilations. And since time-dilation is always positive, regardless of speed and direction, in an orbit of the sort Ron describes the

time of the orbiting body is continually dilated relatively to the central (stationary) one. In other words, the duration of the orbiting body is continually slowed relatively to that of the observer who is (ex hypothesi) stationary with respect to the centre-ofbalance of the angular momentum nexus. Also, insofar as there is a measured diurnal aberration due to the earth’s spin, there must also be a slowing of the durations of bodies on the earth’s rotating surface with regard to the its centre.

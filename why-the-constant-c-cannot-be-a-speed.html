Why the Constant $c$ Cannot be a Speed

N. Vivian Pope & Anthony D. Osborne

Abstract

Herman Bondi and this author, in the 1950s, concurred that the constant $c$ in relativistic physics need not be interpreted as a speed, that all the standard consequences of Relativity can be deduced far more simply without Einstein's Second Postulate which interprets $c$ as the 'constant speed of light in vacuo'. This paper produces evidence, from some common knowledge of electrical engineering, that not only supports the view of the redundancy of that 'speed' interpretation of $c$ but also proves its logical invalidity.

The Nature of Electrical Conduction

A common belief is that 'Electricity' is a 'flow of electrons' measured in amps, analogous to gallons of water flowing along a pipe.[1] However, in electrical conduction along a wire, no electrons actually 'flow' in that way. Rather, what is ineptly called the 'flow' is a general drift of localised shifts in the orbits of electrons around their atomic nuclei in the direction of the so-called 'current' with none of the electrons travelling all the way. In this sense, the word 'current' is inappropriate, since there is no flow of electrons in the manner analogous to that of the continuous flow of water molecules in what is truly called a 'current'.

What actually occurs is a series of incipient, limited local movements of electrons in which the electrons travel at very small speeds of only millimetres per second[2] over distances of no more than about $0.0000007$ metres (about the distance of ten atoms). What is said to 'travel' around the circuit, therefore, are not electrons as such but their disembodied charge measured in coulombs. It is this charge, conveyed in an altogether disembodied way, that is alleged to 'travel' around the circuit at the 'constant speed $c$', which is the same as the alleged 'speed of light'. The driving force for these disembodied charges is an electromotive force (e.m.f.). Measured in volts, this is defined as 'the characteristic of any energy source capable of driving electric charge around a circuit.'

Let us assume, then, that what is said to 'travel' around the circuit at the 'speed $c$'[3] is this disembodied charge, in ultimate units of sixteen million billionths of a coulomb (the amount of charge ascribed to a single electron). First, then, let us examine what is commonly known as 'speed'. This, of course, is the rate of change of distance in time, a notion which explicitly involves the idea of an object travelling.

The Road Circuit Analogy

A typical commonplace example is as follows. In Figure 1 is a road-test circuit along which commercial road vehicles, manufactured at A, travel from the exit at A around to the parking compound at B on the other side of the factory. At A there is a checkout recording the number of vehicles exiting the depot at short regular intervals. At the far end of the circuit is a swing-bridge which, when opened allows canal barges to pass and when closed allows passage for the vehicles from A into the compound at B.

Now it is characteristic of anything having what can truly be called a 'speed' — such as any one of those road vehicles, for instance — that it can set out from A and then travel freely as far as the swing-bridge at B, regardless of whether that bridge at the far-end of the circuit is open or closed. If the bridge is closed, then the vehicles pass over it and complete the circuit into the compound. If the bridge is open, then they cannot pass but get held up at that point. Meanwhile, at the checkout the count of vehicles exiting A into the circuit remains undiminished, regardless of whether the bridge is open or closed.

Eventually, however, if the bridge remains open, the steady stream of vehicles produced at A creates, at the open bridge B, a traffic hold-up which backs up to fill the road to its maximum capacity as far as the entry point A. The road is then choc-a-bloc. Nothing moves. Everything is at a standstill. The count of vehicles passing the checkout is zero. That is what happens with objects that can truly be said to have a speed of free motion.

The Electrical Circuit

But now let us replace that road circuit with a simple electrical circuit, as in Figure 2. This consists of a loop of electrical wire, a battery with a switch to one side of the battery and any chosen kind of electrical detector on the other.[4]

Now it is to be noted that:

(i) When the switch at B is open (off) there is no detectable flow of anything analogous to that of the vehicles passing the checkout at A and filling up the road. Regardless of which way around the detector leads are connected, absolutely nothing is recorded by the instrument. Nor, when that switch is open at B, is there anything analogous to the build-up of traffic and backing up of vehicles behind those which cannot pass at that point. Despite the capacity of the circuit to accommodate the flow, even with a large capacitor added, with the switch open at B, the detector at A reveals nothing passing into the conductor. The circuit remains completely empty until the switch at B is closed. This is an observable fact, well known to electrical engineers, and it can be confirmed by anyone in a simple experiment with easily available bits of apparatus.

However, in contrast to this fact, the next statement is no more than a postulate, namely:

(ii) The speed of the conduction of the electricity (the electrical charge) around the circuit when the switch is closed is the constant speed $c$, of approximately 300,000,000 metres per second. This is the same constant $c$ that appears in Einstein's Second Postulate of his Special Theory of Relativity, namely, that 'the speed of light in vacuo is a constant, $c$, for all observers in all states of relative motion or rest'. This implies that the conduction of electricity takes place at the same distance-time rate as light in space, that is, 3.3 nanoseconds per metre of length.

However, in view of the simple experiment just described, these two propositions, (i) and (ii), plainly contradict each other. For the notion of 'speed' to make any sense in the electrical case, the conduction must start, as in the road example, from one pole of the battery, travel around the circuit, like those vehicles, and finish at the opposite pole.

However, if we assume, as in (ii), that the current 'travels' from A through the detector at that point and on around the circuit at 'speed $c$' towards B, then that implies that the flow from A has to be detected at A before it reaches the switch at B, regardless of whether that switch is open or closed. But this creates a mystery, because the current won't even set out from A if the switch at B is open. This implies that the current somehow 'knows', ahead of time, that the switch at B is closed, before setting out from A. In other words, the effect, detected at A, of the closing of the switch at B precedes that event by 3.3 nanoseconds for every metre length of the conductor — bearing in mind that the circuit may be any number of metres long. This implies a reversal of the natural order of causality, and hence reveals the absurdity of the assumption that $c$, in this electrical example, is a 'speed'.

The Resolution

So, to think of $c$ as the 'speed' of electrical conduction creates nonsense. Things that have what are truly called 'speed' simply do not work in that altogether perplexing way. However, as soon as that 'speed' interpretation of $c$ is abandoned, the situation makes perfect sense. Formula (1), below, is a simplified version of the standard relativistic time-formula:

$$t_R = \sqrt{(s/c)^2 + t^2} \tag{1}$$

This is essentially the same as the Einsteinian time formula[5] except that $c$, in this derivation, is not the 'speed of light' but is simply what Herman Bondi calls a dimensional distance-time conversion factor. The formula tells us, in effect, that in the case of $c$, every quantum of the energy that 'starts out' from A arrives instantly at B; that is, in the quantum proper-time $t = 0$ in the formula. This is why the quanta poised, as it were, to leave the higher-energy source, at A, seem to 'know ahead of time' whether or not to 'set out'. It is because, in their own proper-time $t = 0$, they are, in a definite sense, 'at both places at once'.

This is the same with quanta in general, with electricity as with light. The same formula shows that when that quantum time $t = 0$, the relative time $t_R$ is the observationally delayed time $s/c$. This means that in the time-frame of the observer locale, the detector at A gives its reading after the closing of the switch at B, by an interval which is the length $s$ of the circuit in metres divided by $c$.[6]

This reinstates the normal sequence of cause and effect by putting the effect after the cause, hence avoiding the absurdity of reversed causality. However, despite the signal appearing to have this 'speed' in all experimental measurements of $c$, that 'speed' is not really a speed but, as Bondi and this writer have concurred, a pure ratio of conventional measuring-units of metres and seconds.

If $c$ is interpreted as a 'speed', then we have the absurdity that the 'speed' of the current around the circuit is in a direction opposite to that of the signal. Otherwise, as a pure constant, this non-speed interpretation of $c$ as expressed in the formula, satisfies perfectly the requirements of both Relativity and Quantum Theory according to which, when the proper time (quantum time) $t$ of the contact between A and B is instantaneous (i.e., the quantum jump $t = 0$), the observer-time (relative time) $t_R$ is the ordinary delayed-time $s/c$.

What Does This Prove?

What is traditionally called 'electric current' is therefore indistinguishable from 'light'. Indeed, any transport of energy that does not involve a travelling mass is transported at a proper-speed (i.e., Newtonian speed) $V$ according to the Newtonian equation $V = \sqrt{2e/m}$ which, for $m = 0$, is infinite — hence, in relative time, $c$.

What this 'electrical' demonstration proves, then, is the same for 'electricity' as for light, which, as Bondi says:

Any attempt to measure the velocity of light is … not an attempt at measuring the velocity of light but an attempt at ascertaining the length of the standard metre in Paris in terms of time units.[8]

Bondi and Pope have shown, independently, and by mutual agreement,[9] that all the practical consequences of Einstein's Special Theory of Relativity can be deduced far more simply without Einstein's Second Postulate regarding the alleged 'velocity of light in vacuo'.[10]

What our practical demonstration proves, therefore, is no more than what pure logic tells us about the transport of quantum energy. This is that quanta, in the case of any such transportation, of radio, light or whatever, has to be instantaneous, otherwise there is a time-interval between a quantum 'leaving' a source-atom and being consummated at a distant atom, which the law of conservation of energy cannot allow.

This means that despite the phenomenon of time-delay in the transport of pure energy, be it light, radio or electricity, each element of the phenomenon is a virtual quantum touching in accordance with Newton's third law of reciprocal action-reaction. This is a somewhat neglected consequence of the Special Theory of Relativity, which Gilbert Lewis demonstrated just twenty-one years after that theory's first appearance in 1905.[11]

In short, what this demonstration does is to support the logical contention that neither in the case of electric current nor light-conduction can the quantum energy 'start out' from a source if there is nowhere for it to be received. This can be seen in the Thomas Young two-slit light-interference experiment, where the quanta 'avoid' those areas on the final screen where their frequencies, if they 'landed', would be out-of-phase with one another, and 'steer' in greater numbers for those areas where they would be in-phase.

Refraction and Optic Fibre

Now it is well known that according to standard relativity, all processes in a system travelling at a speed $v$ relative to the observer are time-dilated, or slowed, in comparison with the observer's local time. An electrical conductor or an optic fibre is such a system in which, as Feynman has said, 'everything is jiggling'. The difference is that in the electrical conductor the electrons involved in the current 'jiggle' at very slow speeds as compared with those in optic fibre, in whose atoms the electrons vibrate at frequencies consistent with the relayed light-energy.

From this it follows that if $c$ is the distance-time rate of light-signals in vacuo and $c_1$ the rate of light/electrical signals in the medium, then the ratio of the slower process of light-conduction in the medium to that in the vacuum is:

$$c_1/c = t_R/t_{R1} \tag{2}$$

where $t_R$ and $t_{R1}$ are observer times according to the time-dilation equation (1). It is scarcely coincidence that this ratio is the same as the refractive index, $\mu$, ordinarily given as $\mu = \sin i / \sin r = c / c_1$, where $c$ is, again, the 'speed in the vacuum' and $c_1$ the 'speed' in the medium.

In an electrical conductor the vibration speeds of the (unbound, or 'free') electrons are, as we have seen, extremely small, so that according to the usual relativistic equation, where $t_R = s/c$, we have $t_{R1} \approx t_R$, whence, according to (1), $c_1 \approx c$. By contrast, in an optic fibre, the vibrations of the (bound) electrons are very large, depending on the density of the medium and the frequency of the incident light, being upwards of $10^6$ metres per second. In that case, the fibre can be likened to a system moving as a whole relatively to the observer at an effective (rms) speed $v$, due to which the dilated time $t_{R1}$ is greater than $t_R$. In consequence, for the fibre, $c_1$ is smaller than $c$.

Conclusion

Both light and 'electricity' are disembodied energies, whence their behaviours in respect of their distance-time rates of conduction are, in essence, the same but for the difference in degree. However, this does not mean that 'electricity' can be identified as a form of light. An electric current cannot flow without a completed circuit, whereas light flows in a fibre towards an open end. The reason for this particular difference is not clear, but that does not logically contradict our conclusion that both electricity in a wire and light-in-vacuo are conducted at the same rate $c$, hence, to prove that $c$ is not a 'speed' in the one case is to prove also that it is not a 'speed' in the other.

This follows from the Newtonian mass-energy equation $e = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$, where $V$ is the classical, or proper-time velocity. In other words, if $m$ in the energy equation is zero, then $V = \sqrt{2e/m}$ is infinite, which, for any source-energy $e$, makes the relative velocity in the vacuum $v = c$, and in the medium $v_1 = c_1$. ■

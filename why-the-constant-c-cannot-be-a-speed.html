Why the Constant c Cannot be a Speed Viv Pope and Anthony Osborne

Abstract Herman Bondi and this author, in the 1950s concurred that the constant c in relativistic physics need not be interpreted as a speed, that all the standard consequences of Relativity can be deduced far more simply without Einstein’s Second Postulate which interprets c as the ‘constant speed of light in vacuo’. This paper produces evidence, from some common knowledge of electrical engineering, that not only supports the view of the redundancy of that ‘speed’ interpretation of c but also proves its logical invalidity.

A common belief is that ‘Electricity’ is a ‘flow of electrons’ measured in amps, analogous to gallons of water flowing along a pipe. [1] However, in electrical conduction along a wire, no electrons actually ‘flow’ in that way. Rather, what is ineptly called the ‘flow’ is a general drift of localised shifts in the orbits of electrons around their atomic nuclei in the direction of the so-called ‘current’ with none of the electrons travelling all the way. In this sense, the word ‘current’ is inappropriate, since there is no flow of electrons in the manner analogous to that of the continuous flow of water molecules in what is truly called a ‘current’. What actually occurs is a series of incipient, limited local movements of electrons in which the electrons travel at very small speeds of only millimetres per second [2] over distances of no more than about 0.0000007 metres (about the distance of ten atoms). What is said to ‘travel’ around the circuit, therefore, are not electrons as such but their disembodied charge measured in coulombs. It is this charge, conveyed in an altogether disembodied way, that is alleged to ‘travel’ around the circuit at the ‘constant speed c’, which is the same as the alleged ‘speed of light’. The driving force for these disembodied charges is an electromotive force (e.m.f.). Measured in volts, this is defined as ‘the characteristic of any energy source capable of driving electric charge around a circuit.’ (Enc Brit. 2001. C.D.) Let us assume, then, that what is said to ‘travel’ around the circuit at the ‘speed c’ [3] is this disembodied charge, in ultimate units of sixteen million billionths of a coulomb (the amount of charge ascribed to a single electron). First, then, let us examine what is commonly known as ‘speed’. This, of course, is the rate of change of distance in time, a notion which explicitly involves the idea of an object travelling. This is the same constant c that appears in Einstein’s Second Postulate of his Special Theory of Relativity, namely, that ‘the speed of light in vacuo is a constant, c, for all observers in all states of relative motion or rest.’ This implies that the conduction of electricity takes place at the same distance-time rate as light in space, that is, 3.3 nanoseconds per metre of length. However, in view of the simple experiment just described, these two propositions, i) and ii) plainly contradict each other. For the notion of ‘speed’ to make any sense in the electrical case, the conduction must start, as in the road example, from one pole of the battery, travel around the circuit, like those vehicles, and finish at the opposite pole. However, if we assume, as in ii), that the current ‘travels’ from A through the detector at that point and on around the circuit at ‘speed c’ towards B, then that implies that the flow from A has to be detected at A before it reaches the switch at B, regardless of whether that switch is open or closed. But this creates a mystery, because the current won’t even set out from A if the switch at B is open. This implies that the current somehow ‘knows’, ahead of time, that the switch at B is closed, before setting out from A. In other words, the effect, detected at A, of the closing of the switch at B precedes that event by 3.3 nanoseconds for every metre length of the conductor – bearing in mind that the circuit may be any number of metres long. This implies a reversal of the natural order of causality, and hence reveals the absurdity of the assumption that c, in this electrical example, is a ‘speed’. So, to think of c as the ‘speed’ of electrical conduction creates nonsense. Things that have what are truly called ‘speed’ simply do not work in that altogether perplexing way. However, as soon as that ‘speed’ interpretation of c is abandoned, the situation makes perfect sense. A question arises as to whether, the same effect we have described in relation to the electric wire might also take place in an optic fibre. Now it is well known that according to standard relativity, all processes in a system travelling at a speed v relative to the observer are time-dilated, or slowed, in comparison with the observer’s local time. An electrical conductor or an optic fire is such a system in which, as Feynman has said, ‘everything is jiggling’. The difference is that in the electrical conductor the electrons involved in the current ‘jiggle’ at very slow speeds as compared with those in optic fibre, in whose atoms the electrons vibrate at frequencies consistent with the relayed light-energy. [13] From this it follows that if c is the distance-time rate of light-signals in vacuo and c1 the rate of light/electrical signals in the medium, then the ratio of the slower process of lightconduction in the medium to that in the vacuum is: c1/c = tR/tR1 (2), where tR and tR1 are observer times according to the time-dilation equation (1). It is scarcely coincidence that this ratio is the same as the refractive index, μ, ordinarily given as μ = sin i/sin r = c/c1, where c is, again, the ‘speed in the vacuum’ and c1 the ‘speed’ in the medium. : Now in an electrical conductor the vibration speeds of the (unbound, or ‘free’) electrons are, as we have seen, extremely small, so that according to the usual relativistic equation, where tR = s/c, we have tR1 ≈ tR, whence, according to (1), c1 ≈ c. This answers to the ‘speed of electricity’ in an electrical conductor, which is said to be c (ref. FN 3.). By contrast, in an optic fibre, the vibrations of the (bound) electrons are very large, depending on the density of the medium and the frequency of the incident light, being upwards of 10 6 metres per second. In that case, the fibre can be likened to a system moving as a whole relatively to the observer at an effective (rms) speed v, due to which the dilated time tR1 is greater than tR, (where tR is, again, the minimum time s/c according to the Pythagorean timeequation, (1) for the proper-time t = 0). In consequence, for the fibre, c1 is smaller than c.[14]

In an optic fibre, then, the ‘speed’ of energy conduction is predictably ‘slower’ than that of ‘electricity’ in a wire. In the wire the energy is transported at virtually the distancetime rate c, while in the fibre it is at the refracted rate c1. Otherwise, the conduction in the fibre and in the wire are, in principle, the same, that is, they are both conduits for disembodied energy. This follows from the Newtonian mass-energy equation e = ½mV2, where V is the classical, or proper-time velocity (in relativity, the relative velocity v multiplied by √1 – (v2/c2)]. In other words, if m in the energy equation is zero, then V = √2e/m, is infinite, which, for any source-energy e, makes the relative velocity in the vacuum v = c, and in the medium v1 = c1 (see above). It is a moot point, then, as to whether or not in the case of light, a fibre-optic circuit might yield results consistent with those of our electrical example. What is certain, however, is that in that electrical example, for those reasons already discussed, the constant c cannot, logically, be interpreted as a ‘speed, and it is difficult to see how that same constant c can be a ‘speed’ in one instance and a non-speed in another. Both light and ‘electricity’ are disembodied energies, whence their behaviours in respect of their distance-time rates of conduction are, in essence the same but for the difference in degree. However, this does not mean that electricity’ can be identified as a form of light. An electric current cannot flow without a completed circuit, whereas light flows in a fibre towards an open end. The reason for this particular difference is not clear, but that does not logically contradict our conclusion that both electricity in a wire and light-in-vacuo are conducted at the same rate c, hence, to prove that c is not a ‘speed’ in the one case is to prove also that it is not a ‘speed’ in the other. Experiments with optic-fibre circuits are, of course, not so easily carried out as the very simple one with the battery, switch etc., described in Figure 2. However, on the basis of our ‘electrical’ demonstration, plus what is observed in the Thomas Young two-slit experiment, we may predict that effect of what we have called ‘teleblocking’ of the signal would be the same. In the wire, the teleblocking is achieved by the use of a circuitdisconnecting switch. In the fibre it would have to be by means of some interference condition similar to what occurs in the two-slit situation. This seems feasible in principle, with the only proviso being the purely technical one of containing the energy within the fibre to an extent concommitant with that of an electrical wire. In experiments carried out, so far, by this author and a colleague, a failure to ensure this latter condition has made the results inconclusive. This relation between refractive index and the speed of the moving medium was established by Armand Fizeau, in 1859. It was discovered, in effect, that the refractive index of the liquid increased with its speed v.

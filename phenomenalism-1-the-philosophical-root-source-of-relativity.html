Phenomenalism 1. the Philosophical Root-Source of Relativity

N. Vivian Pope — 1960

Abstract What is phenomenalism? It is the ultimate relativism, as Einstein’s philosophy mentor, Mach, conceived it and from which Einstein took his philosophical cue. Mach, however, felt that Einstein had compromised true relativism by talking about ‘atoms’, ‘electrons’ ‘fields’ and so on, as things-in-themselves, and of light in that same way as electromagnetic waves travelling on their own between these things in a physical vacuum, independently of all human perception and conception in a metaphysical, God’s-eye-view kind of way. So, when people hailed Mach as the philosophical precursor of Einsteinian Relativity, Mach firmly refuted that allegation. Relativity, as he conceived it, was pure phenomenalism, not the eclectic mish-mash of unexamined conventions which Einstein had cobbled together to produce what he, Mach, considered his idiosyncratic and philosophically sterile Theory. The following is a review of phenomenalism, or true relativism, as developed recently along the original lines pointed by Mach. This reveals how certain logical fallacies and omissions committed by its proponents in the history of phenomenalism (including Mach, it must be said) have led to a complete misrepresentation of that approach. Removal of those fallacies and omissions leads to a new and updated form of phenomenalism called Normal Realism. This is a consistently phenomenalistic approach to modern physics which, uniquely, dispenses with Einstein’s relativistic Second Postulate that light travels at a constant velocity in vacuo, hence with the notorious ‘Einstein separation’, or ‘light-barrier’, that Einsteinian ‘Relativity’ places between phenomena and their theoretical ‘sources’. The central contention, then, of this paper, strange as it must at first seem, is that ‘light does not travel’. Herman Bondi and this author concurred, in the early 1960s, that the constant c is not a ‘velocity’ but is no more than a dimensional ‘conversion factor’. Without this radical, truly ‘Copernican’ revolution in thinking, phenomenalism stands no chance whatsoever of being even considered as the natural foundation for future relativistic and quantum physics. This revolutionary shift in thinking about light does not dispute the practicality of Einstein’s Special and General theories. As a purely phenomenological constant, c retains exactly the same value and dimensions as Einstein’s ‘velocity in vacuo’, so that all the relevant equations remain the same. However, the new context in which these equations appear enhances our understanding of Relativity, not only by re-connecting it, logically, with its true Machian heritage but also by extending its physical and cosmological scope in the way that Anthony Osborne describes.

1. Primitive phenomenalism When we look out on the world, what do we see? Do we see things everywhere all at the same time, as an all-seeing, omnipresent ‘God’ might see them? Obviously not. We see things as we see them, with properties, qualities and dimensions that are defined relatively to ourselves, conceived and described in terms of the language of our culture. Any objectivity they may have is ascertained by systematic changes in perspective. We move about, we walk around things, measure them, sense their solidity, their weight, their scent, their sound, their taste, etc., etc., in all those ways known to ordinary commonsense, including consultation with other observers like ourselves. But never – never – do we perceive things from all those different perspectives all at once in the way that

some ubiquitous deity might be presumed to perceive them, far less in any way which is independent of language. Recognising this essential finitude of human perception and knowledge – of language in effect – in the pursuit of physical science defines the relativistic approach called Phenomenalism. According to the phenomenalists, a material object is not a mysterious something ‘behind’ those appearances of things that people experience in sensation. If it were, they say, then the material world would be unknowable. Indeed, the very conception of matter becomes unintelligible other than with reference to sense experiences, or phenomena. [1] However, for reasons to be analysed in due course, Phenomenalism is not, currently, the main approach to modern physics. The majority approach is what, by contrast, physicists call ‘Realism’. This implicitly presumes a ‘God’s eye view’ of an absolute reality underlying all the ‘appearances’, a reality with a space of its own, in a time all of its own, with properties, qualities and dimensions that, conceived by analogy with human perception and conception, are nevertheless somehow independent of their perceptual and conceptual roots. ‘Relativity’ as Einstein presented it, is an almost incidental theoretical appendage, a grudging recognition of the fact that all knowledge, in science as in commonsense, depends ultimately on observation. This ‘God’s-eye-view’ Realism was introduced into Western science by René Descartes (1596-1650). Practically all of subsequent physics became based on it. Even now, physicists who may never have heard of Descartes nevertheless subscribe, even if only subliminally, to the dogma he instituted and of which we are now scarcely more aware than fish are of water. For Descartes, there was not just one reality, but two. One was the world as it appears to us (the phenomena). The other was the world as it appears to an all-seeing, all-over Presence, which he, Descartes, identified with the God of canonical Christianity. In this splitminded or dualistic – one might almost say, schizophrenic – conception of reality, Descartes decided that these ideas of his, as to what God is and how that deity perceives things, were somehow radically distinct from his other ideas of how he himself perceived things. He notoriously failed to see that his ideas of what God perceives and his ideas of how he himself perceived were all, in the end, uniformly, his own ideas. The only difference was that while the one set of ideas were obviously his own, his other, more implicit set of ideas about what ‘God sees’ had been arbitrarily instilled into him by his social upbringing. The strength of that conditioning blinded him to the fact that commonsense objectivity cannot lie in comparing our up-front perceptions and conceptions with any ingrained ideas we might have of ‘God’. This is no more than to compare one set of ideas with another set of ideas within our own minds. Objectivity arises from comparing our own perceptions and ideas, not with our own ideas of God, but with the ideas of others like ourselves, whose perspectives systematically differ from ours but which are comparable and communicable in a common language. Nor do these different perspectives have to be those of other humans; they may well be those of instruments and instrument systems designed to probe where no human being or other organism may venture, and which provide information supplementary to bare personal sense perception. It includes information gathered and/or transacted at any and every level of nature, animal, vegetable or mineral. It incorporates not only verbal but also behavioural information – in fact, all information whatsoever. According to phenomenalism it is to this one and only world of logically processed and intercommunicated observational information, not to the forms of sanctified convention, that science has properly to be addressed. There are, however, many Realists of the Cartesian persuasion, in both physics and cosmology, who conceive it to be the aim of these subjects to ‘read the mind of God’. They would be coy of admitting that, of course. Indeed, they may vehemently deny any connection whatsoever with ‘God’. Yet the effect is the same. They start out, in the way that Descartes did, with an incipient, preconceived notion, cast in anthropic terms, of how ‘the Universe’ appears to an imagined infinite, superhuman, all-over presence. They then seek to explain how, from such imagined ‘beginnings’, we and what we perceive come to exist at random, by processes of the sort we are familiar with in our ordinary workshop and garden experiences of mechanical interaction and growth. In this way, they project into the nature of things, such ideas as the ‘anthropic principle’, ‘accidental fortuity’, ‘emergent evolution’, or ‘creation’ (invariably in terms of ‘the Big Bang’), ‘cosmological inflation’ and whatever. More often than not, this is by the use of Mathematics, conceived in the purely abstracted, Platonic way as ‘universal’ and superhuman (i.e., transcendental), having nothing to do with perspectives of any kind, far less those of finite human experience and observation. The term ‘Universe’, common in physics parlance, is therefore customarily taken in this way as referring to something that no-one ever perceives as such but is only ever imagined and theorised about, according to whatever might be the current fashion in speculative physics and cosmology. This could be the current view of all space, time and matter resulting from the explosion of a super-dense atomic ‘pea’ or, as in Hindu cosmology, something supported by elephants standing on the back of a giant turtle swimming in an infinite sea. In the last analysis, however, all these ideas, both ancient and modern are, from the phenomenalist point of view, uniformly metaphysical, as any projection of the infinite from the standpoint of the finite is bound to be. As an approach to physics, therefore, Phenomenalism differs radically from both Cartesian Realism and Mathematical Platonism, insofar as it does not seek to construct theories as to what exists and how it behaves ‘behind’ or ‘beyond’ the phenomena. Its business is essentially and entirely with phenomena, its scientific agenda being simply to find the most efficient, most logically consistent and coherent classifications and divisions of the phenomena themselves. These phenomena include observations as to what things are, what are their ultimate constituents and processes, how things behave and under what conditions, how they interact with one another … and so on. Another name for this phenomenalist agenda is empiricism, which is the scientific view that beliefs are to be accepted and acted upon only if they have been confirmed by actual experience. When we are assured that we have expressed the results of pursuing this phenomenalist/empiricist agenda in the clearest, most unambiguous use of language, then so far as the phenomenalist is concerned we have reached the level of natural reality, the one and only reality it

makes sense to speak of. For instance, when we have established, by all the usual means available, that a duck we see before us is not a decoy duck, a mirage or an hallucination, then to wonder whether that duck is a real duck or just an optical image of a duck is pure nonsense. As the philosopher J. L. Austin would say, ‘If that is not a (real) duck, then I don’t know what is.’ To seek any reality beyond that is therefore as pointless as a violinist in an orchestra, when he has tuned a string to perfect pitch, keeping on tuning towards some even higher perfection. In the same way, seeking for transcendental realities beyond actual phenomena does no more than extend physics into metaphysics, creating an ideational swamp for the proliferation of wild and unnecessary theories.

2. Traditional phenomenalism Unfortunately, as an approach to science, phenomenalism has been mostly confined to the science of psychology, with the emphasis placed on introspective modes of perception rather than on objective physics. This is due to the tradition of phenomenalism being shot through with certain fallacies and omissions that are revealed only in retrospect. First is the fallacy in the legacy bequeathed to phenomenalism by one of the foremost in the line of the ‘English Empiricists’. This was George Berkeley (1685-1753), whose famous dictum on which, for far too long, phenomenalism was based, was ‘esse is percipi’ (to be is to be perceived). Regrettably, this implied, for critics such as Samuel Johnson, that nothing exists except in the mind of the observer, the view called solipsism. This kept phenomenalism in a virtual straightjacket of alleged ‘subjective idealism’ or ‘psychologism’ for nigh on two centuries until the fallacy in that dictum was revealed in 1903 by the Cambridge philosopher, G. E. Moore, in his famous essay ‘Refutation of Idealism’. On grounds of logical analysis, Moore proved conclusively that there is no warrant whatsoever, logical or empirical, for regarding that Berkeleyan dictum as true, even for phenomenalism.

3. The era of Logical Positivism The second fallacy was in the move towards what became known as Logical Positivism. This began among a group of physics philosophers known as the Vienna Circle, which flourished from the 1920s to the outbreak of World War II. Based roughly on the sense-datum theory of Ernst Mach and the positivism of Auguste Comte, Logical Positivism had been developed to its final, decisive stage by proponents such as Alfred Ayer and Ludwig Wittgenstein and, to some extent, by Bertrand Russell and Alfred Whitehead. These Logical Positivists – or Logical Empiricists, as they were sometimes called – believed that by reducing observation to what they conceived as ultimately incorrigible ‘sense-data’ and reconstructing these along strictly logical/mathematical lines, all traces of subjectivity, all need for creative guesswork, or theory, could be removed. This, as they envisaged it, would produce a scientific meta-language of absolutely objective, non-theoretical descriptions of physical phenomena. In fact, however, Logical Positivism was never more than a passing phase in the history of phenomenalism. From 1929 onwards, this programme of formulating a meta-language of observational truths was as described in a source book Tractatus Logico Philosophicus written by Wittgenstein in 1921. This was partly based on the axiom systems of logicians such as Russell and Whitehead. However, that programme was found to be abortive and was therefore abandoned by its erstwhile adherents, led by Wittgenstein himself. Studies revealed that no

data of sense or instrumentation are objectively specifiable but are always contextual, depending upon the ways in which language is used. They found that it is in terms of these different usages (Wittgenstein called them ‘language games’) that the ‘data’ take their character, not vice versa. Consequently, in the later works of both Ayer and Wittgenstein, there took place a radical ‘sea change’. This was when phenomenalism took off in yet another radically new direction. Partly based on Moore’s Refutation of Berkeley, this launched a whole new genre of phenomenalist philosophy called Linguistic Analysis, associated with the names of such luminaries as John Austin and Gilbert Ryle, as well as the reformed Alfred Ayer and Ludwig Wittgenstein.

4. The era of Linguistic Analysis These developments, however, confined as they were to academic Philosophy, were a ‘closed book’ to physicists. In consequence, a persistent stumbling block to the acceptance of phenomenalism as the natural expression of the relativistic and quantum approaches to physics is the tendency, still prevalent among physicists, to confuse phenomenalism – and, indeed, Philosophy in general – with ‘Logical Positivism’. It was the realisation of the extent to which language organises and directs perception that led, in the name of Linguistic Analysis, to what was conceived as the proper usage of ‘Ordinary Language’ as the ultimate arbiter of truth and reality. Unfortunately, by language, these Linguistic philosophers (who became known, disparagingly, as the ‘Language Police’) tended to mean human language; in particular, the educated, in-group language of these Oxbridge Arts scholars. Taking this ‘Ordinary Language’ as oracular, they rejected scientific language as mere uneducated ‘jargon’ – or ‘scientism’ as many of Wittgenstein’s followers dubbed it. Natural reactions of physicists against what they regarded as this linguistic ‘fascism’ kept Phenomenalism banished from its rightful place in physical science for over half a century. This was the period during which Physical Science and Philosophy went their entirely separate, mostly unsympathetic – indeed, hostile – academic ways, the vestiges of which hostility can be discerned even today,

5. The crucial issue of light velocity Meanwhile, doubtless due to this academic ‘apartheid’ between Philosophy and Physics, something else, far more serious, was ailing phenomenalism as a satisfactory approach to natural philosophy. This was the surprising failure of academic phenomenalists, from Berkeley onwards, to cope with what, since Olaus Römer’s discovery in 1676, was taken to be the experimentally proven fact that the light by which we see things travels in space. The effect of this was, seemingly, to separate the phenomena from their atomic sources. Since the advent of Einstein’s Theory of Relativity in 1905, this problem has become more acute, due to what is now called ‘Einstein separation’, which ruled that no physical influence whatever can travel faster than the ‘constant speed of light’. This separatist implication is encapsulated in the basic postulates of Einstein’s Special Theory of Relativity. These state that the light by which we see things travels in space at a finite and constant absolute speed c, which is the same for all observers regardless of relative motion or rest. While this confounds the ordinary meaning of a ‘velocity’, it nevertheless supports the old, dualistic splitting-off of physical reality from our perceptions of it.

However, this last barrier was quietly removed in the 1960s with the logical discovery, made independently by Herman Bondi and this author, that the ‘velocity of light’ in the sense of ‘Einstein separation’ need not be thought of as a ‘velocity’ at all but only as a pure constant interrelating the observationally projected dimensions of distance and time, all uniformly within the context of phenomena. This entails exactly the same observational and experimental characteristics – even the time-delay – in the transaction of light information over distance as if light travelled in the conventional way, except that there is no ‘velocity’ of anything between the atoms involved in the transfer. Each element of the transfer is, in itself, a proper-time-instantaneous quantum jump, and it is only in observational sequences of these, in relative time (observer time) that these quantum jumps have the distance-time dimensions c. [2] In this case, it is truer to think of the space being in the light than of the light being in space, since the space and the time, for each and every observer, are projections out of the information contained, in the very first instance, in the light as observed. [3] This mends the artificial split between the ‘two realities’ of classical physics, uniting them as one and thereby removing all traces of both Cartesian dualism and Berkeleyan ‘subjectivism’ in the phenomenalist approach to modern physics.

6. Normal Realism: the restoration of phenomenalism as the root of relativity In this way, the theories of Relativity, both Special and General, stripped of the dualistic implications of Einstein’s Second Postulate, have been reformulated along much simpler, more philosophically consistent and altogether less metaphysical lines. This has been developed in association with Dr. A.D. Osborne, of the Mathematics Department of Keele University, UK, and Prof. A.F.T Winfield of the Computing, Engineering and Mathematical Science Faculty, at the University of West of England (UWE)). But, of course, in some areas of modern physics there have been similar moves towards a restored phenomenalism as, for instance, in Niels Bohr’s observer-based approach to the interpretation of quantum phenomena. However, these moves have been proposed on a more or less ad hoc, or pragmatic basis, with no consistent, well-worked-out philosophical underpinnings. So far as is known, it is only in the work of POAMS (the acronym for the Pope-Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis) that the phenomenalist philosophy that is implicit in both Relativity and Quantum theory has been fully articulated as a systematic and self-consistent, logical and mathematical alternative approach to modern physics. [4] This new or, rather, revived, Natural Philosophy approach to modern physics has been developed in a multidisciplinary association between exponents of science, mathematics and philosophy. The neo-phenomenalist philosophy on which it is based has been called Normal Realism.[5] Coined at the University of North Wales, Bangor, in the 1960s, this title signals a distinction from other ‘Realisms’, such as, e.g., Critical Realism, Naïve Realism and the remote realism (‘Realism’) of Descartes, Newton and Einstein. It retains, however, the empirical ideal of Positivism to dispense with theory, wherever possible, in favour of objective, up-front observational truth. This does not mean that it dispenses with theory altogether in the manner anticipated by Logical Positivism. What it does seek to dispense with are theories which either overtly or covertly presume, in the Cartesian manner, to be speaking for 'God' in matters of objective science. Now, of course, many people, including physicists, believe in a supreme Being, whether named ‘God’, ‘Allah’, ‘Jaweh’, ‘Cosmos’, ‘Universe’ or whatever. Adopting the phenomenalist approach to physics by no means implies that these beliefs have to be regarded as necessarily false – or, of course, necessarily true. The essentially agnostic view that Normal Realism takes is that from our standpoint of finite humanity, any preconceived notion we might have of 'The Infinite', whether it be in religion, physics or cosmology, just has to be pathetically small in relation to any such totality. To theorise, therefore, about ‘The Universe’, especially in seeking to name it or personify it is, for Normal Realism, a travesty of presumption. Even worse – and more to the point of this dissertation – is for theorists to claim, however tacitly, a special dispensation, as did Descartes and Einstein, for accepting the truth of theories based on premises involving these arbitrary, God’s-eye-view presumptions. (A glowing example of this was Einstein’s stated belief that ‘God’ does not ‘play dice’ which, throughout his life, inhibited his understanding of quantum phenomena.)

7. POAMS (the Pope-Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis) The twenty year history of the Pope-Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis (POAMS), based on the neo-phenomenalism of Normal Realism, starts in 1985. This was in a rare association between Philosophy, Mathematics and Information Theory, mainly involving, at the time, Dr. Osborne, Prof. Winfield and the present writer. The aim of this ‘POAMS group’ is to review the experimental and observational findings of modern eclectic physics and reinterpret these findings in keeping with the philosophy of phenomenalism – or, rather, Normal Realism. Now POAMS is often accused of being hostile to ‘other theories’. There is much truth in this, but not, it must be said, for the reasons that are so often imputed to it. In the first place, POAMS is not a ‘theory’ to compete with ‘other theories’. It is a whole scientific method. In effect, it is a paradigm which is distinct from and incommensurable with other paradigms, not least with the classical physics paradigm. This method is, surely, no more nor less than what would have been the standard, ‘default’ method of empirical science, had it not fallen foul of those fallacies that have been noted. After all, true relativity, quantum theory and observation naturally belong together as a group in the pursuit of physics. This does not deny the existence of atoms and so on as things in nature. It simply means that insofar as these things do exist it has to be in terms of observable data, couched in a language that is always subject to revision. This is in the light of a programme of critical logical and linguistic analysis that may reveal fallacies, ambiguities and redundancies that reduce the efficiency of that language in its articulation of natural truth and reality. POAMS and its philosophical parent, Normal Realism, therefore admit, unreservedly, to the charge of being hostile to certain kinds of theory. These are identified, by ‘Socratic analysis’, as systems of thought which, on the face of it,

purport to be ‘theories’ but which prove, on analysis, actually to be dogmas. These are regarded as disruptive of the integrity of science by being immune to rational negotiation, hence wasteful of dialectical effort. Mainly, the ‘theories’ which POAMS seeks to eliminate are those that are found to be based on the dualistic dogma of Descartes, the fallacy of which modern Philosophy has revealed but of which modern physicists mostly remain uninformed. Otherwise, POAMS thoroughly recognises the need for theories that are entirely in keeping with the empiricist, anti-dogmatist agenda. Observation is essentially open-ended and interpretative. Theories as to what it is, exactly, that one is observing are therefore sometimes essential. Things don’t come readily classified and labelled for what they are. It is we who do that classifying, dividing and labelling, by our creative uses of language; and very often, in science especially, that language has to be revised in the light of advancing experience. Revising the language, rearranging its classes and categories so as to achieve the most logically coherent and efficient descriptions of what there is, is an altogether analytic, theoretical and linguistic operation – which, of course, points the relevance (for all its faults) of linguistic philosophy, as pursued by Wittgenstein, et al. For these reasons, phenomenalism is essentially anti-dogma. Theories that are based on uses of language taken as fixed and oracular, which have become immune to logical re-examination and revision, are regarded as detrimental to any prospect of scientific progress and it is to these that POAMS is hostile. Apart from that, since observation is essentially open-ended, theories are recognised to be the very life-blood of the phenomenalist approach to physical science, just so long as they avoid presumptions of God-given certainty, either in their choices of premises or in their conclusions. God only knows what God knows! In seeking to second-guess that deity’s Views and Intentions, therefore, Theoretical Physics and Cosmology make lifeless ‘graven images’, not only of nature but also of themselves. Surely it is now time to consign those graven images to the melting pot and to remodel Natural Philosophy in keeping with its original, phenomenalistic form.

8. Summary The truly revolutionary – for many, no doubt, alarming – alternative that POAMS offers as its contribution to modern physics, is to recast the disparate observationalist trends in both Relativity and Quantum Theory in the form of an up-dated and unified phenomenalism relieved of its traditional fallacies and omissions. Central to this paradigm flipover is the reinterpretation of the constant c in the manner concurred by the present author and Bondi, in the early 1960s, as a pure constant of phenomenological projection instead of the customary ‘velocity of light in space’. In POAMS, both the ‘space’ in which light is alleged to ‘travel’, and the time taken, are projections out of light as actually observed; that is, light, in its fullest spectral range as the source of all the information on which our knowledge of the world is ultimately based. Some people ask ‘Where does all this information come from?’ The answer POAMS gives is that it comes, not out of some metaphysical empyrean but from (or, rather, it reduces to) the interactions between the atoms down to which things in the world may be phenomenologically analysed. The ultimate irreducibles of these interactions are the quanta h and h/2π which are the elements both of physical interaction and phenomenological information. These elements do not ‘travel’ in any sense, but simply occur in information-statistical patterns and sequences, similar to the manner in which,

from the occurrences of screen pixels, the viewer projects the space and the action of a video scenario. A physics based on information, in this way, is altogether different from a physics based on intuitions about unseen mechanical events and processes of the classical analog kind underlying the actual phenomena. The wholesale paradigm shift that this change in thinking about light entails is therefore from the traditional mechanics-based to a modern information based physics, which combines Modern Physics with modern Information Theory, all within the context of observational relativism, or phenomenalism. Perhaps the best way of describing this shift is that it matches, in Physics, the revolution that has already taken place from ‘analog’ to ‘digital’ in practically all areas of modern communication. *

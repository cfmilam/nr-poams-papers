Two of the Biggest Mistakes Science Ever Made

N. Vivian Pope

Great men, it is said, make great mistakes. One of the two Greatest Mistakes ever made by our Great Men of science was by Albert Einstein. This was with regard to something that was discovered in 1676 by the Danish astronomer, Olaus Römer.

As the earth and Jupiter pursue their different orbits around the sun, the line-of-sight distance between the two planets sometimes gets larger and sometimes smaller. Studying Jupiter's main satellite, Io, as it orbits its parent planet, Römer noticed that while the distance of Jupiter from earth was increasing the orbital revolutions of Io around it seemed slower, and while the distance was decreasing those revolutions seemed quicker. The amount of time lost or gained in relation to the distance, Römer measured to be in the ratio we now know as a constant, c, of 186,000 miles, or 300,000 kilometres to the second. In other words, it was assumed that the apparent changes in the times of the observed revolutions of Io around Jupiter were due to light having to travel progressively greater or lesser distances from Jupiter as it and the earth orbited the sun. It was in this way that Römer's discovery of the ratio, c, of distance-measure to time measure in our observations of distant objects came to be known as the 'speed of light in space'.

This ratio, c, of 300,000 kilometres to the second (or, more exactly, 2.99792458 × 10⁸ metres to the second) for 'light in vacuo' was subsequently confirmed in other sectors of science, not only on an astronomical scale but also on the scale of the lengths of table-top apparatuses.

However, there was a snag. All speeds have to be measured relatively to something. For instance, the speed of someone walking along the corridor of a train may be three miles per hour relative to the train; but if the train is travelling at a hundred miles per hour relative to the track, then the passenger is travelling at a hundred-and-three miles per hour relative to the track – that is, if he walks in the forward direction of the train. If he walks in the opposite direction he travels at ninety-seven miles per hour relative to the track. All this is, of course, plain commonsense.

However, in space – in a void or vacuum – what is the light travelling relative to? The whole idea of light travelling at a constant speed in or relative to a vacuum – relative to literally nothing – is pure nonsense. The well-attested fact is that for all observers, regardless of whatever states of motion or rest they may have with regard to one another, all measures of distances in space are times in that constant ratio of units, c. But it does not logically follow that because c has the dimensions of a speed it is a speed. It is undeniable that all speeds are distances divided by time, but it is no more valid to infer from this that all distances divided by time are speeds than to infer from 'All Chinese are human' that 'All humans are Chinese'.

Besides, what sort of 'speed' can possibly be the same relative not only to a vacuum but also to all observers moving relatively to one another in it? There simply is no way in which light can have a speed in any meaningful sense of the word. Indeed, it is not too much to say that this altogether incredible oversight on the part of classical scientists, in interpreting Römer's constant, c as a 'speed', has perverted the whole of modern theoretical physics.

Arguably, the first to discover this fallacy was Herman Bondi who, in his book Assumption and Myth in Physical Theory, wrote:

Any attempt to measure the velocity of light is … not an attempt at measuring the velocity of light but an attempt at ascertaining the length of the standard metre in Paris in terms of time-units.

The advantages offered by this minimalist, non-velocity interpretation of c, towards both the understanding and teaching of relativity theory are undeniable and have been widely published by Bondi and this present writer, who both, independently, reached that same conclusion at about the same time.

This minimalist re-interpretation of Römer's constant does not in the least destroy the practical efficacy of Einstein's relativistic formulae. What it does is to change the philosophical infrastructure of the theory in such a way as to get rid of its theoretical over-elaboration which has so notoriously confounded ordinary commonsense ever since the theory first appeared.

So the Great scientific mistake made by Albert Einstein was in his second basic axiom of relativity which states that:

The velocity of light in vacuo is a constant, c for all measurers regardless of their states of motion or rest with respect to one another.

This can be replaced by the much simpler and more logically accurate, Bondian axiom:

The ratio of units of space measure to time-measure is a constant for all observers.

To add that this is the same for all observers in all different states of relative motion and rest would be unnecessary, since it is impossible to imagine how it could be otherwise – as if there could be more or less inches in a foot or more or less than 2.2046226 pounds in a kilogram for different measurers in relative motion.

But if this simpler way of interpreting Römer's constant makes no practical difference to the theory of relativity, then why should it bother us, especially if we are not professional scientists? For Bondi, the main – and perhaps the only – advantage offered by this non-velocity interpretation of c was its sheer simplicity in the teaching of relativity to his students. However, the logical implications of the interpretational change are by no means so trivial. This may be appreciated when we see how that Great Mistake of Einstein's was compounded with another Great Mistake that had been made by his illustrious predecessor, Isaac Newton.

In 1687, in his monumental work, the Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica (Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy), Newton stated his well-known 'Laws of Motion'. The first of these 'laws' states that if a body is at rest or moving at a constant speed in a straight line, it will remain at rest or keep moving in a straight line at constant speed unless it is acted upon by a force.

Now when did anyone ever see force-free bodies behaving in that way? The planets certainly don't. If we throw a ball in the air, it doesn't keep on going upwards. Nor does anything ever remain at rest relatively to other bodies. Indeed, the plain observational fact is that nothing ever stands or moves in the way Newton's First Law declares. Evidently, then, the sort of force-free motion that Newton's First Law describes is a fiction.

And this is where science, having first lost its balance with its over-interpretation of Römer's discovery of c, takes its plunge into the sea of mysticism which we now know as Theoretical Physics. Newton was saying, in effect, that physical reality is not what we see but is something we do not see, something known only to scientific reason as the underlying and unseen causes of whatever we do see.

But of course, what we actually see is that all force-free motion is naturally cyclic, or orbital. So if we accept Newton's First Fiction as true, then we have to accept another, supporting fiction. This is Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation, according to which what we see as force-free orbital motion is never really force-free but is caused by unseen 'forces' that bodies exert on one another in proportion to their mass, so that all masses are drawn into loops around one another by those hidden 'forces'.

The fact is, however, that what we see as natural orbital motion can be explained quite simply in purely observational terms without any reference whatsoever to those hidden 'forces' of Newton's.

As Aristotle argued, there cannot be any such thing as force-free motion in a straight line. This, he said, is because a straight line cannot be infinite but has to begin and end somewhere which, in either event entails the influence of a force. The only way, therefore, Aristotle concluded, in which a force-free motion can be both continuous and finite is by being cyclic or orbital. And indeed, this is what we find when we study the way in which bodies actually move.

Newton quantified motion as the mass of a body multiplied by its velocity, or momentum. This, however, omits one very important observational measure, which is the distance of that moving body from other bodies. When that neglected measure is included, Newton's fictional straight-line 'momentum' becomes angular momentum, which is automatically curved, or orbital in the way Aristotle described. That is to say, while Newton's 'momentum' is mass multiplied by velocity, angular momentum is Newton's 'momentum' multiplied by that body's distance from some mass or other, so that its angular momentum sufficiently describes its orbit around that other body.

In this case, the only straight-line motion of a body, in the way Newton imagined, is an angular momentum of infinite magnitude at an infinite distance from any other body, an orbit of infinite radius being theoretically indistinguishable from a straight line. But if straight-line motion is that of an infinite angular momentum on a theoretically infinite radius, then it follows that all finite angular momentum is on a finite radius, hence automatically curvilinear or orbital.

This disposes of any need to think of there being any hidden in vacuo 'forces' responsible for the orbital motion that we everywhere observe. Since angular momentum is sufficient in itself to explain orbital motion in observational terms, without recourse to Newton's fictional 'forces', the only true forces we need be concerned with are those real, felt and measurable forces that have to be exerted in order to prevent bodies from following their natural, force-free angular momentum trajectories.

A commonplace example of this is the real force which has to be exerted by the drum of a spin-dryer to squeeze the water out of the washing and prevent the clothes from following through the holes that are provided for the water's escape. Another common example is the force that is measured by a weighing scale which prevents bodies which have insufficient angular momentum to orbit above the earth's surface from orbiting freely where they otherwise would, somewhere way down below our feet.

But, again, so what? What does it matter to us in our daily lives, whether 'gravitational force' is something we can actually feel and measure or is known only to scientific and mathematical reason as something acting between bodies in empty space? What matters is that it creates an esotery, like that of the middle ages, which separates all specialised knowledge of that alleged 'underlying reality' from ordinary understanding.

It was this fashion of denying the reality of what we actually observe and removing it into realms apart, into hidden 'force-fields' and other unseen mechanisms – in effect, giving to the nothings between things the characters of things – that was followed by Einstein with his 'electromagnetic' depiction of the constant c. This was based on the field-theories of Michael Faraday and others who modelled their ideas of electrostatic and magnetic force on the same lines as Newton's 'gravitational force', as 'fields' acting in vacuo.

The observational end-product of all these alleged 'forces' is always, of course, the movement of some body or other, and it can be shown that these motions can all be explained, perfectly adequately, in purely observational terms of angular momentum, without involving these esoteric 'field-forces' in any way. However, once firmly established as a respected tradition, to revise it in favour of any new, simpler and more empirical approach to physics becomes well nigh impossible.

In this way, the Newtonian tradition of behind-the-scenes theorising has created a new kind of priesthood in which, like the 'heaven' of the medieval Church doctrine, 'the physical world' needs to be interpreted to ordinary people by others claiming a special intimacy with it, in a language which, largely, those ordinary folks cannot understand. Arguments like those of the medieval Church scholars, as to how many angels might sit on the head of a pin are now replaced by similar arguments as to how many altogether strange and exotic fragments matter may be split into so as to explain life and ordinary reality. The logic of it is as though by pulverising chessmen into ultimate bits, classifying these and giving them bizarre names, we should somehow eventually explain the rules of chess.

To most people, of course, it doesn't matter a jot what these theoreticians do or that huge sums of their hard-earned money goes into funding internecine disputes over issues as remote from them as the back side of the moon. Huge sums of money are spent also on funding expensive experiments designed to verify this theory or that, among so many and various theoretical predictions. But, surely, that's alright, some might say, since this has always been the way forward for empirical science in an open-ended search for knowledge.

Unfortunately, all too much of this money, time and effort is now squandered in trying to prove theories which even the slightest attention to logic reveals to be complete absurdities. All that can be achieved in such cases are silly answers to silly questions. For instance, to detect waves, of either light or 'gravity', in a vacuum, are logical non-starters. This is for two logical reasons: first is that wherever a detector is placed is, at that point, no longer a vacuum and, second, there is, by definition, nothing to 'wave' in a vacuum.

And if, as Newton assumed, light consists of travelling particles, which Einstein called 'photons', then since a 'photon' (or quantum light-particle) is by definition irreducible, logically it cannot be detected anywhere between its points of emission and absorption, which, for the photon itself, since its energy is unsharable, have to be one and the same event, regardless of the distance over which we say it 'travels'. Experiments, therefore, designed to determine how the photon behaves in space promise to be as fruitful as experiments to find the compressibility of ectoplasm or the power-to-weight ratio of an angel flying around in heaven.

For reasons such as this, expensive experiments designed to detect 'gravity waves' and/or 'gravitons', based as they are on those Great Mistakes made by our physics luminaries should, surely, be the concern of those of us who, without our say-so, are forced to subscribe so much of our earnings towards them.

Of even greater concern, to some, however, is the inhibiting effect these vain theories have on our natural need to understand the world around us. With that natural curiosity monopolised by the theoreticians, people lose interest in 'Theoretical Physics' as something that seems not to concern them. Indeed, so discouraged has commonsense become, due to what they see as the remoteness of the subject, that 'physics', originally conceived as something that concerns us all in our daily contacts with the world, needs to be rediscovered.

Worst of all is the consequence that those we entrust with the care of our society and the duty of keeping it on moral tracks can do so only with a truncated knowledge of how we and our society fit into the physical nature of things. So they concentrate on keeping the rest of us 'under wraps' whilst leaving science, for the large part – the part they are not qualified to understand – to do 'what the hell it likes'. This disengages that esoteric part of science from the normal democratic process which keeps issues that largely concern us under the control of commonsense.

But with our minds deluged, via the public media, with 'authoritarian' ideas over which we have no commonsense jurisdiction – such as that the whole of space and time exploded out of nothing and expanded into nothing, some billions of years ago, that the vacuum is full of whorls and eddies, with waves in it like an ocean, that there are holes in it blacker than empty space itself … and so on, what recourse do we have but to exclaim 'Gee whiz!' and pay up.

Imagine some political party standing for election on the slogan: 'Vote for us and we will take your hard-earned money to blind you with science.' Would you vote for them? I certainly wouldn't! ■

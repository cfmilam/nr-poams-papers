The Lingering Death of Western Dualism

N. Vivian Pope — ANPA Conference, Cambridge, September 1994

Abstract

The aim of this talk will be to examine the strange compulsion, not only in physics but also in what we happily call 'commonsense', to think of our perceptions of physical objects as mediated by light travelling invisibly in a vacuum.

The dissociation this creates between 'the real world' and our perceptions of it, which philosophers call dualism, persists despite Gilbert Lewis's having pointed out in 1926 that in relativity the intrinsic or 'proper' distance and time of any direct light-interaction are precisely zero, so that the 'photon' is not a travelling light-particle but an event common to both object and observer. The dualistic dissociation also persists in spite of the plainly demonstrable fact that relativity theory is much more easily expressed and understood without light-velocity than with it.

Moreover, dualism persists regardless of the fact that by splitting the world into 'objective' (i.e., physical) and 'subjective' (i.e., observational) in this artificial way, the 'velocity of light' conception is plainly wrecking our society. This it does by implying that the primary reality consists of mindless atomic mechanisms and space, and that all values of a non-physical (i.e., perceptual or 'mental') nature, including individual and social responsibility, morality, religion — in fact, just about everything on which civilization depends — are just secondary spin-offs; that is, as the 'realists' read it, abstract, ideological rubbish.

The taboos that evidently militate against setting aside, even in theory, this dualistic 'velocity' interpretation of light in favour of seeing light simply as observer-processed bits of quantum information will, it is hoped, be discussed in some depth, followed by the question of how this bears on the prevailing ANPA methodology, called the 'Combinatorial Hierarchies'.

Transcript of Talk

A Digest of Discussions at the 'Blue Anchor' Pub on the Gower Peninsula, 1994

I would like to examine, here, and then discuss with you, the strange compulsion we have in our theories of physics to talk about 'the velocity of light' as though it were a fact of nature that light travels in space at a finite and constant speed, $c$. Now I'm not disputing either the constancy of $c$ or that it is one of the most meticulously measured constants in physics. I simply want to point out (or reiterate, for those who have heard me before) that there is no logical necessity to think of $c$ as a speed and that our compulsion to interpret it as such is no more than the troublesome relic of a now moribund yet strangely recalcitrant philosophical dogma called dualism. Let me explain.

Every physicist, however hard-nosed and illustrious, was, of course, once a child and like every child in every culture he learns to distinguish himself (his system of arms, legs, thoughts, feelings, and so on) from the general background consisting of other entities, some like himself such as other members of his family and community; and others unlike himself, such as the sun, moon and stars, animals, rocks, trees, and so on.

As he grows he learns not only to construct more and more of these concepts but also to sort them into various groups and kinds, such as animal, vegetable and mineral, concrete-abstract, material, mental, and so on. Different cultures may differ in the sounds and other signs by which members signal to one another their recognitions of those same concepts and concept-categories. They may also, however, differ far more radically, in the ways in which they form the concepts and categories themselves. Eskimos, for instance, have different names for many kinds of what we call 'snow'. These various arrangements of classes and divisions of experience, communicated by the spoken and written word, bodily behaviour and so on, are what in its broadest sense philosophers call language.

There are, nevertheless, common factors in all languages, otherwise those languages could never be interpretable to one another. What culture, for instance, fails to distinguish day and night or up and down? Thus we may talk of a sense of things which is common to all cultures and which we may take as the basic meaning of 'commonsense'.

Accordingly, let us call that globally common way of looking at the world the Normal or Commonsense way. But now we come to that culture which is founded on the traditions of our Western science. Here, the child begins in the commonsense manner of all cultures, by classifying and categorising his forms of experience in a communicable and socially acceptable way. However, as he grows he imbibes, in one way or another, the idea that the things he perceives as 'other than himself' are not what they seem; that they are no more than sensory representations, or impressions, which he constructs in his mind out of little bits of energy given off by the only truly real things, atoms (or, as physicists now say, 'fundamental physical particles'). And modern physics tells him that the fastest possible speed at which that energy can be transacted is the 'speed of light'.

His perceptions are therefore always what scientists call localised; that is, always to some extent or other remote in space and time from the actual reality of physical things, which are also localised, or isolated in the same way from one another. What things really are or how they are 'in themselves' is thus, if he thinks about it, a complete mystery. All he can ever do, at best, is to guess what they are from his own sense-reconstructions out of whatever bits of information those things may happen to throw off into space and which, after travelling for aeons in some cases, may just happen to impinge on his sense-organs or scientific instruments. And since there is no way of knowing those things as they are 'in themselves' other than through those intermediary processes and end-impressions, then how can he ever know whether those impressions are true or false? Thus, at his first flush of intellectual awareness he is confronted with a great problem which is endemic in our whole Western way of thinking and which philosophers call the Problem of Knowledge.

This, it needs to be stressed, is no mere academic problem. It means that in our society we all suffer, in one way or another, from an institutionalised schizophrenia in our attitudes towards 'reality'. For a symptom of this malaise we need look no further than at the way in which our educational institutions traditionally distinguish, in an entirely polarised way, between Arts and Science, between, on the one hand, subjects like Philosophy, Psychology and Theology, and on the other hand, subjects like Physics, Chemistry, Mechanics, and so on, as though that split reflected some real, radical divide in nature. Indeed, our very language has evolved around this same dualistic assumption, with its category distinctions between 'mind' and 'matter', 'body' and 'soul' and so on. This 'double-talk' in which we direct and organise our thoughts and actions signifies that right at the very root of our Western society there is a built-in duplicity which makes us think, speak and theorise, however inadvertently, 'with forked tongue'.

There are all sorts of social problems arising from this, not least of which is the question of whether to put our faith in 'the appearances' (the 'mental' world) and go for the Arts, as I say (or Humanities, as it is now more often called) or to believe in the underlying atomic sources of those appearances (the 'world of matter') and go for what, by implication might be called the In-Humanities. If we go for the Humanities, then those Sciences which are modelled on Physics are inimical to us, since they are, to us, so innately 'mindless and materialistic'. And if we go for Science, then all talk of humanitarian (i.e., 'spiritual') values, such as justice, morality and — needless to say — religion become no more than psychological or social 'conventions', meaning that in what we implicitly regard as the 'real world' of stars and atoms none of these things signifies, or that they signify nowhere but 'in our minds'. So the only 'God' the Science-sophisticate can contemplate, if any, is some great and utterly detached 'Design-Engineer' of the mechanical scheme of things; and the best that remains of 'morality' for him is to remain cynical or stoical in the face of a beautifully ordered but uncaring universe. The more popular this despairing sort of 'realism' becomes the more the premiums fall, especially in the young, either on philosophical indifference or on escapism by whatever means is available — in some form of religious or political dogma, perhaps, or in 'wine', as Omar Khayyám advocated — or drugs, as is nowadays more and more commonly the case.

The scandal is that in the two thousand years or so of the formation of our present culture, and despite the fact that practically all of our academic institutions of philosophy have been devoted to solving it, there has never been any satisfactory, authoritative solution to that Problem of Knowledge — nor, needless to say, to all the social and intellectual confusion this Problem has generated. Doubtless, this is why practically-minded people like physicists tend to avoid the subject of Philosophy like the plague — because studying it, as everybody knows, 'gets us nowhere'. However, that mind-bending 'Problem' which has for so long enervated our philosophical institutions — not to say our whole society — is, as it transpires, really no more than a conventional, self-imposed logical trap, a conundrum. The reason, as it turns out, is astonishingly simple. If we say there is a 'veil of perception' preventing us from knowing things as they really are, then how can we claim, in the same breath, to know that there is some problem about knowing them? In other words, how can there possibly be a 'Problem of Knowledge' concerning things that cannot possibly be known?

Realising this, some modern philosophers have completely changed tack. Instead of perpetuating the vain academic task of seeking to solve that insoluble problem they have sought to dis-solve it, declaring it to be what the philosopher Wittgenstein called a Scheinproblem. In taking that different course, however, these philosophers have had to break with the traditions of what they call the 'scientism' which creates and sustains that problem with its ideas about how our perceptions of things are separated by a vacuum and mediated by light. Accordingly, they have cast off all connection with that Western tradition of dualism and reverted to the alternative way of thinking identified at the start as the Commonsense way. Unfortunately for these new 'Commonsense' philosophers, so prestigious has that 'scientism' now become and so endemic in Western culture is its idea that the light by which we perceive things travels in space, that in going out on a limb against it, these new Commonsense philosophers have seemed 'unscientific' — that is, at best irrelevant and at worst ridiculous.

But now things are definitely beginning to change. Since the discovery of relativity and the quantum, each new discovery nudges modern science more and more in that new philosophical direction. That is to say, among scientists the realisation is gradually dawning that our ultimate observational interrelations with external objects are not mediated in the way we have customarily supposed, by light-waves or light-particles ('photons') starting out from atoms at one instant in universal time and finishing up in our nerve-endings or sensing-instruments at another. A little-known chemist, Gilbert Lewis, showed in 1926 that in what relativists call 'proper-time', these 'photons' are not travelling particles but quantum-events happening at the same instant to both object and observer. In other words, he showed, in effect, that there is no longer any scientific justification for thinking of the physical world and the perceived world as inexorably separated. At the most basic level common to all perceptions and physical processes — that is, at the fundamental quantum level — those 'two worlds' are now seen to be, logically, one and the same.

The philosophical consequences of this radical shift in scientific thinking are profound — some find it even disturbing. Perhaps it is fortunate, then, that the realisation of these consequences, even in science, is slow in coming. However, for anyone with the temerity to consider them, these consequences are as follows.

In classical physics, the ways in which things and collections of things configure as wholes were confined entirely to perception and were therefore, in comparison with 'physical reality', illusion. So if all perceivers disappeared, then all that would remain, in some way which it is impossible to describe without equivocation, would be the ultimately reduced and further irreducible sources of our perceptions, that is, atoms, floating in a self-sufficient void, as mindless of one another as of the absence or presence of ourselves. In modern physics that complete dualistic dissociation between 'the world of wholes' and 'the world of parts' disappears and, as soon as ever we come to realise it, along with it goes our old schizophrenic view of reality. The wholes and the parts then come together in the one and only real world that it makes sense to speak of, the world that stands right there before us.

What some find so disturbing about this is that since only perception can interrelate one mindless particular and another, this new emphasis on interrelations among things, which we call relativism, seems, to them, to 'mentalise' the world, reducing everything to perception, which we are accustomed to think of as a world of illusion. However, if the old dichotomy between 'mind' and 'matter' is false, then to deny that everything is matter no more proves that everything is mind than denying that a man is Welsh proves that he is English.

By the same token, 'humanising' science in this way by no means reduces the world to human proportions, any more than 'relativising' it reduces it to the perceptions of a single observer (i.e., oneself). In relativity, 'an observer' may be anything from a scientist or a scientific instrument to the smallest and simplest object there is. What qualifies something to be an 'observer', no matter how complex or rudimentary, is simply its capacity to relate to something other than itself in the context of the whole interrelational network. An atom, for instance, exists insofar as it is a rudimentary 'observer' that can communicate its existence to (i.e., interrelate with) another atom in some holistic, observational/experimental context — for example, in transacting optical energy-blips of an identifying spectral signature or, at the very least, in contributing to the overall balance of angular momentum of the stars, planets and so on. The world, according to modern science, is therefore a communicational system of indeterminate quantum-transactions, as different from the old 'mechanical universe' as a computer is from a steam-engine.

In being forced, in this way, to become at least as much the study of systems of interrelations (wholes) as of atomic relata (ultimate parts), relativistic physics has become what commentators refer to as holistic, as opposed to the partistic, or reductionist character of earlier physics. In other words, in this new holistic approach, the emphasis in the scientific search for reality shifts away from reducing everything to 'elements' (i.e., fundamental particles, numbers, or whatever) towards discovering what things are and how they function, first and foremost, as integrated and interrelated wholes. This result of the combined relativising and quantising of modern physics has therefore made the subject fall more and more into line with the basic Commonsense form of realism described at the beginning.

The main philosophical consequence of this scientific 'sea-change' is that in becoming relativistic or relationist, modern physics now accepts, as natural objects of serious scientific study, many of the things and situations formerly regarded as capable of existing only in the 'human psyche'. Thus, as other sciences like biology and zoology have already been forced to concede that intelligence is no longer an attribute solely of human behaviour but that it extends also to animals and even vegetables, so physics has now had to follow suit in attributing intelligence (holistically, that is) to the last remaining category, that of minerals. Thus, intelligence, under the new neutralised name of information, is now a universal attribute in which all natural processes, physical and mechanical as well as biological, partake to a greater or lesser extent. The fact that it seems nonsense to attribute intelligence to minerals — as someone said to me 'Does that mean that we can expect there to be conferences among flat-irons?' — merely illustrates the extent to which our dualistic 'double-talk' ill-prepares us for such logical developments.

Now some might say that a 'universal intelligence' is, by definition, a deity. If so (and that inference, of course, whilst logically permissible is by no means logically obligatory), then any deification of Nature or cosmism according to this 'new deal' in science would have to be what theologians distinguish as a theistic rather than a deistic conception, that is, a communicating, information-transacting deity, not a machine. Moreover, with the removal of mechanical certainty the essence of morality is restored as a principle of nature in the responsibility this places on organisms capable of choosing between one course of action and another, for carving out their own futures — in the same way that being free to operate the keys on my word-processor makes me responsible for whatever communicational triumphs or disasters that instrument produces. By the same token, principles like justice, right and wrong, good and evil and so on, cease to be what science has so long regarded them, as mere ideological noises made by priests and politicians and become, once again, all part and parcel of the evolving global Commonsense ontology. That is, they complement the laws of physics, mechanics, medicine and so on, as factors in the same holistic (or, in terms now regarded as archaic, 'holy') equation.

This promise of a reconciliation between modern science and what we customarily regard as 'mysticism' is, of course, disquieting to Western traditionalists, especially since some physicists seem to have gone to what those Western traditionalists regard as mystical (usually Eastern) extremes. Such rapid innovation, however, inevitably produces polarisation into a radical Left and a reactionary Right, with all sorts of shades of difference and indifference in between — indeed, the 'don't knows' and 'don't cares' are doubtless well in the majority at present. However, when all this settles, as sooner or later it must, then perhaps we just might witness what some foresee as the dawn of a New Age of true understanding, when the collapsed philosophical underpinnings of Western politics are renewed, when ordinary commonsense wisdom catches up with the expansion of scientific and technical knowledge and global communication replaces the now cynically accepted self-centredness and strife. ■

BETWEEN TWO STOOLS

The Philosophy/Physics Theory of Relativistically Instantaneous Action-at-a-Distance

This paper is written in preparation for seminars, on the occasion of the visit to Swansea (from the University of Zacatecas, Mexico) of Professor Andrei Chubykalo. These seminars begin at the University of Wales, Swansea (3.30. p.m., May 14th 1998) with a talk by Prof. Chubykalo on the subject of action-at-a-distance in relation to modern electrodynamics. This is at the kind invitation of Dr. Farzin Deravi of the University's Department of Electronics Engineering.

The theory which has interested Prof. Chubykalo, hence the purpose for his visit to Swansea, is as described below. The intention of the seminars which his talk inaugurates is to continue the ground-breaking task, initiated at Keele University, Staffs. in 1985, of merging the efforts of scientists and philosophers in solving some stubborn mysteries of modern physics.

BETWEEN TWO STOOLS The Philosophy/Physics Theory of Relativistically Instantaneous Action-at-a-Distance

1. Introduction

In 1954 a short correspondence took place between a young Swansea man and Albert Einstein, just before the great man's death. Seven years later, this was reported in the Swansea Evening Post under the title 'Swansea Telephone Lineman's Lines from Einstein' (Philosophical Glimpses 15b.) also in the Herald of Wales (June 13th, 1964) as 'A Mini-Einstein Theory' (Phil. G. 38). The recognition for these ideas which the reporters anticipated at the time has been slow in coming. Now, however, more than forty years on, following a presentation of the theory by its author, Viv Pope, at York University, Toronto, Canada, there have been some interesting developments. One of these is that some physicists have recognised the value of Pope's central theme of relativistically instantaneous action-at-a-distance in solving anomalies in modern electrodynamics. One of these physicists, a Russian Professor, Andrei Chubykalo, from the University of Zacatecas, Mexico, has been invited to the University College of Wales, Swansea, for discussions on this subject. This is with a view to collaborating with Pope and other colleagues, internationally, in exploring further implications of his proposed philosophical unification of modern field-theory along the lines of relativistically instantaneous action-at-a-distance.

2. Academic history

Pope's educational career began, in Swansea, at Gors Road Infants School in 1936. In 1940, he moved to Cwmhwria Junior School, then on to Manselton Secondary School. From there he went, at the age of thirteen, to the Swansea Junior Technical School (JTS) and at sixteen, transferred to Dynefor Grammar School. Distinctly unacademic in attitude and achievement, he was very much a 'free-range' thinker who learned most of his science in scrap-yards when truant from school. In this practical, hands-on way, he became interested in electronics and radio-communication and, by a

natural extension of these interests, eventually in philosophical matters concerned with how bodies interact at great distances. In the Swansea Astronomical Society, of which his father, Don Pope (also a telephone lineman), was a founder member, and for which he (Pope junior) mixed the concrete for the Society's first telescope at Tycoch, he was introduced to the subject of Einstein and Relativity and to the puzzles which that theory notoriously generates. With plenty of ideas, but being almost completely untrained in the ways of presenting them, he married a teacher, his childhood sweetheart who had been a very promising pupil (in fact, an Oxbridge-hopeful) at the Girls' High School Swansea. With her tutelage and some casual help from authorities at University College of Wales, Swansea, e.g., J. R. Jones, Rush Rhees, Howard Mounce (Philosophy), Gareth Evans and J.R. Jones (Maths), Mel Hopkins and P. M. Davidson (Physics), he developed his own commonsense- geometrical alternative to Einstein's theory. It was during visits, on a day-release basis from his lineman's duties with the GPO Engineering Department (now British Telecom), to the university Philosophical Society to which, in the 1960's, he was invited by the then Head of Department, Prof. J. R. Jones, that he learned about modern philosophy from Rush Rhees, the well-known friend of the famous exponent of linguistic analysis, Ludwig Wittgenstein.

Eventually, the Einstein correspondence, which had come to the notice of the papers after its display at an Open Day of the Astronomical Society, led to Pope being advised by Drs. Evans and Hopkins, to develop his ideas in a proper academic setting (to 'come in out of the cold' as Evans put it.) On their recommendation, Pope then became a student at the Workers' Education College, Coleg Harlech, in North Wales. There he was advised, on the strength of his invention of a novel mnemonic in logic, to extend his special-leave sabbatical in order to continue his studies at University College of North Wales, Bangor. This was at the invitation of the Head of the Bangor Philosophy Department, Prof. C.K. Mundle. After obtaining an Honours degree in the History and Philosophy of Science, his employers, the GPO, who had not been sympathetic to the efforts of the Swansea University's authorities to get Pope 'de-industrialised' (his degree was Arts not Science), constructed his dismissal on the grounds, in effect, that he was now overqualified for his previous job of telephone lineman. With no other recourse left to him, having moved his family to live with him

in North Wales, Pope stayed on at college in Bangor to obtain teaching qualifications and to pursue his bid for a higher degree on the basis of his innovative central idea. On the strength of these teaching qualifications, he obtained a position at a college in the Midlands, teaching Liberal Studies (to day-release students) and History and Philosophy of Science in Adult Education. He also taught Logic and History of Science for the Open University.

3. Educational Aims and Objectives

Pope's philosophical aim, as he presents it at conferences, particularly in his capacity as Secretary to a Physics Society in Cambridge, is to end what he calls 'the intellectual apartheid' between science and commonsense. This, he says, is not by any top-down, 'crumbs from the table' condescension on the part of science towards commonsense but by actually involving the latter with the former in a fully integrated 'two- way interactive' partnership.

4. The essence of Pope's ideas

Pope's central idea, which proved extremely unpopular when it first appeared in academic and other circles, is that a fundamental presupposition of Einstein's famous 'Special Theory of Relativity' is false, namely, that light travels at a constant speed in a vacuum. In a vacuum, Pope argues, there is by definition nothing that can serve as a datum of reference for a speed, constant or otherwise, and he demonstrates that all the practical consequences of Einstein's theory can be deduced much more simply without all those references to the 'speed of light in the vacuum' that are usually considered obligatory. When this idea was presented for a higher degree, it was received by Pope's advisers and examiners in much the same way that, in 1615, Galileo's support for Copernicus' theory (that the earth is not the centre of the universe) was received by the Inquisition. Pope's dissertation was, to say the least, vigorously discouraged and his thesis was rejected twice in a situation that was later described by no less a person than the philosopher Sir Karl Popper, as 'scandalous'. (Someone at Bangor intimated to a mutual friend, at the time, 'The Department is having a great deal of trouble over Vivian Pope's M.A. thesis!' (Phil. G., 211)

For Pope, however, all was not totally lost. In 1985 he was invited by Dr. Anthony Osborne, a member of the Maths Department of Keele University, Staffordshire, to develop his thesis under the aegis of the Department as an official research project. It was later suggested, by the Head of Department, Prof. G. Wilks, that the research should be pursued under the proper provisions of a doctoral thesis. When it was pointed out that Pope's first degree was in Arts whereas the Doctorate would have to be in Science. Members of the Philosophy Department at Keele were asked to help but they refused on the grounds that as philosophers, they were 'not competent to judge matters of science' (Phil. G. 4774).

In spite of these setbacks, this rare (some think unique), academically unclassifiable collaboration between Arts and Science at Keele has led to several publications, in prestigious journals, of what has come to be known as the 'Pope-Osborne theory of simultaneity'. The theory has also been presented at numerous international conferences, including the Conseil de l'Europe, Strasbourg (Sec. Burton Mail, Oct. 7, 1986. Phil. G p.3311. also Evening Post, Nov. 1st, 1986 Phil. G p. 3331).

5 The heretical nature of the theory

In 1981, a writer in Australia, Robert Traille, having read of Pope's work, described him to Professor Eric Laithwaite as 'another relevant heretic'. (Phil. G 1713). Traille's description is true. What has damned Pope's thesis as a heresy, in the eyes of the establishment is his advocacy of a complete and fundamental switch away from thinking of space as a great empty room or receptacle in which physical events (quantum events) take place, towards thinking of space and time as having no existence except in these events themselves. 'No events, no space and no time' as Pope puts it. Natural phenomena are thus composed of quanta, similar to the way in which a TV scenario is composed of sequences of little fleeting light-dots or pixels, with nothing of significance to the plot or the action happening in between.

The sort of mental flipover this requires is the one that is foreshadowed, with the coming of computers, from the traditional mechanical-engineering (mechanistic) accounts of natural phenomena to information-theoretical (stochastic) ones - not

4

unlike the switch that is gradually taking place from 'analog' to 'digital' in modern telephone and CD systems. The implications of this conceptual shift, when fully worked out, are every bit as profound and far-reaching - and no doubt, disturbing - as those of Galileo's rejection of the biblical assumption that the earth is the centre of the universe. According to this flip-side, information-based way of thinking, the distance and time measures traditionally described as the 'speed of light' are dimensions informationally projected out of the light as actually seen or instrumentally detected, not those of a primordial vacuum in which the light is supposed to travel like waves in water. This replaces the customary 'constant in a vacuum' interpretation of light with that of a constant in observation where, in projecting the distances of objects, measures in metres have the same constant ratio, c to measures in seconds for all observers in all circumstances - e.g., no matter whether they are moving or stationary with respect to one another. As Pope and his Keele colleague, Dr. Anthony Osborne, have demonstrated many times, this leads to a 'digital' (quantised) derivation of relativity which is much simpler and much less mystifying than Einstein's notoriously abstruse 'analog' version, with its well-known conflict with quantum theory concerning the ways in which bodies interact at a distance.

6. The perceived 'danger' of Pope's thesis

Reactions to this demonstration have been, to say the least, very curious - though perhaps understandable in the light of the following analysis. The fact that the mathematical consequences of Pope's theory and Einstein's are, as commentators have had to concede, mathematically equivalent (or, as it has been described, 'isomorphic') has led, in some cases, to the Pope theory being treated as a merely 'idiosyncratic' version of Einstein's more orthodox and more familiar one, differing, as someone has said, only in the matter of 'metaphysics and/or language', meaning, of course, that it is trivial and surplus to physics requirements. However, that is to avert recognition of - if not deliberately to turn one's back on - the extreme difference in the conceptual implications of the two theories. Indeed, it is scarcely too much to say that the implications of the Pope theory seem to involve an element of danger. For instance, in the early part of the 18th century the gain in scientific objectivity that the Copernican revolution had achieved by removing man from his fancied 'central' role in the system

5

of things was almost completely reversed. This was when the Irish philosopher, Bishop George Berkeley, conceived the notion that 'to be is to be perceived'. The upheaval in thinking that this presented – one might even say threatened – was to remove reality from the world of matter and relocate it in the mind of the observer. The philosophical consequences of this were, not surprisingly, deemed disastrous because, of course, it seemed to imply that everything is just a dream. As the Greek philosopher Protagoras had put it in an earlier version of Berkeley's dictum, 'Such as appears to me is for me, and such as appears to you is for you.' This is an extreme form of relativism which entails, ultimately, that only oneself exists and that all other persons and things are no more than figments of one's own imagination (the view philosophers call 'solipsism'). The danger of this, needless to say, is that of just plain madness.

Unfortunately, to many present-day physicists, 'philosophy' is synonymous with that subjective-idealist theory of Berkeley's. Very few are aware that modern analytic philosophy stems from an outright rejection of Berkeleyan idealism. For instance, in the philosophical journal Mind, 1903, the Cambridge commonsense philosopher, G.E. Moore, wrote a short but powerful essay called the 'Refutation of Idealism' which may fairly claim to have destroyed that form of Idealism for ever. What Moore did was to show that there is no warrant whatsoever for assuming that 'to be is to be perceived'. It obviously is not true in the way that 'A bachelor is an unmarried man' is true, nor is it true in the way that 'The earth orbits the sun' is true. On this analysis, finding no good reason to accept Berkeley's dictum, Moore rightly rejected it, from which time on, in modern philosophy, knowledge of the world reverts to whatever is decided by common consensus, as expressed in evolving common language – not excluding, of course, the language of a properly articulated and authentic science. Moore's commonsense paraphrase of Berkeley's 'To be is to be perceived' would therefore be something like: 'To be is to be whatever commonsense decides, on the basis of ongoing scientific experience and reason'.

Unfortunately, among those who are unaware of these post-Berkeleyan developments in philosophy – which includes, it seems, most modern physicists – the implications of Pope's thesis are summarily rejected as being equivalent to those of Berkeley's dangerous idealism. For instance, as the eminent physicist Jean Paul Vigier

typically expressed it at Toronto, in reaction to Pope's theory: 'This exactly resembles the theory of Bishop Berkeley'. These things, he said, 'have to be settled by the experimental physicists, and no philosophical theory drawn from some philosophical heaven is going to tell us any hard facts' on the subject. (The subject was 'The Present Status of the Quantum Theory of Light', at the first of two biennial symposia in honour of Vigier at York University, Toronto, 1995.)

7. Recent developments

This continued confusion of modern philosophy, in the minds of physicists, with Berkeleyan idealism, and the underlying fear of its solipsistic implications, goes a long way towards explaining their reactionary attitude to Pope's thesis. Why else would they so steadfastly refuse even to consider a perfectly valid logical contribution to the debate about light and the now admittedly flawed orthodox Special Theory of Relativity? The blame lies undoubtedly with the dichotomy that our education system has artificially created between Arts and Science, which has ensured that scientists are as non-competent in matters of philosophy as the Keele philosophers admitted to be in matters of science. In any event, with practically no knowledge of the finer philosophical distinctions, the only alternatives that these physicists tend to recognise are what they see as the polar opposites, Berkeleyan 'idealism' (or 'Positivism' as they sometimes call it) and their own reactionary 'realism' – each of these alternatives being as philosophically stunted as the other. Meanwhile, Philosophy goes on, in its own professionally circumscribed and cloistered way, with little or no effective interest in what Rush Rhees scornfully described as 'scientism'. This, undoubtedly, is why Pope's philosophical contribution to physics has been prevented all along from being objectively evaluated. It continually falls between the widely separated academic stools of Philosophy and Science. In his truancy from formal education, Pope never learned to observe these academic distinctions. Nor, as he says, did Nature. The result is that Pope's extramural, yet essentially natural approach knows no such sectarian division. His ideas are presented, therefore, as one commentator puts it, 'from an Academic no-man's land where he is bound to get shot at from both sides'.

Recently, however, following a second presentation at Toronto, the relevance of Pope's central idea has been recognised, at least within that narrow band of experimental-physics concerns dealing with what is called action-at-a-distance. Seen against the background of conventional relativity, the phenomenon of infinite-speed action-at-a-distance is completely mystifying since it obviously contradicts Einstein's notion that nothing can connect with anything else in less time than it takes for light to pass between them at the 'the finite speed c'. In Pope's mathematically equivalent or 'isomorphic' theory of relativity there is no such mystery because, of course, it is not based on any 'finite speed of light' assumption. This is the point on which Pope's small but growing number of supporters are now agreed, that in his philosophically modified theory of relativity, instantaneous action at a distance and the time-delayed transaction of energy can be seen, without contradiction, as inseparable components of the propagation of that observational information we commonly call 'light'

On the strength of this acceptance of the central aspect of his work, Pope has now been invited, by physicists, to edit and compile a book on the subject of action-at-a-distance and to collaborate in advancing his forty-year-old idea in view of the new practical developments in modern relativistic and quantum physics. Now in his late sixties (his theory was conceived in his early twenties) he is scheduled to re-present his thesis this summer at another international conference at St Petersburg, Russia, and also at both Cambridge and Imperial College, London, where (at the latter) he is a regular participant in biennial conferences sponsored by the British Society for the Philosophy of Science (BSPS).

8. An encapsulation of the theory for physicists

In complete contrast to the conventional teaching of relativity, which is based on Einstein's notion of the constant speed of light relative to the vacuum, Pope's relativity is based on the conception of a quantum state of angular momentum in which fundamental particles are paired and balanced to form instantly extended distributions similar to those of objects in the 'stills' of a cinematographic film, or movie. The change from one such quantum state to another is then an element of time, in the constant ratio c of conventional metres to seconds.

8

The essence of Pope's theory is thus that there is no space apart from these quantum angular momentum states, and no time other than in the discrete quantum jumps between these states. In keeping with the aforementioned revolutionary switch that is taking place from 'analog' to 'digital' in our scientific gathering of information about the world, what determines the occurrence of these quantum events, for Pope, is neither the mechanical certainty of classical physics nor the geometrical determinacy of Einstein's General theory, but probability/improbability, as in information-theoretical (e.g. 'digital') contexts. As he puts it, in this quantum-digital rendering of relativity, the events to which objects and our perceptions of them are reducible are fundamentally the same, so that the observer, even at a distance, is a 'communicator' with nature not a passive bystander

In Pope's theory, the basis of that resonant interaction between particles that is called 'light-interaction' are sequences of those direct and instantaneous distance-extended elements which experimental physicists call quantum non-localities, and the sequences of these non-local elements, in the distance-time ratio of units c, provide the time-delayed aspect of the interconnection which physicists have traditionally interpreted (or, perhaps, misinterpreted) as the 'finite speed of light'. In other words, in its own time (relativistic proper time) every quantum of light-energy connects the light emitter and absorber instantly, in the same way that in the celluloid matrix of a movie-film the objects in the frame are both instantly separated and instantly connected (by the celluloid) at every discrete stage in the action. While this, for the movie, is at the rate of about fifty-frames per second, for physical phenomena it is, of course, millions of billions of quantum connections to the second.

In the case of real physical phenomena the 'cinematic' separation/connection between the elemental objects is, needless to say, not 'celluloid' but angular momentum, in irreducible 'stills' of Planck's constant divided by 2pi. And unlike the stills of a movie, which occur in a determinate and regimented sequence, the physical sequences of quantum stills are indeterminate and unregimented. Indeed, in the Pope theory it is more a case of the real world analysing down to these terminal irreducibles than of its being a phantasmagorical construct of 'underlying realities' in the theoretical tradition of the Greek atomists.

9

In contrast to the underlying substantiveness of conventional 'atoms', the ultimate atomic elements of the Pope theory are, so to speak, epistemologically and ontologically 'hollow'. That is to say, in accordance with the best modern traditions of analytic philosophy, anything that can be sensibly said about these entities has to be said about how they configure in experience in the round (in terms of how the movie-goer sees them, in the 'running of the film', so to speak, not as God might be supposed to perceive them, in themselves, laid out in some behind-the-scenes cosmical 'Cutting Room'. In this philosophically inverted, or heretical way of thinking (see above, section 5), it makes no sense to think of a particle as 'electrically charged', statically 'frizzing away' in the void, waiting to interact with other particles and with the world in general. Nor does it make sense to think of 'the void' or 'the vacuum' as pre-extended and 'ticking away metaphysically' waiting for such events to happen in it. A void is a void. It has, in itself, no bits that are larger or smaller than others, nor bits that are older or younger.

In the Pope theory, then, bodies are not separated by space (void or vacuum). They are separated by action as a film-director might understand it, and where there is no such action there are no physical phenomena to speak of. As in a movie, there are two components to this sort of action, in the absence of either of which no such action can take place. One of these components is the instantaneous distance-separation between the bodies, defined by the matrix of the angular momentum 'still' (the 'celluloid', in the cinematic analogy). The other is the proper-time separation defined by the sequences of these 'stills' in the constant ratio of conventional units c. The typically time-delayed transfer of optical information from A to B, at the rate c is therefore the 'movie'-like result of these two orthogonal components, as described in the Pope-Osborne publications, in which Einstein's notoriously abstruse space-time formulae are derived by plain Pythagoras.

A theory according to which physical interaction at its ultimate quantum level is direct and instantaneous obviously needs no 'fields' or any other sort of mediating agency to facilitate that longitudinal interaction. If correct, the implications of such a theory for the current science of 'electrodynamics' might seem intellectually devastating, were it not that for the theory to be correct, it has to be both mathematically and factually 'isomorphic' with the conventional theory. However, as

we have seen, being 'isomorphic' by no means signifies that the difference is 'trivial' (i.e., merely a matter of mere 'language' and/or 'metaphysics'). In the discussions at Swansea it is hoped that this breakthrough between the academic disciplines, Science and Philosophy that began at Keele will be extended internationally. ■

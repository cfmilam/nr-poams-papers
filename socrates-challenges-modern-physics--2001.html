Socrates Challenges Modern Physics by

Viv Pope of

Socrates is well known for having been executed for asking awkward questions of the Authorities his time. That was in the fifth century B.C. Could that happen nowadays? Here are some suggestions as to how, in challenging modern physics, a reincarnated Socrates might be executed all over again.

Imagine a present-day seminar in which a professor of physics is explaining the fund-amentals

of the subject to Socrates. We enter the conversation where the professor makes the following statement.

professor Every body perseveres in its state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line, except when it is compelled to change that state by forces impressed on it. That's Newton's First Law of Motion from which practically the whole of modern physics stems. Socrates But how could that 'Law' ever have been taken as true when it is plain that nothing travels

in

the way it describes?

Prof. Ah, but you see, that's because there's another law of Newton's which states that all bodies

product

attract one another across space with a gravitational force which is inversely proportional to the product of their masses divided by the square of the distance separating them. That's Newton's Law of

Universal Gravitation.

Socr. And what evidence is there for the existence of this 'gravitational force'? Prof. The evidence is that, as you say, no bodies travel in the way Newton's First Law describes, except in deep space, where the bodies are separated by very large distances.

Socr. But even in the deepest depths of space, bodies are never infinitely far from one another, which means that in space no bodies ever behave in the way that Newton's First Law describes. So, I repeat, how was it ever accepted that free-moving bodies behave in accordance with that 'Law' which is so obviously untrue? Why didn't they accept what Aristotle said, that all force-free motion has to be naturally cyclic or orbital?

Prof. Why does it have to be? Socr. Because, as Aristotle argued, force-free motion in a straight line is impossible, for unless that straight line is of infinite length the motion has to begin somewhere and end somewhere, which requires a starting force and a stopping force. So, even in the ideal case of an infinite length, which is unreal, it nonetheless implies just one straight line. Therefore, as Aristotle concluded, all motion which is naturally true, as truly force-free motion must be, describes a curve. Why, then, did modern physicists, instead of accepting what anyone may easily observe to be true — that is, that motion is naturally curved — accept Newton's First Law, which is plainly false, and then elaborate all sorts of fictional 'forces' and 'fields' to explain away its falseness, instead of Aristotle's view that force-free motion is naturally orbital? Prof. The answer to that is complex.

We would just have a simple description of natural orbital motion, the only 'force' being the real, measured, surface force of mechanical constraint.

Socr. Why, that's precisely it! Instead of describing, as a fiction of gravitational force, the measure of the energy that would result from a body travelling in its natural force-free orbit, which is a real, or actual force, instead of the merely imaginary force which is supposed to prevent bodies from travelling in theoretically straight lines. Prof. Nothing's wrong with that I suppose, except that to invert the traditional way of thinking in the way you suggest would be, to say the least, inconvenient. Think of the upheaval it would create in our systems of education and in society in general. So, whatever you might say about the way modern physics evolved, the fact is that that is the way it did evolve, so now that has to be the way it stays, if only for reasons of social expediency and stability.

Socr. Even if it makes us think that there exist in nature things that are purely fanciful or fictitious?

Prof. Like what? Socr. Like those 'forces' we were talking about, which no-one can perceive or measure.

Prof. But what harm can it do to go along with those fictions, since they are not only so practically useful but are no\v more or iess embedded in the language we use to describe nature. Why not accept them as simply convenient fictions, or working-hypotheses, for the practical purposes ofphysics? Any other consideration has to be the concern ofphilosophers not physicists. Socr. Okay, but if these 'forces' are no more than 'convenient fictions', then so, logically, is the whole mess ofexplanations as to how those'forces; are conveyed across the void from one body to another, by what media, such as 'helds', 'ethers' and so on. So, where does that leave the concerns which modern physicists have about whether that conveyance is instantaneous or time-delayed? Are those legitimate questions for physicists or should they be dismissed, as you say, as questions for philosophy?

Prof. Well, no, I suppose not, since those concerns remain very much at the heart of modern Physics. More and more Physics conferences, I admit, are dedicated to discussing such issues. But how can that be avoided?

Socr. By adopting the Aristotelian interpretation ofnatural force-free motion, how else? Ifyou declare the simple observational truth of the matter, that free-moving bodies orbit one another at a distance depending on the product of their mass and speed in the relation called angular momentum, then you get rid, not only of Newton's imaginary in vacuo 'force of gravity' but also the whole panoply of questions as to how that and other similar 'forces' are conveyed, by what, at what speed and so on.['] All that needs to be said is that the force-free motions of bodies are balanced and correlated in accordance with the measure of their automatically paired angular momentum. Those issues which, as you say, are discussed at more and more Physics conferences are then relegated to Mythology, like discussions among classical scholars about the affairs of the gods in the Greek pantheon.

Prof. Hey, hold on! If students got wind of that, then how would we get them to pass their exams? Their minds would be completely comrpted. It would undermine the authority of the whole Educational system. Teachers of Physics, like myself might as well be sent back to school and departments of Physics might as well be shut down - or turned into departments of practical technology. I can't see how that would be allowed to happen!

Socr. Nor can I. But just for the hell of it, let's follow this conversation where logic leads, eh! Prof. Well alright, just for the hell of it, as you say. But don't count on my voting for it! In any case, your suggestion is purely academic, because physics has moved on since the days of Newton. Now we have Relativity, both Special and General, and Quantum Theory to deal with. Socr. Tell me about it.

Prof. Well, in the first place, we have the fact, of which Newton was unaware, that light travels in the vacuum at a constant speed which is the same for all observers regardless of whether they are stationary or moving relative to one another. The experimental confirmations of this are manifold. Socr. Now let's see if I understand. What you say is that there is a definite speed with which light travels, and that this speed is the same for all observers regardless of how they move relative to one another. But what I want to know is: what is the

constant, and what is it a speed of?

Prof. Well, let me explain in terms of some concrete examples.

In the first place, there is no question of anything

moving of a material kind.

Prof. All right, let me try to explain this in terms of the elegance of the Theory of

Relativity. I realise that most people find this less than easy to grasp, but I shall try to explain.

Socr. Please do.

Prof. Well, firstly, that the speed of light in a constant-rate vacuum is known from very careful measurements by astronomical observers and in the best laboratories with light-rays transmitted over precisely measured distances. These observations have confirmed over and over again

that the speed of light in vacuum is 299,792,458 metres per second, now known as the constant c. And the fact that this truly is a constant is known from further experiments which reveal that this measure, c, is the same no matter how the measurer moves relatively to the source of the light or to other measurers. In other words, all measurers, regardless of how they are situated or how they move relatively to one another, obtain the same precise measure c for light in vacuo. It is simply a plain, all-attested fact of nature that light travels in that way.

Socr. But how does that prove that there is anything travelling in the vacuum, let alone at a speed which is constant relative to that vacuum as well as to everything in it, moving or stationary? A speed, properly so called, is that of a well-defined object, and it differs for different observers with respect to which that object has different motions. Anything not fitting that description cannot properly be described as a speed. So c fails on both counts: it is neither attached to an object nor does it have a speed that differs in the way speeds characteristically do.

Prof. But what else can it possibly be other than a speed, since its dimensions are definitely that of a speed?

Socr. You seem to be saying that because speed has the dimensions of distance divided by time, anything that has the dimensions of distance divided by time has to be a speed? But that's like saying that because all men are human all humans are men. Many women would contest that. Prof. But I repeat, what else can that ratio c of distance to time possibly be, if not a speed?

Socr. A pure dimensional constant, why not? In other words, it seems to me, that what you describe as the'fact'that light in space has a constant speed relative to everything is not afact at all. The pure unembroidered fact is that by pure circumstance, the distances in space we measure in metres and the

times we measure in seconds just happen to be related in that constant ratio of units. Different choices of units produce different constants. If our choices of units had been singularly fortunate, c might have been unity, in which case we would never have any need to talk about the 'speed' of anything 'travelling' in the way you describe.t2l

Prof. But if light doesn't have a speed - if it doesn't travel - then how does it get from A to B in those observations and experiments which show that it does?

Socr. Hey, you're the one with the theories here. I'm the one with the questions.[3] So you tell me, in modern physics, how does light travel from A to B in the vacuum at the speed c?

Prof. In the form of waves. Socr. But in a vacuum what is there to wave?

Prof. I don't think you appreciate the immense subtlety of modern physical theory. Let me put it to

you this way. A consequence of Einstein's Theory of Relativity is the well-confirmed fact that energy is mass and mass is energy in the relation supplied by the well-known formula \(e = mc^2\). If you deny that then you have to deny that all those people died in Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Socr. No, I don't deny that.

Prof. Good! And do you deny that waves have energy? Socr. No, I don't deny that, either.

Prof. Logically, then, if you accept that mass is energy and that waves are energy, then mass is waves. And if that is true, then the reverse is also the case, that waves are masses.

Socr. I pass on that because, strictly speaking, it is not rigorously true, but I concede that it might be. So, please, continue your argument.

Prof. Thank you. Well, anyway, that's how the modern physicists settled it. And in this way it was decided by the Physics community that light-waves are also masses. Add to that the fact discovered by Planck and Einstein that light-energy is quantised in indivisible units of Planck's quantum h and you have the result that light consists of point-like particles, which Einstein called 'photons'. Socr. So that's how modern physicists think of light travelling in the vacuum, between A and B, in the form of wave-particles called photons?

Prof. Basically, yes. The photons travel as particles which, when they strike something, manifest themselves as waves.

Socr. And at what speed do those 'photons' travel?

Prof. At speed c, obviously. socr. But I thought that according to Relativity nothing can travel at that speed. Prof. Nothing except photons.

Socr. But I also understand that according to the Theory, the reason why nothing can travel at that speed is that at that speed its mass would be infinite, so that no force could accelerate it further.

Prof. Yes, that's true.

Socr. Are those photons, then, infinitely massive, since they travel, as you say, at the speed c?

Prof. No, of course not. If you divide their energy by c2 you obtain values for their mass in the order

of 10⁻³⁸ of a kilogram.

Socr. Which is immensely small, by any standard. Prof. As you say, immensely small. Socr. Then forgive me, but isn't that a contradiction? On the one hand you say that anything travelling at the speed c has infinite mass. Then you say that the photon is a particle which travels at that speed, and yet its mass is extremely small. How can modern physicists be happy with that?

Prof. Yes, I see how you are confused. But, you see, modern physicists are not worried about such things, because they learn to accept the facts as they come without philosophising about them. So far as physicists are concerned, nature comes first and logic second. Things normally called 'contradictory' they call 'complementary' That facility of accommodating both A and non-A in the same theory is called the Principle of Complementarity. It is a very powerful tool of modern physics. Socr. But surely you have to haye some explanation as to how a photon travelling at a speed which would give anl,thing else an infinite mass has a mass which is so minuscule!

Prof. Well if an explanation is demanded, then it is that the rest-mass of the photon is zero. Socr. In that case, the photon at any speed has to be zero, since any number of zeros is still zero. But I thought you said that the photon has a mass in the order of 10⁻³⁸ kilogram.

Prof. Well, the truth is that no-one really knows the actual rest-mass of the photon. There are all sorts of estimates as to its value, though none has been agreed upon yet. But modern physicists don't regard that question as vital. Sooner or later, some experimenter will determine the rest-mass of the photon and that'll be that.

Socr. And how is any experimenter going to do that, since the photon is never at rest but always travels at the speed c, which is the same in all systems of reference? I mean, in what possible frame of reference is the photon ever at rest? It seems to me that your 'explanation' is tantamount to explaining the impossible. At one time, to deduce from an argument a result that is impossible was regarded as a refutation of that argument, traditionally called reductio ad absurdum, or reduction to absurdity.

Prof. Not any more. It's just that its realism places undemocratic strictures on the freedom of modern theoreticians if you insist on such irrelevant guidelines. To remove that stricture is precisely what complementarity — the Principle of Complementarity — is for. You see? Socr. So why, then, don't Modern Physicists dispense with theories and models and simply catalogue the observational data with no prejudicial interpretation of any sort? Prof. Now you're talking. Among those who tried it was the Positivist school, to that effect, based on the teachings of the 1930s Vienna Circle — Carnap, Popper, Feigl — philosopher-physicists. For them, observation in itself comes complete with its own intrinsic analytical content, so all ordinary systems of explanation, in terms of our purely subjective ideas and theories, merely interfere with that datum level of sensory-instrumental perception.

Socr. But I see no Modern Physics theories presented, still less pursued, consistently with that Logical Positivist ideal. Why is that?

Prof. Well, I guess it's because no-one has yet discovered how to prosecute that ideal in any way that enables us to make practical predictions. All you get by that method is, at best, a 'natural history' type of schema like that of a naturalist's classification of flora and fauna. But some day, perhaps, all the various bits of physical data will be fed into a computer, which will sort it all out without human intervention.

Socr. Like some magical 'black box', which knows all the answers but which no-one understands. There's no likelihood of that right now, though, is there?

Prof. I guess not! Socr. So in the meantime, Modern Physics remains based very much on the old logical arguments but with a let-out clause, based on that futuristic projection, that where contradictions arise they can be ignored. And the basis of this belief is that in the end, all human logic and language in the description

of things may fade away, leaving bare, untainted cosmic truth. Is not that a rather 'utopian' sort of belief? I confess I don't share implicit faith in bare computation, with the help of computers, to solve problems which have defeated the very best of scientific minds. Besides, with no more immediate prospect of implementation than that of reaching heaven, what does that sort of 'physics' projection offer that is different from what is projected by religion? It's a very similar sort of 'pie in the sky'.

Doesn't any of this disturb you?

Prof. No, why should it? I served my time as a conscientious physics student, qualifying me to teach Physics in hallowed halls such as this. I mean to say, look around you. I think you'll agree, it's better than working down a mine! Socr. Very impressive, I do agree.

Prof. Then why would anyone seek to subvert an institution such as this, unless perhaps out of pique at not having qualified to be a part of it?

Socr. In some cases, no doubt, the motive is pure envy. Many megalomaniacs might fancy themselves another Einstein. But would you entirely exclude the possibility that the motive for subversion might, in some instances, be a conscientious concern for truth?

Prof. Tush! Who knows what 'truth' is? Socr. No-one for sure, perhaps. But we sure as hell know what it

is not! And what is science if it is not a continual winnowing out of ideas we have of nature which can be shown to be erroneous — that is, by the empirical method of experimental observation and the most logical interpretation of the evidence? So there's your answer: truth is simply what remains when you eliminate error. What else, so far as science is concerned, might truth possibly be? Prof. But isn't that what modern physics does, eliminate error in the way you describe? Socr. No, because the signature of error is logical contradiction, and as you say, the 'powerful tool' of complementarity dispenses with that criterion, turning Modern Physics into what some might regard as pure sophistry, hence providing a good and conscientious reason for subverting it.

Prof. But it's all very well to subvert Modern Physics, for reasons however 'conscientious'. But I don't recall reading of any valid alternative. So, as well as telling us what they are against, those subversives of yours should tell us, plainly and succinctly, what they are for.

Socr. What they might well be 'for' is to jettison the sophistic principle of complementarity and reinstate the logical Law of Contradiction, subjecting the whole body of Modern Science to logical scrutiny in terms of that law and then re-mobilising the old principle of Occam's Razor to eliminate theories that are found wanting or else surplus to logical requirements. That way, perhaps we might re-gain a very much clearer glimpse of nature behind all our more and more fanciful notions.

Prof. So why has no-one succeeded in doing that, to any significant extent?

Socr. How do you know they haven't?

Prof. Because if they had, I would have read about it in the journals.

Socr. And what journals would you have read that would give interest to such radical subversion? What Physics department would give it house room? There is, I think, so far as concerns the Modern Physics Establishment, significant radical alternatives, conceivably more efficacious than the orthodoxy, but banished by the current establishment into the wilderness unseen.

Prof. But that's perfectly understandable, surely! To retain their credibility in the physics community, publishers have to produce the goods that physicists demand. To keep their jobs, publishers' referees have to protect their employers from publishing material that is inimical to them, subscribed by those there always are in every generation seeking to knock the existing authorities off their pedestals.

Socr. And where's the scientific justification for pandering

to this purely protectivist concern?

Prof. The justification, as a science spokesman, Polanyi, has pointed out, is, as he says, that there must at all times be a perennially accepted scientific view of the nature of things in the light of which research is conducted by a community of scientists, and that there has to be a strong presumption on behalf of this community that anything counter to it is irrational.

The Educational Black Whole

N. Vivian Pope — 2002

As an ex-student of the Welsh mature-age Liberal Studies College, Coleg Harlech, Viv Pope comments on the decline in free-range philosophy in our modern society.

What is the 'Educational Black Hole'? It is, I suggest, the impenetrable blackness of our current understanding of nature in those areas of modern Physics and Philosophy on which our Western culture is based. It is the universe of discourse that there once was but has now ceased to be, and into the void of which our society is falling at an accelerating rate.

Science and philosophy were once allied in the search for truth and reality, but these words 'truth' and 'reality' mean almost nothing, nowadays. Natural Philosophy, as it once was, is no more. Even the name has been officially withdrawn from our Educational curricula. What is the reason for this catastrophic loss of natural curiosity? Why is it now impossible for Education to cater for the sort of wonder about the world that illuminated the Golden Age of Greece?

The reason is not hard to find. It is that so many of the discoveries that were made in that first flush of unencumbered curiosity have become enshrined as dogmas in our stultifying scholastic traditions. How can the merely circumstantial growth of tradition be expected to produce an understanding of the world that is anything like being logically coherent and consistent? If our philosophical traditions have been in any way organised, it is in sectors of particular interest now called 'the sciences'. Starting separately, these have ended up more and more isolated from one another. In some of these areas, advances have been made that are truly spectacular. But these advances do not hang together. For as many sectors of science as promise to enhance life on this planet there are now as many others that threaten to destroy it.

When our traditions of science fail to gel, it makes nature nonsensical to us. In that case, what else can Science do but embrace that nonsense as expressed in the jargon with which it describes any further ideas and discoveries – and what else can our social institutions do but wallow in the wake of that prestigious nonsense? So, whereas many people think there is a problem in Science's inability to communicate its ideas to the common man, the fact is that scientists find it almost impossible to coordinate ideas in and among themselves. So, while in almost any other human endeavour there is some coordinating 'philosophy' that may be identified, in Science there is no such thing.

The gap left by the Philosophy that was once described as 'the Clearing-House of the sciences', has been filled by politics and general chicanery. But far from being dedicated to developing a global understanding of natural truth and reality, the aim of Politics, and the administration it commands, is simply – well, what else but to be politic. This means, at best, trying to minimise the sorts of risks that attend radically new ideas, and maintain as peaceful a coexistence as possible between warring factions with radically new ideas as to how science should proceed. Politics and philosophy have therefore always been notoriously at odds.

One of the originators of Politics was the Greek, Protagoras, whose sophistic slogan was 'There is no such thing as truth, only opinion.' What should have been seen as the obvious flaw in this dictum was pointed out by his contemporary, the philosopher, Socrates, who responded with 'Is that true or only your opinion?' The Sophists had no answer to that. Nevertheless, as the saying goes: 'I see and approve the better course but follow the worse'. Present indications, therefore, are that it is sophistry, not philosophy, that has won the day – not by any fault of philosophy as such but due to its stultification at the hands of the scholars who graduated with the gowns and funny hats.

In Plato's Academy, all the philosophical issues that had been traded freely prior to his death, were afterwards netted, pinned down and catalogued by his nephew, Speusippus, like butterflies in a collector's cabinet. That 'cabinet' was, virtually, the first University, the institution that still rejoices in the name of 'academia'. From then on, learning was not to be had from nature direct, but from an initiation into and an examinable familiarity with the thoughts of 'The Greats'. Even now, acceptance into this select group still involves the ritual of our having to wear those very silly caps and gowns. (This present writer suffered such an indignity.)

But where else, in a society that rewards this kind of scholasticism with money and status, can this process possibly end but in the confusion we see all around us? For reasons that are as 'right' for politicians as they are wrong in the nature of things, Philosophy has been phased out of the Educational curriculum as being 'non cost-effective'. To a very large extent, philosophers have been themselves to blame for this, by so slavishly sticking to academic traditions of thought which, as it is now plain to see, end nowhere except in what funding authorities see as a sinecure for scholars to do nothing but 'write footnotes to Plato', as someone has said.

But think: how can there possibly be a society without an underlying, shared philosophy? On what basis can a society exist, far less a global one, without some logically deliberated and continually debated consensus view as to what life is and what it is all about? And how can that consensus ever be approached other than on the basis of an administrationally unfettered philosophical dialogue?

Plainly, then, if this planet's expanding population is to survive, the present void, or 'black hole' in our understanding has to be eliminated, and what is now absolutely certain is that none of our current traditions of science, philosophy, politics or religion is capable of fulfilling that requirement. So what are we to do? The only answer, it seems, is to return to philosophical 'Square One', where natural curiosity began, freely and academically unshackled.

Somehow or other, the emphasis, in Education, has to be shifted away from fostering academic conformity towards encouraging the sort of free philosophical dialogue that was commonplace during the Golden Age of Greece. That is to say, we have to be encouraged once more to think for ourselves. This means subjecting every edict of academic authority and its Educational providers to commonsense-logical scrutiny.

Veneration of 'The Greats' that was once accorded to academics now falls more and more to pop stars and sports personalities, and anyone who, for whatever reason, captures the attention of 'the media.' This signifies, perhaps, something of the natural need for freedom from enslavement to a failing academic authority. But it leaves us a long way to go. Film stars, pop idols and sports stars are no wiser than the rest of us, so their successes on the scale of social adulation are no substitute for truly philosophical wisdom. Successful escapism simply sends us faster into the blackness, and it scarcely serves our interests for science to have provided us with more and more drugs capable of anaesthetising us to the pains of realising it. Replacing the uniforms of Authority with the shaven heads and other uniforms of the iconoclastic drug-culture certainly won't save our society.

The ultimate authority has to be ourselves, as intelligent, free-thinking individuals. And if we feel that we are not up to the job, then, at the very least, we must insist that those we entrust with controlling our thinking justify that trust by explaining to us, when questioned, their deliberations in the plainest possible language – that is, without resorting to specialised in-group conceptions and their associated 'blinding with science' jargon.

The 'Catch Twenty-Two' in all this, of course, is that current academic and other conventions are a virtual barrier to the adventurous sorts of sallies that were commonplace among those youthful experimenters-with-ideas who attended the Greek thinking-and-drinking parties, or 'symposia' as they were then called.

Some people might say that the way around this barrier that our evolution is seeking is via the 'Internet' which, up to this present time of writing, has defied all attempts at authoritarian control. This originally had the capacity for releasing us from enslavement to tradition and to the mindless reaction it so often generates, by enabling anyone to present critical ideas for sensible public evaluation, in the way that the stoa in Athens and the soap-boxes in Hyde Park provided the opportunity for anyone with ideas to present them for public attention and consideration.

With the Internet, therefore, all the apparatus was initially in place. All that was needed was the will to exploit the freedom this offered in the way the Greeks did with the apparatuses of their time. Surely, we must see to it, as they did, that the system is not swamped by pure business and commercial interests and sheer frippery, with its commercial 'dumbing-down' of serious philosophical and scientific issues – or worse, with its avenues for the expression of sheer depravity. In other words, we must ensure that the thinking process that started in Athens does not end in a modern 'Babylon' or 'Sodom and Gomorrah'.

For this, however, Education must play its part. Instead of cramming young minds into conformity with teachings that have long become stultified, it needs to encourage pristine, socially uncommitted curiosity and intellectual adventure. This means abandoning its increasing obsession, under the demands of Politics, with the legal and political requirements of curricula, with its incredible 'risk-assessment' oversight of adventurous research, and industry-dictated premiums on 'grades' and 'examinations'.

'Nonsense!', some will say. 'How can we turn our modern infants into Greek philosophers?' However, what alternative is it to turn them into mere fodder for commerce and industry? But, it will be said, the Greeks had the luxury of slave-labour to relieve them of having to do all the important practical work, leaving them the opportunity of being more contemplative. Don't we, though, nowadays, have even more willing slaves in the form of machines and computers to provide that same function? Shouldn't we, then, pass over that mundane function more and more to our mechanical slaves, retaining our mental energies for resuming the philosophical occupation that built our civilization in the first place?

This was an ideal of Education that was, in fact, explored in the early part of the 20th century in the name of 'Liberal Studies'. Like Philosophy, however, this subject was soon phased out, for much the same reason as nowadays, that the custodians of our Educational policies were not philosophers and therefore had little notion of what the subject was about and how, as they saw it, it fitted the here-and-now needs of Industry and Commerce. How it fitted the needs of students as human individuals received scant consideration, especially in the technical colleges and Polytechnics, where the subject was mostly administered under the wary and astigmatic eye of their public investors.

Meanwhile, in colleges dedicated to Liberal Studies, such as, at one time, Coleg Harlech, in North Wales, the subject flourished according to its lights – until the shades of the prison-house closed upon that growing institution. Like the University College of North Wales, Bangor, Coleg Harlech was founded by people like miners, for the purpose of liberating workmen from the tyranny of their industrial masters. Unfortunately, these institutions eventually had to capitulate in the face of materialistic convention (especially after being accidentally burned down in the case of Coleg Harlech, in 1968).

No doubt it was too soon, in retrospect, for that concern for the individual to be introduced into our society against what was then seen as a Gadarene descent of Education into the blackness of vested commercial interest. Some say that money is essential for survival in this modern world. Yes, and so is water, but that is to sustain us not drown us.

In any event, it is now plain that, so far as Modern Education is concerned, what was once 'Athens' has now become 'Rome' – or, perhaps, 'Babylon'. In other words, a deep black hole now yawns in Education, where the sorts of open-ended Arts and Science subjects that were once the life-blood of our universities have been virtually withdrawn from the curriculum. Is this the way it is to be from now on, or is it now time, perhaps, for that experiment in liberal education to be tried again? ■

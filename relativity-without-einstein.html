Relativity Without Einstein

N. Vivian Pope

The essence of what physicists call relativity is the relativity of time. Without understanding the relativity of time, no-one really understands relativity. 'Time is relative' means that there is no absolute 'God's Time' in which everything endures in parallel. It means that instead of bodies being in time, as classically supposed, time is in bodies. This is just plain commonsense, as may be demonstrated.

Two cars set out to travel a hundred miles. Your car travels the whole distance at a steady speed of 50 mph. My car dawdles over the first fifty miles at 25 mph and then, to make up the lost time, speeds up to 75 mph for the next fifty miles. My average speed over the whole hundred miles is the same as your actual speed. However, the effect of varying my speed means that while for you the time taken for the journey is two hours, for me it will be two hours and forty minutes. This reveals the 'Hare and Tortoise' principle, known to the ancient Greeks, that time lost in dawdling cannot be made up by hurrying.

This same commonsense principle applies not only to time lost and gained by cars travelling along roads, it also applies to the time taken by the hands travelling across the faces of clocks. We cannot, of course, read clocks at great distances. However, the ultimate clocks are the optical periods of the atoms of which all things including ourselves and all our clocks are made. The instrument for reading these fundamental clocks, as far away as any telescope may reach, is the spectroscope. This provides a coloured 'bar-code' of the optical periods of the atoms by which the character of a substance is identified. The spectrograph also tells us, by the wholesale shifting of that bar-code with respect to a fixed standard, whether those periods are slowed (reddened) by motion of the atoms away from us or speeded-up (blued) due to motion towards us.

This slowing or quickening of optical periods is due to the fact, discovered by Olaus Römer in 1676, that astronomical distances are times in the constant ratio of 186,000 miles (or 300,000 kilometres) to the second, since known as c. From this minimum of information the relativity of time can be deduced in the following simple way.

Let there be two telescopes fitted with spectroscopes. I take one of those telescopes and travel away from you at a steady speed such that, watching me in your telescope, you see my atomic periods slowed to twice the standard length. (For you, of course, the periods of your own constituent atoms remain at the standard rate.) After one year of my atomic periods (two years in terms of yours) you see me turn around and approach you at the same speed as that with which I left, so as to return in the same time, for me, that I took on my way out. This reversal of my motion is signified by the periods of my constituent atoms relative to yours, being, from then on, halved, so that a year for me is only six months for you.

So, when you and I meet up again, the round trip will have taken, for me, two years, while for you it will have taken two and a half years. If you and I happen to be twins, then the effect of my motion away from you and back will be that at the end of the motion I am half a year younger than you and you are half a year older than I. If it had been you who had undertaken the turning action, then the difference in ageing would have been the other way around. (Only in the case of us both partaking in that reversing action at a prearranged time could we contrive to end up the same age.)

This, in plain commonsense terms, is basic relativity. It takes objects out of time and puts the time into objects. So all we need, in order to understand the relativity of time on which all relativistic physics is based, is an elementary knowledge of the distance/time ratio c and the 'Hare and Tortoise' principle that was known to the ancient Greeks. 'Einstein' need never have come into it! ■

Profile of a New Philosophy

N. Vivian Pope

Profile of a New Philosophy by N.V. Pope The classical view of reality is that 'out there' is a great void, sprinkled with bits of solid matter and pervaded by 'force-fields' of various kinds. This is regarded as the fundamental reality. To this is then added the concept of mind (i.e., awareness, thought and rationality) as being either a separate kind of substance – a 'soul-substance', pervading space like one of the force-fields – or else the occasional product, at various places and under specific conditions, of matter and mindless mechanical processes.

It seems, nowadays, that while this classical view is generally regarded as implausible there is no general certainty as to why, nor any overall confidence as to what other view should rightly take its place. We cannot look to modern physical science for an answer, because modern physical science remains basically in this same mechanistic mould; and we cannot look to modern psychology because that has to complement modern physics.

Besides, it is not the business of these special sciences to remake their common philosophy. That is a task which falls, properly, to philosophy itself. And what does modern philosophy advise regarding this problem? The short answer to that is that right now it is too early to say.

At least, there is, so far, no consensus that an encyclopedist might list as to what, in general, could be considered a satisfactory modern philosophical solution. That is not to say, however, that in modern philosophy there is no solution. It is simply to say that in order to find out whether there is it is necessary to consult with individual philosophers.

That, it seems, is the unavoidably diagnostic stage we are now at in the development of our understanding concerning the most common and most fundamental question of reality. The thesis that bears the title of Normal Realism is one such solution that might be considered.

The extent to which it is representative of modern philosophy generally cannot be stated, for those reasons already given. Suffice it to say that it is by no means unconnected with what other moderns are doing, both in philosophy and in science. The basic theme of this thesis is that the whole of the classical way of thinking is a delusion, that the primary and most fundamental reality is not space, time and mechanical process, nor yet mind or 'soul-substance' but the set of logical (i.e.,linguistic) relations out of which these concepts are constructed.

These are of the kind known immediately in ordinary perception and cognition, the epistemological raw-material out of which are refined, through the process of inculcating and creating language, all concepts of space and time, mind and matter, self and non-self, of processes physical and psychological, of things abstract and concrete, animal, vegetable, mineral, or whatever.

To regard any single one of these concepts as more fundamental than the whole from which it derives is logically absurd, yet that was all too often what the traditional philosophers tried to do – to get 'mind', 'awareness', 'perception', 'language', or something of that kind, to 'lead out beyond itself' to something else more fundamental and substantial, like 'material substance' or some other such metaphysical principle.

So much of traditional philosophy, therefore, was akin to the old search for 'perpetual motion' or for 'the philosophers' stone' – a totally vain and pointless undertaking, like trying to lift oneself up by one's own bootlaces.

To avoid this absurdity occasioned by calling the foundation of all perception and conception 'mind' (as opposed to 'matter') or 'ideas' (as opposed to 'mechanical processes'), or anything of that dichotomous kind, it is necessary to refer to it in such a way as to impose upon it no precept whatsoever.

This the ancient Greeks did by referring to it as the logos, the root of our modern words like 'logical', 'logarithm', and various 'ologies' that signify departments of science. The word logos, in other words, signifies basically no more than 'all those logical and mathematical relations, dimensions and physical measures, etc., that underlie any meaningful employment of language.'

That, at any rate, is what the word logos means in the context of Normal Realism. What else is to be said about it? Well, it is certain that this logos, this logical and mathematical infra-structure of experience, is not absolutely self-evident. That is to say, it is not something that can be grasped completely in the very first act of perception: it is something to be learned as experience progresses.

As such it is something which proves to be of indefinite extent. Man's grasp of it, both individually and corporately, is always finite and imperfect, leaving indefinite scope for improvement. The logos has, in this sense, a cosmical aspect, but not of course in the classical sense of being extended indefinitely in absolute space and time.

Again, unlike the classical cosmos, the logos, no matter how imperfectly grasped, is always immediate (immanent). That is to say, it is not some remote and transcendental principle like the 'material substance' of classical physics, which lies essentially behind and beyond perception, all pointers to its nature being relayed by mechanical processes like light-waves in space and electrical impulses along wires, nerves, etc. The logos is something of which we are aware as prerequisite to such conceptions, and to deny its existence is to deny all sensible discussion.

It is the logos, therefore, not matter, space and time, nor mind, nor any other physical or metaphysical principle, that constitutes the primary and fundamental reality in any analysis whatsoever. This logos, to the extent that it is grasped by human individuals, corresponds to the 'language' of modern linguistic philosophy – that is to say, the whole infra-structure of human experience consisting of those rules, verbal and grammatical, logical, mathematical, dimensional etc., that are embodied in all true commonsense and science.

Everything that displays true logical and mathematical structure – or, at least, hangs together coherently – displays some aspect or other of the logos. Anything that does not lies in the residual category of nonsense, or gibberish. Since it is in this logical infra-structure, or logos, that all physical dimensions and measures, such as mass, distance, duration, motion, energy, charge, force, frequency, etc. and things like atoms, electrons, protons, etc., basically have their being (at least, insofar as they remain viable conceptions), then all those laws and principles which are now referred to as 'laws of nature', like the laws of mechanics and electrodynamics, of thermodynamics and energy conservation, like relativity and quantum theory, etc., should be seen as intrinsic aspects of the logos not as properties of the classically conceived 'physical universe'.

And what about all those physical processes that are governed by these laws? Are these processes real? Does physical causality really occur? Does a physical stimulus act upon our perceptions in the way that physiologists and neurologists attest, or is all physics and physiology etc., mere illusion, like some multi-dimensional 'video' presentation, with all that happens prepared in the logos as on some film or tape?

The answer that Normal Realism gives to that is that physical causality is real, but that it does not take place in any absolute space and time or any other 'universe' conceived after that fashion. The logos is not one such reference-frame, but many – as many, in fact, as there are agents to be considered. There is thus no 'void' of self-sufficient space and time that it makes sense

to discuss. One agent acts finitely upon another, and from the logic (logos) of these interactions is constructed, in sufficiently complex organisms, a picture of how these agents relate to one another in general. The picture thus communicated corresponds to what we call objective reality. To transplant all the trees of modern science into this more fertile philosophical soil might be deemed impracticable at this late stage in their development. But if we cannot uproot a whole forest we can surely transfer a few seeds. The main seeds that Normal Realism deals with in this fashion are those of relativity and quantum theory, whose promises of a deep new understanding have never really flourished outside science. Perhaps, given the right soil for philosphical development, these will provide the general solution we have been looking for to the common question of reality.

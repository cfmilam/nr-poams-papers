Abstract Key words

1. INTRODUCTION It is universally acknowledged that Newton was the first to try to explain gravitational effects in a systematic way. Using his three laws of motion, together with his inverse square law for gravitational attraction, he was able to obtain mathematically, amongst other things, the equations for the orbits of the planets. He was also able to deduce all of Kepler's laws, which had been formulated on the basis purely of observational evidence. In his first law, Newton declared that all inertial, or force-free motion is naturally rectilinear, which implied that orbital motion is unnatural, caused by an invisible ‘gravitational force’ acting in vacuo. His mechanistic philosophy did not allow him to contemplate any holistic or non-local approach to this ‘gravitational’ interaction. For instance, he states, "That one body may act upon another at a distance through a vacuum, without the mediation of anything else, by and through which this action and force may be conveyed from one to another, is to me so great an absurdity, that I believe no man, who has in philosophical matters a competent faculty of thinking, can ever fall into it" However, if a system is supposed to be in a state of overall balance, then that balancing influence cannot be thought of as conducted in Newton’s mechanistic way, as the knock-on effect of one body upon another. A balance can make sense only if conceived holistically. And if that holistic balance is to be maintained, then the motion of each body in the system must be instantly correlated with, or balanced with, that of each and every other, with no question of invisible ‘forces’ being involved. It is well-known that one of the first people to criticise Newton's mechanical-force theory of gravitation was his contemporary, the Irish philosopher and cleric. George Berkeley. Berkeley argued that to make the vacuum a conductor of theoretical "forces" of any kind was nonsense, and he went so far as to hint that perhaps what was involved might be some direct influence of a universal, holistic kind. This argument was developed by Mach at the end of the nineteenth century. Mach formulated a principle which was, in essence that the influence of distant matter in the Universe may account, physically, not only for ‘gravitation’ but also for the local phenomena of inertia, momentum and centrifugal force.

In the physics-philosophy of Mach, the emphasis was placed on observation, as opposed to theoretical speculation, and such was the influence of Mach’s writings that in physics, from then on, the primacy of physical observations began to be emphasised. It was thus insisted that the abstractions from which any model of physical reality may be constructed should be formulated, so far as is possible, in these basically observational terms. Also, since those pioneering writings of Mach, it has become increasingly clear, on experimental grounds, that there is a need for an approach to physics that is both more holistic, or non-local, and more observationally centred. In particular, it has long been evident that holistic laws, such as overall conservation of energy, action, angular momentum, and so on, are at odds with the conclusion of Einstein's theory of relativity, which limits the speed of all physical interaction and information to the finite speed c We address this need for a holistic directness of physical action by accepting as it stands the empirical evidences of so-called ‘action-at-a-distance’ as simply revealing the natural tendency of objects to move and distribute (balance) themselves in instant conformity with the requirements of the various holistic conservation laws. In this way, we address the need that Phipps, Graneau and Assis (4)(5)(6)have stressed, to rethink our ideas of space, time and motion along those more holistic, non-Newtonian lines proposed by Mach. In particular, our approach to gravitation is to postulate that the natural paths of freely moving bodies are not rectilinear but closed, so that all force-free motion is naturally orbital, with no need to postulate any in vacuo forces being responsible for these curvilinear motions. Our hypothesis, counter to that of Newton, is that it is the holistic conservation of angular momentum which keeps these natural paths in balance. This holistic approach to gravitation was first proposed in our 1987 Physics Essays paper (2) and then developed and encapsulated in our second (1989) Physics Essays paper (3). As explained in those papers, this alternative approach calls for a radically different philosophical approach to physics from that of Newton, and the one we have adopted is what we have named ‘normal realism’. In a nutshell, it is an up-dated approach to physics of the observer-centred, or relationist. sort pioneered by Mach. However, whereas Mach’s philosophy emphasised the dependence of science on ‘sense-data’, normal realism emphasises the part played by ‘language’ in the organising of all sense-experience. Thus it rejects the unwanted "idealistic" element in the positivistic, or sense-datum, philosophy of the Vienna Circle which based itself on Mach and which continues to alienate most practically minded physicists (This is the extreme view, generally regarded as nonsensical, that only what is directly perceived at an instant can be real.) By dispensing, then, in the Machian way, with all nonsensical talk of light travelling inscrutably in a self-sufficient void, which isolates objects from our perceptions of them, normal realism seeks, by methods of linguistic analysis, to remove the philosophical problems created by the traditional two-worlds view of matter and perception and sustained by the ‘mechanical intermediary’ view of light and gravitation. In other words, in the continual linguistic process of ascribing existence to things, in normal realism everything begins on a par, basically as items in the language we evolve to adapt and re-adapt our understanding to cope with our experience of things in the world. This includes, of course, what we have learned to think of as matter, space, time, gravitation and light. In normal realism, then, we refer to ‘objects’ our ‘perceptions’ or ‘ideas’ of those objects, the ‘spaces’ between them and even ‘voids’ and ‘vacua’ all in a straightforward, logically adapting and re-adapting, ordinary-language sense. Also, since perception is not self-supporting (as the ‘idealists’, or ‘solipsists’, assumed), we may

sensibly refer to a residual class, or ‘substratum‘ of unperceived and perhaps unknown things, without falling foul of the bogus dispute between the positivists (‘idealists’) and the reactionary antipositivists, or ‘realists’. It is in this ordinary language sense of dealing with things that are ‘relative‘ to perception without being confined to perception that we speak of ‘space’, ‘time’, ‘matter’ and ‘motion’.

2. AN ANGULAR MOMENTUM APPROACH TO ‘GRAVITATION’ In accordance, then, with our normal realist philosophy, the substance of the ‘universe’ does not consist of bits of pure matter or mass floating about self-sufficiently in a void, in the way Democritus originally envisaged. Nor can there be any question of there being any self-sufficient and isolable measures of mass, length and time. In accordance with our relationist approach, we see the substance of the "universe" as an inseparable combination of these measures, mass, length and time in the form of angular momentum. From this point of view, Newton's assumed uninterrupted free motion in a straight line is not physically possible. The straight line path of a free-moving particle has to begin somewhere and end somewhere, which entails interruption; otherwise the straight line would have to be infinitely long, which is not physically realisable. Hence, as Aristotle argued, all continuous free motion has to be cyclic, that is, orbital. The natural measure to use in this context, therefore, is angular momentum. The reason why freely moving bodies behave the way they do, we argue, is that the angular momentum of the whole system is naturally balanced and conserved. For inertial or ‘force-free’ motion in general, we postulate that all bodies seek to move, if allowed, in closed orbits or trajectories with respect to one another, trajectories of the sort we observe in astronomical space. What better examples of this natural balancing of angular momentum examples could there be than, for instance, the spiral galaxies? In Newtonian theory, the total angular momentum of any number of particles in an isolated system is constant. However, conservation of angular momentum alone does not imply that the trajectories of freely moving particles should form Aristotelian closed orbits. This was why Newton had to introduce the notion of an ‘in vacuo’ force of gravitation to explain the natural tendency of all masses to move around one another in closed orbits of constant angular momentum. However, if constant angular momentum does not entail that a trajectory should be closed, then neither does a closed orbit entail the existence of Newton's inverse-square attractive force. In short, then, according to our normal realist approach, we accept the observational evidence as it stands and postulate that all non-interrupted free motion is essentially ‘orbital’‘ (1) and that angular momentum is holistically conserved. The natural orbit of any particle may therefore be thought of as the geodesic described by the particle when all restrictions on its motion are removed, that is, when the particle moves freely under the influence of nothing but its own (angular) momentum or inertia. One reason for taking this approach is that we agree with Hawking when he states, "... if we do discover a complete theory, it should in time be understandable in broad principle by everyone, not just a few scientists."(7) In contrast to Hawking however, our philosophy does not allow us to claim that we would ever "know the mind of God".(7) For example, the earth is in its natural orbit around the sun, so that on earth we cannot detect any (centrifugal) force produced by the orbit. Moreover, according to our approach, the weight with which a body presses on the surface of the earth is not caused by some unseen mysterious ‘pull of gravity’. We simply assume that the body, where it stands, has insufficient angular momentum to orbit freely (weightlessly), so that the force it exerts on a weighing scale

is the force of the reaction by the earth’s surface which prevents it from orbiting where it should, with the angular momentum it has, much closer to the earth's centre. This idea is made explicit in Example 4.1.

This method of dispensing with Newton’s ‘gravitational force’ is also employed, of course, in general relativity. The difference is that in our theory, the natural, or geodesic. path of the body is determined by angular momentum considerations and not by the underlying space-time geometry. This also dispenses with the absurdity of having to think, as in G.R., of the earth’s surface as somehow accelerating upwards under our feet at 32 ft/sec 2. What needs to be stressed before proceeding with this analysis is that it is not our intention to offer a superior way of actually calculating the paths of freely moving bodies. Hence, at this stage, our suggested philosophical departure from the standard Newtonian account of orbital motion might seem trivial. However, this is far from being the case. Our approach can incorporate general relativistic orbits on the macroscale, although in our new departure there is, ultimately, no underlying continuous space-time manifold to conflict with the discovery of quantum discontinuity. Our approach therefore differs significantly from both Newtonian theory and general relativity at the ultimate microphysical level, since on this scale, we suppose that world-lines are as discrete as the particles forming them. So, for us there is no self-extended continuous metrical void. Nor is there any temporal continuity in our world-lines of the kind that characterises the Minkowskian geometrical approach to relativity. We shall demonstrate how our theory applies at the microphysical level, where angular momentum is ultimately quantised in discrete action events of h/2. Thus, by extrapolating our angular momentum approach to the quantum level, we shall indicate the possible redundancy of the traditional ‘field forces’ of Newton, Faraday and Maxwell.

ANGULAR MOMENTUM AND SPIN Recall that in our approach, the total angular momentum of any system is naturally holistically balanced, as opposed to being conserved due to the time-retarded causal interaction between bodies at a distance, as in the classical approach. This commits us to predictions which are distinct from those of other theories and, in particular, Newtonian dynamics. One of these predictions concerns the necessity of including the effect of other forms of angular momentum additional to orbital angular momentum, and, in particular spin. In Newtonian theory, the ‘total‘ angular momentum «J« of an isolated system is given by «J« = «L« + «S«

(5.1)

where «L« is the total orbital angular momentum and «S« is the spin angular momentum. Note that in the case of the two-body problem, J «= «S« in the rest frame of the orbiting body, so that «S« can be thought of as the ‘intrinsic’ angular momentum of the body. Consider once again, the two-body problem consisting of a particle of mass m orbiting a body of mass M. According to Newtonian dynamics applied to rigid bodies, if the particle is spinning, then Newtonian laws have to be applied both to the orbit and the rotation of the particle. In this case, the inverse square law has to be applied to each "elementary piece" of the rotating particle and the problem becomes a many-body problem, which is invariably intractable. It can be shown that if the rotating body is perfectly spherical, it acts like a point mass in applying Newton's laws, so that the situation is the same as in the non-rotating case. In other words, the same orbit is predicted.

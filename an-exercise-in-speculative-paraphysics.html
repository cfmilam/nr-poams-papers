He began with a review of what he had leamed about our modem physics and what, as he perceived it, is its most fundamental unsolved problem. This is the problem of what we call action-at-a-distance, of how distance-separated and dissociated bodies can form an unified and integrated system, or '[niverse'.

According to our mos,t famous modem scientist, Einstein, nothing can travel faster than light. But if that is so, he said, then no physical connection between bodies can take place in less time than it takes for light to travel between them at the finite speed c.

This implies that there can be no pre-extended medium for the conveyance of light-waves, that the waves have to make their own medium as they travel through space, which he considered absurd. Besides, he reminded us, the physical connection between freely orbiting bodies we call angular momentum, in any closed system, is a conserved quantity.

So how can the angular momentum of, say, a galaxy be conserved if the ends of the moment-arms on which its stars and so on balance are separated in the Einsteinian way by thousands of light-years? If Einstein's supposition were correct, then when one of a pair of objects moves, the balancing influence on the other may arrive when the first body is no longer there!

Another consequence of this same 'Einstein separation', which he regarded as absurd, is its splitting of the definitively unsplittable quanta into widely separated beginnings and ends.

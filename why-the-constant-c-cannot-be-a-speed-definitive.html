Why the Constant c Cannot Be a Speed (Definitive)

N. Vivian Pope

Abstract

Herman Bondi and this author, in the 1950s concurred that the constant $c$ in relativistic physics need not be interpreted as a speed, that all the standard consequences of Relativity can be deduced far more simply and efficiently without Einstein's Second Postulate which interprets $c$ as the 'constant speed of light in vacuo'. This paper argues not only that the 'speed' interpretation of $c$ is logically redundant but also that it is false.

1. Why Electricity Cannot Have a Speed

A common belief is that 'Electricity' is a 'flow of electrons' measured in amps, analogous to gallons of water flowing along a pipe. [1] However, in electrical conduction along a wire, no electrons actually 'flow' in that way. Rather, what is ineptly called the 'flow' is a general drift of localised shifts in the orbits of electrons around their atomic nuclei in the direction of the so-called 'current' with none of the electrons travelling all the way.

In this sense, the word 'current' is inappropriate, since there is no flow of electrons in the manner analogous to that of the continuous flow of liquid molecules in what is truly called a 'current'. What actually occurs is a kinematic succession of incipient, limited local movements of electrons in which the electrons travel at very small speeds of only millimetres per second over distances of no more than about $0.0000007$ metres (about the distance of ten atoms). [2]

What is said to 'travel' around the circuit, therefore, are not electrons as such but their charge measured in coulombs. The 'current', measured in amperes is defined as this measure of coulombs per second, hence it is this charge, conveyed in an altogether disembodied way, that is alleged to 'travel' around the circuit at the 'speed of light' $c$. [3] The driving force for these disembodied charges is an electromotive force (e.m.f.). Measured in volts, this is defined as 'the characteristic of any energy source capable of driving electric charge around a circuit.' [4]

We may assume, then, that what is said to 'travel' around the circuit is this disembodied energy, called charge, in ultimate units of $1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ coulomb (the amount of charge ascribed to a single electron).

First, then, let us examine what is commonly known as 'speed'. This, of course, is the measure of motion of a body, being the rate of change in the distance of a body over time. Note that this explicitly involves the idea of an object travelling. Without that, the notion of 'speed' makes no sense. What is it, then, that truly defines a speed?

A commonplace example is as follows. In Figure 1 is a road-test circuit along which road vehicles, manufactured at A, travel from the exit at A around to the parking compound at B on the other side of the factory. At A there is a checkout recording the number of vehicles exiting the depot at short regular intervals. At the far end of the circuit is a swing-bridge which, when opened allows canal barges to pass under, and when closed allows passage over the canal for the vehicles from A into the storage compound at B.

Now it is characteristic of anything having what can truly be called a 'speed' that it can set out from a source and then travel freely as far as it may, regardless of what awaits it at the far end. In our example, if the bridge at B is closed, then the vehicles pass over it and complete the circuit into the compound. If the bridge is open, then they cannot pass but get held up at that point. Meanwhile, regardless of whether the bridge at the far end is open or closed, at the checkout the count of vehicles exiting A at regular intervals into the circuit remains undiminished.

However, if the bridge remains open, then eventually, the steady stream of vehicles produced at A creates, at the open bridge B, a traffic hold-up which backs up to fill the road to its maximum capacity as far as the entry point A. The road is then choc-a-bloc. Nothing moves. Everything is at a standstill. The count of vehicles passing the checkout is reduced to zero. Let us bear in mind that this is what happens with objects that can truly be said to have a speed.

But now let us replace that road circuit with a simple electrical circuit, as in Figure 2. This consists of a loop of electrical wire, a battery with a switch to one side of the battery and any chosen kind of electrical detector on the other. [5]

Now it is to be noted that, as a matter of fact, when the switch at B is open (off) there is no detectable flow of anything analogous to that of the vehicles passing the checkout at A and entering the road in Figure 1. [6] With that switch open, no current whatsoever is recorded by the instrument. Nor is there anything analogous to the build-up of traffic and backing up of vehicles behind those that are held up at that point. Despite the capacity (in farads) of the circuit to accommodate the flow, even with the largest capacitor inserted into the circuit between A and B, with the switch open at B the detector at A reveals not even the smallest, most incipient current passing into the circuit.

So we have the first factual proposition:

i) There is no detectable transient flow. The circuit remains completely empty of charge until the switch at B is closed.

Now this is an observable fact, well known to electrical engineers, and it can be confirmed by anyone in a simple experiment with easily available bits of apparatus to any level of precision that may be desired. However, in contrast to this fact, the next statement is no more than a postulate, namely:

ii) The speed of the conduction of the electricity (the disembodied electrical charge) around the circuit when the switch is closed is the constant speed $c$, of approximately $3 \times 10^8$ metres per second – the same as for light in space.

However, in view of the simple experiment just described, if electricity is said to be conducted at the 'speed $c$', then these two propositions, i) and ii) plainly contradict each other. For the notion of 'speed' to make any sense in the electrical case, the conduction must start, as in the road example, from the one pole of the battery, travel through the detector at that point and on around the circuit, like the vehicles into the road in Figure 1, and finish at either the opposite pole (i.e., with the far-end switch closed) or at the switch if that switch remains open.

However, if we assume, as in ii), that the current 'travels' from A through the detector at that point and on around the circuit at 'speed $c$' towards either the opposite pole – or the switch, as the case may be – then that implies that some flow from A has to be detected at A before it reaches the switch at B, regardless of whether that distant switch is open or closed. But this creates a mystery, because the detector reveals that the current doesn't even 'set out' from A when the far-end switch at B is in the 'off' position. This is no matter how large the length and capacitance of the conductor, even if it be, say, a telephone wire of length measured in hundreds or thousands of kilometres.

Thus, if we think of the current as 'travelling', then we have to assume that the current somehow 'knows', ahead of time, that the switch at the far end, B, is open or closed, before 'setting out' from A. In that case, the effect, detected at A, of the closing of the switch at B miraculously precedes that event by 3.3 nanoseconds for every metre length of the conductor. This implies a reversal of the natural order of causality, and hence reveals the absurdity of the assumption that the distance-time rate, $c$, of electric conduction is a 'speed'. [7]

2. The Alleged 'Speed of Light in Space'

To think, then, of electrical conduction as having a 'speed' creates nonsense. However, as soon as that 'speed' interpretation of 'electricity' is abandoned, that absurdity disappears and the situation makes perfect sense.

For instance, let us ask ourselves what it is that is measured in the circuit as 'current'. As we have seen, it is not a continuous flow of electrons. Indeed, it cannot be the 'speed' of anything either corporeal or quasi-corporeal. [8] What else, then, can that 'current' be but a measure of pure disembodied energy, the same as is measured in the form of light between points A and B in space.

Moreover, according to the standard classical equation for kinetic energy, we have $e = \tfrac{1}{2}mV^2$, where $m$ is the mass of the moving body and $V$ is its (Newtonian) velocity. From this it follows that:

V = \sqrt{$\frac{2e}{m}} \tag{1}

For any transport of pure energy that does not involve a travelling mass, that is, for $m = 0$ in (1), its proper-speed (i.e., Newtonian speed $V = s/t$) is infinite (that is, with $t$ in this expression equal to zero). And with that proper-time 'speed' of transition being infinite, its relative 'speed' $s/t_R$ is the finite rate $c$, according to the following simplified version of the relativistic time-formula: [9]

t_R = \sqrt{\left($\frac{s}{c}\right)^2 + t^2} \tag{2}

Note that this formula (2) is essentially the same as the Einsteinian time formula, except that $c$, in this derivation, is not the 'speed of light' but is simply what Herman Bondi (see following) calls a dimensional distance-time conversion factor.

This formula (2) tells us, in effect, that in the case of $c$, every quantum of the energy that 'starts out' from A 'arrives' instantly at B; that is, in its own, intrinsic, quantum proper-time $t = 0$ in the formula. In that case, the relative or observationally delayed time $t_R$ is $s/c$, whence $s/t_R$ is $c$ for any disembodied energy such as electricity or light.

This is why the quanta that are 'poised', as it were, to leave the higher-energy source, at A, seem to 'know ahead of time', whether or not to 'set out'. It is because with their 'transit-time' being instantaneous they are, in a definite sense, at both places at once, which is not a speed but literally a Lewisian proper-time-instantaneous 'quantum touching'. [10]

This means that in the time-frame of the observer locale (see Figure 2) despite the proper-time-instantaneous (Lewisian) connection $t = 0$ between the source A and the sink B, the detector at A gives its reading after the closing of the switch at B, by an interval $t_R$ which, according to the equation is the length $s$ of the circuit in metres divided by $c$. This reinstates the normal sequence of cause and effect by putting the effect after the cause, hence avoiding the absurdity of reversed causality that is created by interpreting $c$ as a 'speed'.

Note that for $t = 0$ in (2), $c$ is the observationally time-delayed contact between source and sink, as manifest in all optical experiments carried out in vacuo. But although $c$ has the dimensions of a speed it cannot really be a speed because if so, then we have the absurdity that the 'speed' of the current around the circuit is in a direction opposite to that of the signal.

Otherwise, as a pure constant, this non-speed interpretation of $c$, as expressed in the formula, satisfies perfectly the requirements of both Relativity and Quantum Theory according to which, when the proper time (quantum time) $t$ of the contact between A and B is instantaneous (i.e., when the quantum distance-time jump $t$ is zero), the observer-time (relative time) $t_R$ of that same jump is the ordinary delayed-time $s/c$.

However, it needs to be stressed that despite this making the signal appear to have this 'speed', that time-delay, over the distance $s$ is not really a speed but a pure ratio of conventional units of metres and seconds in the measuring of distance.

What this argument proves, then, is that neither in the case of light or electricity can $c$ truly be the speed of anything corporeal. Our electrical example gives witness to this in proving that a so-called 'electric current' purported to be 'travelling at speed $c$' is not truly a current of anything, since nothing travels in any manner which is consistent with that definition. The constant $c$ in this case is no more than a pure distance-time ratio. For, again, according to Relativity, anything corporeal travelling at the 'speed $c$' would be infinitely massive, which is obviously not the case with either electricity or light.

3. Why Light Cannot Have a Speed

Light and electricity are, of course, very different. There is little similarity, for instance, between the way in which electricity is conducted in a circuit and the way in which light is conducted in space or in, say, an optic fibre. So is it possible that $c$ can be a non-speed in the case of electricity and not in the case of light? Implausible though that may seem, it is definitely possible, since proving that $c$ is a non-speed in the one case does not prove, logically, that it is a non-speed in the other. [11]

Unfortunately, the sort of experiment with electric circuitry so far described cannot be carried out in the same way with light. However, since, like electricity, the transport of light cannot involve a travelling mass, its proper-speed $V$ according to the Newtonian equation (1), for $m = 0$, is the same for light as for electricity, that is, infinite. This has the same consequence for both light and electricity, namely, that the relative 'speed' $s/t_R$ is $c$, according to the equation (2) and as experiment attests in both cases.

So the question remains as to whether there is some practical way of proving that $c$ is not a 'speed' in the case of light in the same way that is proved with electricity.

Let us look once again at the ordinary commonsense meaning of 'speed'. In any commonsense conception of 'speed' there can be no such thing as a speed of anything which is the same for different observers in different states of relative motion and rest. According to that commonsense criterion, any 'speed' that is the same for all relatively moving observers cannot truly be called a speed. But experiment reveals that light is the same for all such observers. [12]

This is undoubtedly what accounts for the notorious difficulty experienced by commonsense thinkers in trying to understand Einsteinian Relativity in which it is axiomatic that $c$ is both a universal constant and a 'speed'. Besides, any suggestion that $c$ is a constant speed relative to a vacuum, as in the second axiom of Einstein's Special Theory of Relativity, is meaningless when we consider that a vacuum means a void, that is, literally a nothing. So to say that $c$ is a speed which is a constant relative to a vacuum means that it is a speed which is constant relative to nothing, which, for the likes of logical thinkers like Aristotle, is complete nonsense. [13]

If we pocket our precepts about light we may see that what nature is trying to tell us with these experiments is that something which, like $c$, is constant for all observers in all sorts of different states of motion and rest is no more than just that, a constant, just like any other constant, such as the number of inches to the foot or the number of pounds to the kilogram. As Bondi says:

Any attempt to measure the velocity of light is … not an attempt at measuring the velocity of light but an attempt at ascertaining the length of the standard metre in Paris in terms of time units. [14]

Bondi and Pope have shown, independently, and by mutual agreement, [15] that all the practical consequences of Einstein's Special Theory of Relativity [16] can be deduced far more simply without Einstein's Second Postulate regarding the alleged 'velocity of light in vacuo'. [17] [18]

What our arguments prove, therefore, is no more than what pure logic tells us about the transport of quantum energy. This is that quanta, in the case of any such transportation, of electricity, light, radio or whatever, have to be instantaneous (i.e., proper-time-instantaneous) otherwise there is a time-lapse between a quantum 'leaving' a source-atom and being consummated at a distant atom, which the law of conservation of energy cannot allow. This is a somewhat neglected consequence of The Special Theory of Relativity, which Gilbert Lewis demonstrated just twenty-one years after that theory's first appearance in 1905. [10]

Here, then, we have the prospect of a practical proof, in the case of light, of what has already been proved in the case of the electricity, that quantum energy cannot 'start out' from a source if the conditions for its 'reception' at a distance are not instantly assured. In the case of light, this can be seen in the well-known Thomas Young two-slit interference experiment. This shows that the quanta 'avoid' those areas on the final screen where their frequencies, if they 'landed', would be out-of-phase with one another and 'steer', instead, in greater numbers for those areas where they would be in-phase. This is in such a way as to conserve, as though by 'telepathy' (from the 'speed of light' point of view), the overall spread of illumination at the distant end.

Logically, this is what should happen in the electrical example if switches were inserted in the circuit at A and B, respectively, in Figure 2, and designed to vibrate on-and-off at a fixed frequency. There would be increasing distances $s/c$ along the conductor at which the vibration periods of the two switches would be alternately in- and out-of-phase with the conductor-length, thus alternately allowing and inhibiting quantum transfer in the same way that is observed in the two-slit experiment.

In neither of these cases, then, with light or electricity, can the energy 'travel' in the way we think of ordinary objects travelling, that is, setting out on some course in the vague hope of sometime in the future meeting either an entry or a barrier – or, perhaps, going on forever, meeting nothing at all. The law of conservation demands that every quantum of interaction has to be an instantly consummated, double-ended affair in a proper-time $t = 0$ according to the relativistic equation (2), a one-ended quantum jump being as impossible to conceive as a one-ended coil of wire.

The electrical demonstration, then, verifies our logical contention that $c$ cannot be a 'speed' or 'velocity' of anything, that it is no more than an observational time-sequence of pure at-a-distance instantaneous quantum correlations between atoms in the manner of the well-known 'direct action' theory of Feynman and Wheeler. [19]

The advantage of this particular electrical demonstration of what might be called quantum teleblocking is that the quantum interactions can be confined within an electrical wire in a way which they cannot be in, for example, the two-slit experiment. It is a moot point as to whether that same teleblocking effect might be achieved by confining light within some channel such as a laser beam or optic fibre. However, that is a matter beyond the writ of this present paper, which is simply to prove that $c$ cannot be a velocity of anything, whether it be electricity, light or whatever. Relativity confirms this insofar as in that theory, $c$ is not the velocity of anything but is simply the asymptotic, hence unreachable, dimensional upper limit of all true relative velocities.

As Bondi and Pope have concurred, whether $c$ is interpreted as a 'velocity' or just a constant, makes not the slightest difference to the relativistic equations – except for an altogether massive simplification of their conceptual formalism. [20]

However, to assume that this makes no practical difference for physics would be like the Ptolemaic astronomers assuming that the Copernican heliocentric interpretation of planetary motions made no practical difference to the future of astronomy and astronautics. [21]

Finally, a point of pure logic regarding claims that light has been seen and/or photographed travelling in vacuo in the way Einstein's second axiom asserts. This is clearly an impossibility. For anything to be seen or otherwise detected travelling in vacuo, it must either emit or reflect light. This means that in order to be seen travelling in the vacuum of space light would have to produce or reproduce light. And in that same way, for that secondary light to be seen it would give off light, and that tertiary light would have to give off light … and so on to infinity, creating a logical reductio ad absurdum. Interpreting $c$ simply as a dimensional constant avoids this absurdity.

Conclusion

All these considerations taken together, both logical and empirical, testify to the fact that the constant $c$ cannot be the 'speed' of anything 'travelling' between the sources and sinks of either light or electricity. In both cases the transfer, at the quantum level, is proper-time instantaneous in accordance, not only with the relativistic formulae but also with the law of action-conservation. The time-delay which is usually associated with these phenomena is not a feature of the quanta themselves but only of their time-sequences in relativistic (macroscopic) observation. [22] [23]

Now some critics baulk at the suggestion that quantum interaction can be both instantaneous and time-delayed. They see this as a logical contradiction. There is, however, no such contradiction in conceiving an interaction having both instantaneous and time-delayed components. The action in a movie testifies to this. In a movie, the instantaneous connection between objects in the 'stills' (still frames) is analogous to the 'instantaneous' (or quantum) component of atomic interaction, $t$, in (2), and the analogy of the observationally time-delayed component $t_R$, is the sequencing of those stills in the running of the film. Just this one homely example is a logically sufficient refutation of any idea that quantum instantaneity and relativistic time-delay are contradictory. The very fact that these two components are integral parts of the same relativistic equation gives the lie to that notion of contradiction.

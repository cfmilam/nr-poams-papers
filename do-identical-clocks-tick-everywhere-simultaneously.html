Do Identical Clocks Tick Everywhere Simultaneously

Viv Pope — Galilean Electrodynamics, 2014

Do Identical Clocks Tick Everywhere Simultaneously? Viv Pope ‘Llys Alaw’, 10 West End, Penclawdd, Swansea, SA4 3YX, UK. e-mail: pope@nv2473180.freeserve.co.uk Website: www.poams.org

Consider the statement “Identical clocks tick simultaneously.” Is that true? Obviously not. To tick simultaneously, identical clocks must be synchronised. Is it true, then, that when synchronised, all identical clocks tick simultaneously? Yes, that’s true, just so long as we are clear as to what we mean, here, by “synchronised”. So, what does “synchronise” mean in this context? What else can it mean but putting those identical clocks into close proximity with one another and setting them all to run together in unison. But now let us ask whether one of those identical, properly regulated and synchronised clocks continues to tick in unison with all the others if it moves apart from them to some large distance $s$ in space. Well, it has to, say some, because in that separation, nothing has changed. Ah, but something has changed. What has changed is that a distance in metres now separates that clock from all the others, and that distance in metres, as viewed from the standpoint of those other clocks, is a measure of time in the ratio of units $c$. This means that as seen in the telescope of someone standing in that community of relatively close-together, identical and synchronised clocks, that distant clock is no longer ticking in unison with them, due to the interposing of that measure of distance-time. And what is that measure of distance-time? Surely, someone will say, it is the time-interval $s/c$, which is recoverable on the clock’s return to join up with all the others. But no, that cannot be true. The change in distance s/c does not occur by magic. It has to take place in some time t ticked by the travelling clock. This means that relatively to the observer-system (the home clock-group) the time t and the distance-time s/c are orthogonal dimensional components [1] of a resultant time tR according to the following Pythagorean relation:

t_R = \sqrt{t^2 + (s/c)^2} \qquad (1)

\qquad (1).

So, as seen in the telescope of the home system, and judged in that home time, the distance-time that interposes between the beginning of the motion and its end at the distance $s$ will not be just s/c but $s/c$ plus the difference $t_R - t$. So, what happens when that clock returns to rejoin the group? Whilst standing at the distance $s$ away it was seen in the home telescope ticking standard ticks $t$ but lagging behind all the others by an interval $t_R$ - t + $s/c$. When it arrives home, will it have regained that lost distance-time and from then on resume ticking in unison with the rest, as though nothing had happened? No, because in the time of the home observer, the distant clock that has ticked an interval t in its own time (which is $t_R$ plus $s/c$ in the time of the home observer), ticks, again in its own time, a further interval $t$ that is $t_R$ minus $s/c$ in the time of the home observer. So, for the time of the separation that for the travelling clock is $2t$ overall, the overall time registered by the home clocks is $2t_R$ + $s/c$ – $s/c$, which is, of course, $2t_R$. So if t is one year and if the distance travelled by the clock in that one year is, say, $9.460528 \times 10^{15}$ metres (one light-year), then when the clock returns after a time that it logs as 2 years, the time logged by the home observer for that same journey will be, from (1), 2.82842 years – i.e., 2tR. Any idea that despite these on-the-spot differences in the durations of the moving and the stationary clocks, there is some hidden ‘level of reality’ in which, in some presumed ‘God’s-eye-view’ way, all the various identically synchronised clocks continue, everywhere, to tick in unison, is just pure nonsense. This differential-ageing result is the same as what is called ‘time-dilation’ in orthodox relativity. This can be seen if, for s in the Pythagorean equation, (1) we substitute the equivalent $vt_R$, where v is the relative velocity s/tR, and then simplify, which produces the standard relativistic gamma relation:

$\frac{t_R}{t} = $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} \qquad (2)

\qquad (2).

It is to be noted that this formula has been derived, here, independently of Einstein, Lorentz, Minkowski, Fitzgerald, or any of those other names we customarily associate with ‘Relativity’. It may also be noted that it involves no distant synchronising of clocks by means of light-signals, nor any dynamical concepts such as those of acceleration [2], gravitation or the like [3]. The whole deduction is made in terms of the barest minimum of purely observational (i.e. kinematical or geometrotemporal) measures, requiring no theories whatsoever. This parsimoniously logical derivation of relativity seems to work without any reference to the usual scholarly sources. Could it possibly be, then – perish the thought – that all those renowned scientific geniuses we associate with ‘Relativity’ have, albeit unintentionally, ‘blinded us with science’? Copernicus might have protested, against the mathematically intricate world-view of his time, the system of Ptolemy – the medieval ‘Einstein’ – by asking: “Why can’t you guys just set aside all your mathematical ‘wheels within wheels’ with all their associated theoretical paraphernalia and rely on just plain observation and commonsense?” Let’s face it, if something is objectively true, then anyone may discover that same thing, independently and in his own way. After all, as it is said, ‘All the keys hang not at one man’s girdle.’

However, the whole (Gedanken) exercise could be replicated without involving any question of acceleration. This would be by replacing the single out-and-back clock with two clocks moving steadily in opposite directions from outside the system. As one of these clocks passes the home system of clocks, it synchronises its ticks with them in the same proximity as they have with one another. It then continues outwards at its steady speed. The second clock, passing that outgoing clock at the distance $s$/c, moving in the opposite direction but at the same speed with respect to the home system, synchronises its ticks in proximity with the outgoing clock – receiving the time-baton, as it were.

Dear Viv

I have some questions/arguments that have arisen from our NRIG meetings and would be grateful if you could give them your consideration.

Discrete-time universe

When I suggested (many weeks ago) that a discrete time universe might flash in and out of existence and thus raise questions of why there should be any continuity such as causation and "laws" of physics, you said that things have different durations so something always exists. This answer satisfied me at the time but on deeper reflection, my question was ill phrased and my thoughts on time are still up in the air. I think I was conceiving of a "God" time, which ticked by at some irreducible rate, rather than time being something we experience in a relativistic way as the statistical effect of many quantum interactions. My trouble is in thinking about these quantum interactions, which happen in a realm where distance and time are meaningless, as actually occurring without time. Time is only useful as a measure when there is some change of state in existence. If there is no change, there is no time. A quantum interaction is a change of state, so it must signify some time. Am I guilty here of "change implies time therefore time implies change"? Is one quantum interaction sufficient to claim that time has ticked? Is time only a measure on the super atomic level? I've always had trouble connecting important dates and appointments with my day to day existence and when I'm engaged in an absorbing activity, I have no concept of time but now I can't even be sure of what time is let alone what time it is (05:00 and I can't sleep) – help!

Red shift without motion

In a previous talk, you referred to experimental evidence that motion of a radiating emitter away from an observer results in a red shift of the radiation as perceived by the observer. You then pointed out that red-shifted observation of distant objects does not imply motion. This causes me to keep an open mind about theories of an expanding universe. During our most recent meeting, you claimed that some red shift is the result of objects being distant from, rather than in relative motion to, the observer. Is there experimental evidence for this claim? If not, the expanding universe seems to be the best theory to date based on observation. I am in no way a "big banger" and I am by no means convinced that an expanding universe implies that it originated at a point but I fail to see why you ridicule this theory so. You ask what there was for the universe to expand into but that is like asking what is outside the universe now. Maybe questions of creation are best avoided in a paradigm that eschews theories, but in taking a stand against much of current mainstream physics, you will be drawn into arguments over such things. People will not give up a perfectly satisfying theory of creation, even if you point out the logically inadmissible steps in its reasoning, unless there is an alternative that is at least as good. I'm not much bothered about the creation of the universe and doubt whether observation will ever settle the arguments but I would value a clarification of your objections to current theory and faith in any alternative.

Free will only possible in a stochastic universe and implications for morality.

You state that there can be no morality in a deterministic universe because none of the agents have free will, but that in a stochastic universe, there is a possibility of free will and thus morality. I think that you are trading on people's fears that they are not in control of their own destinies and the fear that belief in determinism will result in immoral behaviour to promote the stochastic paradigm over the deterministic. I know people who think that their lives are determined from birth but still have a mainstream moral sense and behave accordingly. There is no reason that the vast majority of people would not carry on behaving in the same way as they do now even if it were demonstrated to their satisfaction that the world was deterministic. The argument that there can be no free will in a deterministic universe is sound but there is no more free will in a stochastic universe. The flow of a raindrop down a windowpane is stochastic but there is no suggestion that the raindrop has free will. My conception of human minds is as functions of human brains, which are, on a molecular level, as mindless as a raindrop. If we start saying that mind is anything other that physical (biological) processes, we tread a path to Cartesian dualism. The stochastic nature of the universe combined with the staggering complexity of the human brain and the inconceivable number of environmental interactions that shape and sustain the human mind render the illusion of free will. This illusion might equally come about in a deterministic universe.

Morality can be thought of as an evolutionary adaptation that I necessary or beneficial to social animals with our kind intelligence. It is a practical function of the brain rather than some property of things in the universe such as right/wrong or good/evil. The ubiquitous nature of certain moral codes is testament to the common heritage of humans rather than the independent existence of such codes. If free will and morality are conceived of in these ways, the stochastic/deterministic argument has nothing to say about them. I am keen to hear your thoughts on the above accusation, standpoint and argument.

Bodies falling down shafts.

VIV ANSWERS:

Dear Nick,

Thanks for your questions.

1. Discrete-time universe

Yes, it is a conundrum to think of time-sequences of quantum interactions in a realm where distance and time are meaningless. It sends the mind round and round. No wonder you can't sleep thinking about it!

What has got you into that mental loop was in thinking. as you say, of "time being the effect of many quantum interactions" (my underlining). The word 'effect', here, is your bogey. The way out of that loop is to remind yourself that there is just no way in which, realistically, you can get down among those ultimate quanta with a clock and a clipboard to check off which of those quanta affect one another and in what time so as to produce on that microscale what we call time on the macroscale. That is pure metaphysics, The only time anyone can sensibly contemplate is that which is measured on the level of ordinary macrophysical processes. That's where all realistic scientific enquiry starts. To assume that there is any scientific way of setting aside all one's ordinary knowledge of time to study these quanta like some mystical micro-demon and then work out, from your clipboard record of their occurrences, how that process leads to time as we know it, is a way of thinking that Normal Realism rejects as metaphysical. In Normal Realism, time is an essential given. In logical language it is epistemologically a priori, and the quantum elements to which it reduces, on analysis are epistemologically a posteriori. To reverse those priorities in one's approach to knowledge is sure to give you chronic insomnia.

2. Red shift without motion

The question of 'experimental evidence' for cosmological theories is a tricky one. The objects concerned are so inaccessible to us that to consider any prospect of 'experimenting' with them, in any sensible meaning of that word, is obviously out of the question.

Now I didn't say that "red shifted observation of distant objects does not imply motion." What I said was that it did not necessarily imply recessional motion. All sorts of random motions can cause redshifts – as, for instance, in relativistic time-dilation (see my answer to Hefin's question as distributed by Penny).

You say you are not convinced that an expanding universe implies that it originated from a point. But how else could it be if the expansion is held to be omnidirectional and continuous? Besides, that is the explanation the Big Bang Creationists claim for their theory, that it all originated from a point; and that is the hypothesis I held in question.

But you want a clarification of my objection to that current theory and of 'my faith in any alternative'. I thought that in the POAMS literature and in my recent book I had already answered this as clearly as I could. (repeated in my answer to Hefin). The details of all this are still there to be studied. You may not have read them yet, of course, but I'm sure you don't expect me to repeat all that in full detail in this short space here. Let's just say, then, that the agenda, so far as POAMS is concerned, is not to prove the expansion theory wrong, which, as I say, would be impossible 'experimentally' on those scales of distance in question. It is to prove – which it definitely does – that the 'expansion' interpretation of the Hubble redshift is not the only logical alternative there is. An argument that selects only one of two or more logical alternatives and arbitrarily excludes all others is a dogma, by definition. And this is what POAMS proves, that the 'expansion' theory is such a dogma. For instance, in propositional logic, 'P implies Q' does not necessarily imply 'Q implies P', hence to say 'P implies Q, therefore Q implies P' is a logical fallacy. For example, if I were to say 'All Chinamen are humans', you would then readily see what a fallacy it would be for me to infer from this that 'All humans are Chinamen'. In the same way, to infer, as do the Big Bang theorists, from 'All recessional motion implies redshifts', that 'All redshifts imply recessional motion', is to commit a fallacy – a logical 'howler', in fact.

3. Free will only possible in a stochastic universe and implications for morality.

Your phrases "trading on" and "fear of" are strange ones to use in relation to a logical thesis, especially one which stresses its disinterestedness in emotional and sentimental evaluations. Please remember that Normal Realism is such a logical thesis; that is to say, it is philosophy, not politics or religion. It expressly has nothing to do with preferences of any kind. It simply points out the truism that automatism and free will are antithetical. That is to say, they mutually contradict one another. There is no more emotion or sentiment involved in that statement than there is in a mathematical equation.

This doesn't mean that you won't find people who, as you say, believe in both of these contradictory propositions. There was someone I once knew who stood as a Liberal candidate but whose affiliations were to Oswald Mosley and the Fascists. And there is someone in my family who is a radical socialist whilst also being a member of the Freemasons. People are like that. No-one has to be logical, And there may be, as you say, good psychological, social and pragmatical – even evolutionary – reasons for that. But the mere fact that there are people who are not logical does not negate the power of logic any more than the fact that there are people who can't do maths negates the power of mathematics. You surely wouldn't condemn mathematics, as applied, say, in engineering and astronomy on the grounds that there are people who don't understand maths! The same with Normal Realism, which uses logic – and mathematics – for purposes of scientific philosophy, not for any pragmatic sociological or political purpose. And what the logic of Normal Realism reveals is that to argue for true freewill and morality in a universe which is assumed purely mechanical, while it is not pragmatically impossible, involves such highly contrived and tortuous rationalisation that it verges on the ridiculous. (I know; I had to study these strange arguments at university!)

Anyway, you say you agree that the argument is sound that there can be no free will in a determinate universe. But then you say that there is no free will in a stochastic universe, either. Well no, if, as you say, at the molecular level, our brains are 'as mindless as a raindrop'. But that echoes your first point, which was that since in a stochastic universe quanta are timeless, ordinary phenomena are as timeless as a quantum. So in answer to this present point I can only paraphrase what I answered to your first point, which is that the way out of that insomniac loop is to remind yourself that there is no realistic way in which you can get down among those mindless brain-molecules with a clock and a clipboard to check off which of those molecules affect one another so as to produce what we ordinarily experience as free will. In the same way as with time, free will, on the ordinary level, is an irreducible phenomenological given. The very fact that we are arguing, now, in this free-minded way, is sufficient testimony to that. I can't think that it is our brain-molecules that are doing this arguing. Nor can I think of any way in which mindless brain molecules, automatically and in themselves, can bring together people from perhaps all over the world to attend, say, a wedding, and for those same brain molecules to coordinate mechanically with the molecular ingredients of the wedding cake to appear just in time for the ceremony. And what about all the other intricately interconnected and necessary effects at every stage in the process of planning and convening the wedding? If this is not done by human free will, then it would have to assume some mystical collusion among the molecules of such things as the ships, planes, cars and so on, plus the molecules in the brains employed in organising the production of the goods, their transport, the economy ... and so on. If anyone insists that there is no real mental volition involved in this process, that it is 'an illusion created by the mechanical actions of mindless molecules', then what could I say to such a person but "Goodbye!"

As for your point about morality. POAMS says nothing prescriptive about that, except to point out that if free will is an illusion, then so is morality – or, at least, it has to be something purely pragmatical along the lines you suggest. However, if free will is not illusory but is real, then so might be morality as religious people see it. But Normal Realism is science-philosophy, not ethics, far less a religion, so it says nothing as to what is or might be moral in that absolutist or religious sense. It has no axe to grind whatsoever on that point.

In other words, Normal Realism says no more about religion, morality, ethics and so on than it does about politics and pragmatism. Its approach to science is radically different from the classical approach in such a way that it does not rule these humanitarian concerns as figmentary in the way that the classical, mechanistic science does, after Democritus. What some people have discerned as the most radical and significant step that Normal Realism makes is its 'seamless joining together of modern information-based physics and the humanities' – meaning, of course, the arts generally, including religion, ethics and morality.

4. Bodies falling down shafts

A body falling towards the earth or the sun falls in a curve. All orbits are instances of freefall. The moon is in continuous freefall towards the earth and both the earth and the moon are in continuous freefall towards the sun. These orbits are, typically, Keplerian ellipses. This can be seen in particular with comets. Their trajectories are clearly elliptical. So are those of spacecraft when they leave and return to earth.

Moreover, it is a feature of all elliptical free-fall trajectories that as the bodies in those ellipses (parabolas, hyperbolas or whatever) get nearer to the earth or the sun (known as perigee or perihelion, respectively), they go faster and faster. This is clearly seen with the planets of our solar system, where the inner ones are continually overtaking the outer ones. This is because in an angular momentum system – which, of course, the solar system is – in the equation for orbital angular momentum $L = mvr = GmM/v$, in general, the smaller the radius $r$ gets, the smaller, accordingly, becomes the angular momentum and the larger the orbital velocity $v$.

So if it is true that bodies in free fall describe curved trajectories, then how likely is it that a body in free fall down a mineshaft will fall straight towards the earth's centre? Indeed, if it did fall straight down it would need some explaining. Remember, too, that the earth is spinning, with an angular momentum of its own. Now I haven't dug a shaft deep enough to test this hypothesis, but I think it very unlikely that an object in free fall down a narrow vertical mineshaft will fall in any way differently from the way in which satellites, meteorites and other bodies fall towards earth, that is in a curvilinear trajectory. Anyway, according to the angular momentum equation, if you throw a ball vertically into the air, although the effect would normally be too small for you to notice, neglecting wind forces etc. it would not fall vertically back on the spot from which it was thrown but would hit the ground some small distance towards the west. This is something you can check out yourself. See if I'm right.

5. The existence of electrons and protons.

By the way, Normal Realism doesn't 'eschew theories'. It eschews only certain kinds of theories. The sorts of theories it eschews are those that are metaphysical, or unempirical, as described in the POAMS literature and in my answer to your first and third questions, above.

But yes, you are right about the Angular Momentum Synthesis having an explanation of what it means by the existence of 'electrons' and 'protons'. The object conventionally called the 'electron' has two kinds of angular momentum around the conventional 'proton'. One of these angular momenta is very large, which is that of its spin; the other is comparatively small, being that which is that conventionally ascribed to its orbit around the 'proton'. However, in the Synthesis, the particles in question don't exist as an 'electron' or a 'proton' any more than the whale exists as a mammal. That is to say, the conventional concepts of these are as particles of matter (mass) imbued with a mystical static 'charge' measured in coulombs and surrounded by a 'force field' spreading out into the vacuum. This, so far as Normal Realism is concerned, makes an esotery of what is customarily called 'electronics'. In the Synthesis we cash out that metaphysically frizzling and crackling 'static charge' measured in coulombs into spin energy measured in ordinary kinetic energy units of joules. The 'electron' is then nothing more than a piece of matter possessed, like our earth, of those two ordinary kinds of angular momentum, orbit and spin. This gets rid of the whole esotery of 'field-forces' in the vacuum and allows the whole thing to be explained in terms of ordinary mechanics. As it works out, compared to the proportion of spin kinetic energy to orbital kinetic in the case of the planets, the proportion of spin-energy to orbital spin-energy in the 'electron-proton' case is huge. In the Synthesis, this accounts exactly for the gigantic difference between what are customarily called 'gravitational' and 'electrostatic' forces. In other words, the names, 'gravitational force' and 'electrostatic force', as traditionally coined are, so far as Normal Realism is concerned, misnomers. That is, they introduce unnecessary complexity and confusion into what would otherwise be the most logical and economical, least esoteric descriptions of the phenomena that are possible.

GPS and Philosophy

N. Vivian Pope

GPS and Philosophy Question to Viv Pope from Penny Gower: 

If someone from GPS could be persuaded to step outside his usual framework of preconceptions, how would you seek to demonstrate to him the importance of the philosophical underpinning of scientific theory? Also in this context, what might be the advantages you could point out to him of adopting the POAMS formula, despite his understandable scepticism about something that doesn’t produce predictions with the degree of exactitude he is used to demanding/expecting for engineering purposes?

Viv’s reply: I think the best way of answering this would be to think of how someone like Copernicus or Galileo might have tried to persuade their Ptolemaic contemporaries of the advantages of the heliocentric system over the, then, ruling geocentric cosmology. The account given by that orthodoxy, of the motions of the heavens was very exact. To describe those motions, every sort of geometrical device had been constructed, such as epicycles rolling on epicycles, equants, and so on; in fact all sorts of theoretical ‘wheels within wheels’, presented with an accuracy that was at least comparable with that of the GPS people concerning their satellite orbits. There was no way that the simple Copernican heliocentric planetary cycles could compete with such exactness. It was not until much later, with Copernicus’ successors, such as Kepler, et al., that the simple orbits of Copernicus were replaced by the much more precise elliptical and parabolic orbits of planets, comets and so on that we know today. In short, there would have been no way, as history attests, of persuading the Ptolemaic authorities of the advantages of the Copernican system in terms purely of precision of representation of planetary orbits. There have always been, in science, these sorts of people who think that nothing more than exactness of observation and measurement is sufficient to an understanding of nature. Spectacularly, however, the Copernican revolution gives the lie to that. No amount of observation, in and of itself, could have told us that the earth orbits the sun, not the other way around. That required a wholesale mental adjustment of the kind that these sorts of people are typically not prepared even to contemplate. So what advantages might be pointed out to these GPS people of adopting the POAMS view? As Anthony has stressed on more than one occasion, it is not that the POAMS approach provides more exactness in matters of physics. It is that it presents a whole new way of thinking about physics, which is as different, in modern-day terms, as the Copernican paradigm was from its predecessor. In answer to your question, then, the GPS people would have to be persuaded that the advantages offered by this new approach are not for their specialism, specifically, but for science as a whole. It would involve removing from physics a whole mess of mystifying theories about things existing and happening inscrutably ‘in vacuo’, such as that whole plethora of ‘gravitational’, ‘electrostatic’, ‘magnetostatic’ ‘fields’ and ‘forces’ and so on, which misappropriate so much of our intellectual energy in physics.

So why, you may ask - as so many other colleagues have asked - have I been so busy arguing with these people when it is obviously such a lost cause? The reason is that it is a way of testing my arguments and dragging out of these disputants observational information which I need but do not possess myself. All arguments and hypotheses have to be tested. They have to meet up with observational fact to some extent, even if only imperfectly according to the specialists concerned. If it all works with these GPS people at the conceptual level (and to get within 60% of their figure is not to be sniffed at), then the ‘fine trim’ can be added afterwards, as I hope it will be in the joint paper that Anthony has suggested we write. As for that paper, I have to say that these ideas which are to be its subject and of the seminar at UWE were gained from that debate. So, regardless of how acrimonious that debate was and how implacably opposed to us the GPS people have remained, at least we now have these new ideas to work on which we wouldn’t have had otherwise. So although this may not be seen as a way of convincing a GPS engineer of the need to consider the philosophical underpinning of scientific theory, whether or not it could ever do that, it does have the advantage of developing the philosophical underpinning of POAMS in relation to the empirical data.

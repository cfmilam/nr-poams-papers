Faster than Light Neutrinos

N. Vivian Pope - 2011

In a BBC News Broadcast, November 18th, 2011, it was announced that further experiments at CERN have confirmed their disconcerting claim to have discovered particles that travel 'faster than light' - 'unless', the newsreader said, 'there is another explanation.' But, of course, there is another explanation, a very simple one, which we have presented more than once to the journals but for some unstated reason has, like all the others, been refused publication. Here it is again, for serious consideration by the commonsense reader as a final solution to this alleged 'mystery'. Suppose that we wished to measure the speed of a racing car. At the opposite ends of a measured track, which we may assume, for simplicity, is a hundred miles long, we place two timekeepers with identical stop-watches, synchronising these at the start of the exercise. If those watches, from then on, run at precisely the same rate, then that distance of a hundred miles divided by the time-difference registered by those watches - say, in this case, thirty minutes - gives the precise speed of the car which in this case, of course, is 200 miles per hour. But now let suppose that for some unknown reason, the clock at the finish end of the track has run slower than the one at the start. If the car leaves the start-line at, say, 12.00 noon, then travelling at 200 mph it should cross the finish line at exactly 12.30. However, in the slowed time of that finish-line clock, the car will arrive some time before 12.30, hence will appear to have travelled faster than 200 mph. This gives us the vital clue to the 'faster than light' claim of the CERN scientists to have discovered particles travelling 'faster than light'.

The phenomenon undoubtedly exists and the measurements are doubtless true, but there is a fundamental fallacy in the interpretation of that phenomenon as providing evidence of things travelling 'faster than light'. With the racing car example in mind, let us examine that interpretation.

As we see, then, vital to any attempt to measure the speed of anything is a precise measure of the distance travelled and an assurance of absolute synchrony between the clocks at the start and finish of the motion. So how is this synchrony to be obtained between the clocks at CERN and Gran Sasso?

This is a problem. Practically speaking, identical clocks can be synchronised only when they are close together. Taking them to any distance apart involves motion and therefore an irreversible relativistic time-dilation. Moreover, clocks that are stationed apart can be 'synchronised' only by swapping light-signals, and in Relativity there is, eminently, no overall ubiquitous time, or 'time of the universe' to be determined by that method - or indeed, by any method whatsoever (see capsule number (6)).

Now, someone may say that while there can be no absolute synchrony between clocks in relative motion there is no significant relative motion between the laboratories at CERN and Gran Sasso so that there should be no problem in synchronising their clocks.

However, it is well known that in 1959, two physicists, R. V. Pound and G. A. Rebka, carried out experiments with a gamma-ray transmitter and receiver in a lift-shaft at the Jefferson Tower at Harvard University, USA. They found that a light signal transmitted from the top of the tower to a receiver at the base was measurably time-dilated at the receiving end.

The time-dilation the experimenters measured over a depth of 22.6 metres down that shaft was given as $2.5 \times 10^{-15}$. This is taken by physicists as proof of what is called the 'gravitational time-dilation' in General Relativity. Many experiments to confirm this relation between time-dilation

and elevation have, of course, been carried out since. Indeed some experimenters claim to have detected similar results due to a change in height near the earth's surface of less than one metre, so we may take this $2.5 \times 10^{-15}$ as a useful and proven working figure.

Now before continuing with this explanation, I should say that I am since informed that those scientists who were involved with the actual experiments dealing with the transport of neutrinos between the neutrino producer at CERN and the receiver of those particles at Gran Sasso have already taken this possibility into account and dismissed it on the grounds that, as they had calculated it, the effect would be 'negligible'.

There is, however, a fundamental flaw in that particular rejection, as will be pointed out in due course. First, however, be it noted that the laboratory at Gran Sasso lies at 1400 metres below ground, which is 62 times the depth of the shaft at the Jefferson tower.

Extrapolating linearly downward, this gives for a light-signal sent from the top of the Jefferson Tower and received at a depth equal to that of Gran Sasso a theoretical timedilation of $1.6 \times 10^{-13}$, which is the nearly same as that given 'officially' for the gravitational time-dilation at that depth.

But now let us turn our attention to the neutrino producer at CERN. This is no more than about 110 metres below the surface, whereas that of the receiver in the subterranean laboratory at Gran Sasso is 1400 metres below ground, that is, more than 12 times the depth of the particle producer at CERN.

But if the time-dilation over the 22.6 metres depth of the Harvard tower is distinctly measurable, then how much more so must it be at the depth of the Gran Sasso laboratory, which is 62 times the depth of the Harvard lift-shaft! That measure of 'gravitational time-dilation' has to be there; it cannot disappear just because it is arbitrarily dismissed as 'negligible'.

However, as already mentioned, one has since been informed that this is precisely what the CERN experimenters have decided, that the effect exists but is 'negligible'. As a fellow member of ANPA (the Alternative Natural Philosophy Association, Cambridge, UK), put it to me, all the various possible explanations have been thoroughly looked at and dismissed as totally negligible. Among these, he says, is included:

…the gravitational red-shift due to the different CERN and LNGS altitudes (which) produces an (entirely negligible) relative effect of $10^{-13}$ on the clocks in between two common-view synchronisations

(my underlining, see following).

The first thing that alerted my suspicions about this statement was the phrase 'the two common-view synchronisations'. What possible 'common view' of the two laboratories and their clocks can there be over a distance apart of 730 kilometres? It seemed to me that some kind of implicit 'God's-Eye-View' presumption must be at work here. In Relativity, there is no such at-a-distance simultaneity, hence no possible 'common view' (God's Eye View) synchronisation of even identical clocks. So, where then, is that 'common-view synchronisation'? Let's face it, there simply is none. But without that ideal assurance of absolute synchrony between the clocks at CERN and Gran Sasso, what can possibly be said about the speed of anything between the two places as to whether it is faster or slower than light? This, as I see it, is the prime fallacy of the claim to have discovered speeds faster than c. Just suppose that the clocks at the start and finish of the 730 kilometres between CERN and Gran Sasso were ideally synchronised - which, of course, they aren't - then the speed of an in vacuo light signal over that horizontal distance would be c and the signal would arrive at the Gran Sasso laboratory at the time registered there of t =

s/c = $0.0023 \times 10^{-6}$ seconds. However, we have seen that the difference between the vertical time-dilation at CERN and Gran Sasso is $1.5 \times 10^{-13}$ minus $1.25 \times 10^{-14}$ which is $1.375 \times 10^{-13}$. This is eleven times the vertical time-dilation at CERN, so that every tick in the light-signal from CERN is received at Gran Sasso in one eleventh of the time it should in the time-frame of that locality.

If this were true, then it would give, for the light-signal as measured at Gran Sasso, a speed of eleven times the standard speed of light. Can there be, then, localities, such as that of the laboratory at Gran Sasso, where nature breaks the Einsteinian speed limit c, especially to that huge extent?

No, because the flip-side aspect of the same time-dilation equation is that of lengthcontraction. That is to say, for that light-signal itself, although in that time-frame of the receiver the speed is eleven times c, the distance of 732 kilometres over which the speed of the signal is measured has contracted relatively to that same receiver to an eleventh of that distance, or just over sixty-six and a half kilometres.

So, no matter what the relative measures of distance and time may be, the intrinsic 'speed' of the signal is always c, the standard asymptotic limit conventionally called the 'speed of light'. (This is known as the Lorentz-Fitzgerald contraction which is that as the speed increases towards c, the length contracts to zero.)

In sum, then, considering the extreme sketchiness of the data supplied, this result cannot be regarded as anything more than an estimate that lands at least in the ball-park, if not in the actual ticketed seat, as to how that transportation between transmitters and receivers at different depths below ground might appear to be 'faster than light'.

How this applies to actual experiments involving 'neutrinos' is an altogether technical issue and is not considered here. Suffice it to say that the same reasoning applies to fast-travelling mass-particles as to light-signals. Thus it is fair to claim it as been proved, logically, that in principle the transportation of signals and particles may appear to break the Einsteinian 'light-barrier' without actually doing so.

From this analysis, no anomalous 'faster than light' measure of the speed of neutrinos takes place, and it remains inviolate that the velocity-limit c is the same in all systems of reference, bar none, as Relativity demands.

In Short

It's like this: the neutrino producer at CERN is switched on and the neutrinos appear at the distant Gran Sasso laboratory. Due to the difference in depth below ground of the two laboratories, the clocks at Gran Sasso are running slower that those at CERN. Just as in the racing car example, therefore, the neutrinos appear to have travelled faster than they would have done if the clocks at the two places were absolutely synchronised, otherwise the true measures of their speed of transportation would be normal sub-c in accordance with standard Relativity. Logically, then, it is on that bogus assumption - and only on that bogus assumption - of a God's-Eye-View synchrony (or 'common-view-synchronisation') between the clocks at CERN and Gran Sasso that the breach of the relativistic speedlimit c can be claimed, otherwise, there is no mystery whatsoever presented by the phenomenon itself. The whole 'problem' is just another case of what ails our contemporary Physics in general, which is that in presuming to see the world as we imagine 'God sees it' we succeed only in blinding ourselves. QED. Neville Vivian (Viv) Pope, Hon Prof. of Relativistic Quantum Physics, POAMS.org

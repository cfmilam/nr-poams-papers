The Nature of Time

Anthony D. Osborne — 2001

Essay on 'The Nature of Time' for the Royal Institute of Philosophy (UK) competition, by Dr. Anthony D. Osborne, Department of Mathematics, Keele University, England.

How would things look from a very high mountain? By this, I mean that if we adopt the loftiest viewpoint possible, what fundamental lessons can we learn from what we can see of the physical world around us? First, we would notice that there is something 'out there'. Let us call that something 'matter'. Next, we might notice that the matter comes in many different forms, and that the forms continually change. Of course, one more thing stands out, a rather remarkable thing. It is a thing so familiar to us all that it is very easy to take it for granted, a thing that we have all marvelled at since childhood and, perhaps, one that we can never quite get used to. It is this: the whole thing comes to us in the form of a magnificent pageant – a truly wonderful show, a breathtaking display. And what is more, we all have a 'front-row seat'!

This pageant of Nature, with all its rich variety of changing sights, sounds, smells, tastes, and so on is nothing less than the sum total of our experiences. 'The totality of facts, not of things,' as Wittgenstein put it. And when I say 'totality', I mean just that. One of the fundamental questions facing science today is: are the phenomena of the world the whole of reality, or is there some other reality behind them?

Before attempting to answer this question directly, let us dwell for a moment on the 'facts' of the world – on what we actually see happening. If there is one thing that is most noticeable, when we look, it is the fact that all things move. Whether we look at the tiniest insect or at the most distant star, all things exhibit a tendency to move. All things seem to be 'on the go'. We might, indeed, go so far as to say that nothing is truly at rest – that it is of the very nature of matter to be on the move. And if matter is always moving, then it must have been moving for all time. We cannot, as the ancient Greeks discovered, put our 'foot' on the brakes and stop it all happening, for that would require no less than an infinite force. And if it must always have been moving, it must always have been changing too. Constant change, then, is of the very essence of things. And there's the rub, for change implies time. 'What then, is time?', asked St. Augustine; and then, sighing: 'If no one asks me, I know; but if I am asked and try to explain, I do not know.'

So, what are the choices? What can we say, at this level, about such a mystifying concept? Well, one thing we can say is that if matter changes, and if matter is everything that there is, then time is an intrinsic property of what matter does. There is, by that logic, no such thing as 'Time' in the sense of a global, cosmic clock, counting or clocking the motions and other changes in every element of matter in the world. Time, rather, is an intrinsic property of what matter does. In a universe of things that change, time is essentially plural, each piece of matter having its own individual time, and the various times of different things being quantitatively related to one another. The 'passage of time', then, is just matter changing – there being as many 'passages' as there are pieces of matter undergoing change.

But the sceptic will ask: how do we know that? Is there not something beyond – beyond the observable world of moving and changing matter? In particular, is there not, behind it all, a universal, a cosmic time? Is there not something, behind the observations, which constitutes a more solid, lasting reality than that which we observe with the senses?

Well, that has always been a tough question, and one which, by its very nature, doesn't seem to admit of a positive answer. But, rather than leaving it at that, it is worth thinking about what the question actually amounts to. To ask if there is anything beyond the world of things that we can see and hear and touch, is to ask if there is something beyond what we know. Therefore, since we can have no knowledge of a thing existing beyond our senses, the question of some 'extra' reality being there, behind the show, seems to amount to no more than an imagining. But an imagining, of course, is a form of knowledge, and so, like it or not, we are locked into the world of our experiences. And since we need time in order to experience anything at all, it does seem rather odd to think that such an essentially experiential thing as time might exist beyond the reaches of experience. Let us, then, not go there. Let us be content to dwell instead on the fact that all matter changes, that time is just a measure of how fast different things change and that time, consequently, is an intrinsic property of what matter does. On the basis of this, what else can we say?

Well, one of the most common measures of time is the motion of a body. And the most basic unit of such a motion is one complete period of an orbit, for example, the orbit of the Earth around the Sun, or of the Moon around the Earth. There are no straight-line motions in Nature, only orbital motions, every orbit having its own particular period. Those orbital periods are the natural clocks. A year is one revolution of the Earth around the Sun; a month is one revolution of the Moon around the Earth; one revolution of the 'balance' wheel on my wrist watch is about a fifth of a second, and so on.

The common factor in all these natural clocks is angular momentum. In orbital motion, angular momentum gives a relation between a body's orbital period and its distance from the centre of the orbit (that is, the 'radius'). It is not difficult to see, then, that the angular momentum connection between radius and period constitutes an intimate connection between distance and time. From this connection we might expect to find some correlation between those two fundamental measures, something which tells us how much time there is per unit of distance. If we could find this and write it down, as a formula, we would, in effect, have discovered the length of a second!

The idea that there might exist such a fundamental link between distance and time was first given substance in 1676 by the Danish astronomer Olaus Römer. Römer was the first person to measure the ratio of distance to time that we now denote by the letter $c$. This, expressed in metric units, comes out at close to 300,000 kilometres to the second (or, in imperial units, 186,000 miles to the second). What this tells us, in effect, is that every metre of distance is equivalent to 3.3 nanoseconds (a nanosecond is one thousand-millionth of a second). We thus have a direct correlation between distance and time which, in the Neo-Machian formalism, allows us to express all distances in units of seconds, and all times in units of metres. (Astronomers already do this with their unit of the 'light-year', a distance measured in years.)

Römer's ratio $c$ is almost universally regarded as the speed at which light (and, indeed, all electromagnetic radiation) travels through space. That, however, requires a modification. An exactly equivalent way of interpreting Römer's result is as a 'conversion factor' or scaling constant between, effectively, two different ways of measuring the same thing: the measure of length in metres and the measure of length in seconds (or vice versa, for time). This more economical interpretation is the Neo-Machian one. Its advantage is that it avoids the unnecessary complications of the classical picture in which our connection with the outside world involves the concept of 'light-signals' travelling invisibly through space.

To summarise, let us say that in the Machian sense, time is not 'something out there'; it is intrinsic to what matter does. As such, it is as varied and diverse as the kinds of matter that 'do it'. And there is no universal, 'cosmic' time in terms of which the 'proper' times of matter may be supposed to be in synchronism, for that would be to deny the relativity which all our observations confirm. Above all, we say that in the study of time, the observations are the physics. It is to the phenomena that we should look for the 'passage of time' – not to the theories about them.

Existence, Science and Language - Dec. 2004

N. Vivian Pope — 2004

Existence, Science and Language Viv Pope What does it mean ‘to exist’? Basically, it is for some set of properties or qualities, Y, to be predicated 2 of a subject X. For instance, to say ‘Socrates is short and stout’ says both that Socrates is (exists) and that he has those qualities of shortness and stoutness. Similarly, to say ‘An electron has a mass of 9.109 × 10 -31 kilograms and a charge of 1.602 × 10-19 coulomb is to say that something we call an ‘electron’ exists in terms of those and perhaps other measured properties. Simply to say ‘An electron exists’ with nothing whatsoever predicated of it makes no sense. In every case, then, where something is predicated of something, existence is automatically conferred on that something by some variant or other of the verb ‘to be’ (i.e., ‘is’, ‘was’, ‘will be’, or whatever.). It will undoubtedly puzzle many readers, to think that merely naming something makes it exist. But that is only because we are accustomed to think of ‘existence’ as what objects have on the ordinary, everyday ‘concrete’ level of thinking, whereas there are all sorts of other categories of existence, as we shall see, here, in due time. It stands to reason, however, that without some such predication, nothing can be described, defined or even named’ To readers who stay the course, here, the implications of this should eventually become clear. What, then, is an ‘electron’? In the very first place, it is a concept in the human mind, a form of thought devised to account for certain phenomena that were observed and measured at some time or other by a human researcher. The name ‘electron’ given to the subject of those predications was, of course, purely arbitrary. It could have been given any name whatsoever by anyone whomsoever Try, then, to think of an electron existing absolutely self-sufficiently, without being predicated by anything or anyone. What a conundrum! To think about it ties the mind in knots. If mankind had never come on the scene, would

those ‘electrons’ have continued existing, regardless of anyone or anything being able to predicate anything of them? But what can ‘exist’ possibly mean in that context? Only retrospectively and in terms of human conception and language can an ‘electron’ exist in such a projected scenario. By the same token, try to think of a ‘Universe’ of things like electrons, ‘atoms’, ‘protons’, ‘neutrons’, ‘quarks’, quasars or whatever existing selfsufficiently, independently of all actual and possible human perception and conception. It is simply impossible. Trying to think it through, consistently, ‘blows the mind’. But this is how it is with the vast majority of things that there undoubtedly are that are completely unknown and totally inconceivable to man. As finite beings, any attempt to comprehend or name that residual class of things as a whole is bound to diminish it. How, for instance, can we be sure that it is a ‘universe’? why not a ‘pluriverse’ or a ‘polyverse’? There is no possible way of being absolutely certain which, if any, of these descriptions truly applies. But, of course, all this pertains only at the very edges of our experience, in realms of microphysics physics and cosmology. In ordinary commonsense matters we can be absolutely certain that such things as trees, animals, furniture, churches, barns and solid objects in general are what they are, no matter what. But can we be so certain? Once it was thought that a whale was a fish, when later inspection showed it to be a mammal, and centaurs were found to be nothing more than marauding men on horseback. In general, all our linguistic classifications and divisions of all things, animal mineral and vegetable are, in principle, revisable. For instance, it was once thought that the categories animal and mineral were absolutely distinct. But then it was discovered that a virus particle can exist in both categories. Studies of even the most solid and stable of objects have revealed that on microphysical levels they are not solid at all, but are constituted, mostly, of energetic motion and change. (Anyone who has tried to throw a marble through the sparsely separated spokes of a fast-spinning bicycle wheel soon sees how motion can create a sense of solidity.). Indeed, the famous formula E = Mcsquared, as applied in the nuclear industry, states that on the ultimate atomic level, the classical categories of matter and energy actually merge.

So none of our language categories, in commonsense or in science carries any absolute guarantee that it is immune from all possibility of revision. Even the most radical revisions take place in some cases, as our experience of the world evolves. This tells us just how much we know or claim to know depends on language and its structure of categories and distinctions. This was the point of Ludwig Wittgenstein’s very much misunderstood statement, ‘The World is Language’. But indeed, thinking hard about it, how else might it possibly be? What is it, then, that scientists study? Do they, uniquely among us, deal with a world of things that exist outside and beyond language? Does Science provide us with some special insight into things that are independent of our perceiving, conceiving and describing them? The very suggestion is absurd. Scientists are as confined as anyone else within the limits of our evolved and evolving language. Too many scientists, however, fall into the fallacy of talking about their ‘electrons’, ‘fields’, ‘photons’, ‘quarks’, ‘quasars’, ‘black holes’, ‘wormholes’, and so on, as though these were not concepts framed in human language but things they know about independently of our finite language and its frailties. But the fact that something can be named, such as an ‘electron’, and that it can be described in terms of the observational/experimental measures allocated to it does not provide an absolute guarantee that anything answering to that name and those predications really exists. That is to say, there is no certainty whatsoever that as our understanding develops, the whole concept of an ‘electron’ may not have to be revised and perhaps even abandoned, like the substances conceived as ‘caloric’ and ‘phlogiston’ in seventeenth century science. Indeed it may turn out, on logical analysis, that some things that were at first thought to exist in the category of real objects cannot possibly exist, other than in the categories of failed ideas or fictions. Such, for instance, is Einstein’s abortive idea of the ‘photon’ as a spacetravelling light-particle. The theory from which this idea stems rules that any piece of matter travelling at what the theory regards as ‘the speed of light’ has infinite mass. Yet the photon’s mass is in the miniscule order of a million trillionths of a kilogram. How can that be? It can’t. The whole concept is

nonsense and was abandoned by its originator – although reverently retained among his followers.. This power of language in itself to secure judgements on scientific matters is not unanimously recognised among scientists, especially experimental physicists. Consequently, huge amounts of money and other resources are squandered on searches to find things the impossibility of whose being can be determined by commonsense logic alone. (Attempts to photograph ‘the photon in flight’ or to measure ‘the speed of gravity’ are just two of many examples.) Scientists who have lost or were never taught a correct awareness of this fundamental connection between their discoveries and language have therefore done mankind a great disservice in creating a world of chimeras, things that they claim to know about but which are inscrutable to commonsense perception. This allows all too many scientists to pose as something akin to the priests of yore whose special intuitions and initiations allowed them to claim an affinity with things as they imagined were seen through the eyes of an all-pervading and allseeing God. Presuming to think and act in accordance with this Divine Perception, those priests were able to impose their own finite and arbitrary wills on the populace at large, with all the trappings of an assumed Absolute Authority – sometimes by methods of ‘correction’ such as torture and burning at the stake. In any event, to claim or imagine that one’s ideas are uniquely free of the essential revisability and negotiability of language – to think that somehow or other one is uniquely and extra-linguistically plugged-in to the Infinite – is disastrous. It forms the basis of some of the most dangerous dogmas that afflict human society. Scientists, however, who are fully cognisant of this and despise it among religious groups all too often fail to recognise it in themselves. For instance, statements in science textbooks about ‘the expanding universe’, the ‘Big Bang’, ‘the first milliseconds of creation’ and so on, are issued with all the overweening confidence and authority of the scholars at the trial of Galileo who was threatened with torture for his honesty in declaring that on grounds of personal thought and careful observation he could not be as certain of things as were these self-elected Authorities posing as servants of the Almighty.

Is it, then, that there is nothing we can ever know for certain? Not so. As already seen, some forms of language provide their own certainty. To say, for instance, ‘There is no such thing as truth’ and hold that statement to be true is to state an obvious contradiction. From this it follows that truth exists, since to deny it creates nonsense. The same goes for ‘real’. If nothing was real, then everything would be an illusion. But in that case, what could ‘illusion’ possibly mean? To know that something is illusory is to know something that is not illusory, something that is, by comparison, ‘real’ – or, at least, more real than was considered so previously. This much, at least, is certain, and it is this facility of language for providing certainty in a way that is not possible for ‘plain observation’ that has been called logic. So at least we know for certain that truth and reality exist. But these are abstracts. What about things concrete? For instance, does matter exist? Well, it all depends on what we mean by ‘matter’. For matter to exist without anything being predicable of it is nonsense, since the word ‘exist’ in such an imaginary context is meaningless. What, then, is matter? What are its defining attributes? On the commonsense level, it is perhaps the most uncompromisingly real set of impressions we have. It is either solid, liquid or gaseous, it has mass, duration, shape, size, weight, volume, form, density chemical composition and all those other qualities and properties known to science. But above all, it is substantial, in the sense of ‘standing under’ everything else. Well, at least, that is how matter was regarded in classical science, that is, as something existing absolutely, permanently and in itself, something which ‘God’ sees and predicates from his all-seeing and all-pervading ‘God’s eye view’. However, modern science has discovered that matter is nothing like that, that ‘matter’, or ‘material’, turns out to be one of the most revisable of all categories. In fact, it now transpires that all those defining attributes of matter that were predicated by classical science, even its basic measures mass, length and duration, are not absolute but relative, which means subject to observational perspective. In discovering this, science has shifted, in the most radical and revolutionary way, from presuming to see the world ‘as God sees it’ to seeing it as it really is from the point of view of a finite human – and humane – perspective. That is to say, in

relativity, all measures of distance, time and so on, are from points not stretched uniformly ‘throughout the universe’, as they might appear to some imagined allpervading and all seeing deity. In other words, what matter is depends, quite literally, on some finite, observational point of view, either of a human observer or of some observational instrument – and all objects are instruments in this sense. Modern relativistic physics says, in effect, that ‘matter’ has no mass, length or duration ‘in itself’ but only in relation to other things, some of which may be human observers or measurers. What, then, is ‘matter’s’ warrant for existing? It is that from observing the interrelations of physical objects with one another, all human observers can concur that ‘matter’ (or in other languages, ousia (Greek), materia (Latin), or whatever), is a constant subject of predications on which humans are agreed in common. Matter is perfectly real in that sense, since it is so easily distinguishable from illusion. To say ‘Matter is real’ is true, therefore, insofar as its existence is common to all the different observational and conceptual perspectives. To think of matter as something existing in itself, independently of all those different perspectives and of all language is impossible, because to describe matter as existing completely unobserved and un-predicated, relatively to nothing but itself is, again, just pure nonsense. To imagine things ‘existing’ in that way is to assume the stance of a deity, hence is more in the character of metaphysics and deistic religion than of science. To describe something as existing, then, unavoidably presupposes some form or other of language. But whose language? Some things ‘exist’ in some languages that do not exist in others, like the spirits and demons that are predicated by some groups and societies. Is there, then, underlying all these social and subjective differences, some common and universal language which provides the ultimate datum of truth and reality? The Greeks thought so, and they called it logos. This, as the Greeks conceived, it is the true language by which the fitness of all other languages are judged. Without that ‘master-language’, or ‘Language of Nature’ as sometimes called, there could be no ultimate truth or falsity, no ultimate reality or unreality other than what was decided by pure convention.

Who, then, speaks this ultimate and perfect language? We do, at least to some extent. It is what provides the common backbone of all human knowledge without which there could be no language at all. It is this logic, or logos that forms the basis of all true commonsense everywhere. But of course, not all of us – or, indeed, very few of us – are anything like fully conversant in that language. On the other hand, there are even fewer of us who are without that language entirely. Even those who are pronounced clinically insane are conversant with it to some extent. To be without it altogether one would have to be either comatose or dead. Where, then, does this logos come from? It can’t be from anywhere ‘out there in or beyond space’ because, like everything else, entities such as ‘space’ and ‘beyond space’ exist first and foremost as subjects of predication in language. So, logically, since the space is in language, then that language cannot also be ‘in space’, any more than the outside of a box can also be its inside. But, of course, language – that is, human language – is not the same as logos. Logos is to language as absolute zero is to temperature, without which reference-datum there could be no objective hotter and cooler among bodies. In our employments of language we may never reach the ultimate logos, but it has to exist, even if predicated as an unreachable datum, otherwise we could never be aware of error. For instance, we know that there has to be a datum-limit to the removal of error, and the nearest we get to that limit is by logic as applied to language in rooting out contradictions, fallacies and inconsistencies. That limit, towards which all logic tends is logos. Logos is the way things are as opposed to the way we merely think they are. It is the interactive and ongoing informational matrix which links all things. That matrix is the manifold of forms, patterns and sequences that we perceive in what, to our limited senses, is ‘light’. In this present context, this is not something travelling invisibly in space, relatively to a vacuum, as classical science and Einsteinian Relativity present it, but is simply what we see, the opposite of ‘dark’. In that fullest sense, what we call ‘light’ is, in fact, a whole spectral range of quantum interactions from the deepest infra-red to the farthest ultra-violet and gamma, including all quantum (that is discrete point-topoint) interactions whatsoever, without remainder.

What, then, we may ask, is the logos in itself, as distinct from human language? Is that a sensible question to ask? Yes, because we know that this distinction is a real. Logos really exists, since without it, all language is just noise. In terms of linguistic predication, it is a language that is the ultimate and perhaps unreachable reference datum of all language, hence, by definition, superhuman. Does it have any other predications? In the Christian religion, the Greek logos, translated in Hebrew, as ‘The Word’, is identified both with ‘God’ and with the biblical persona of the man Jesus, named ‘The Christ’. In a famous painting by William Holman Hunt, this same person is portrayed, interestingly enough, as ‘The Light of the World’, and in Wittgenstein’s thesis, the World is identified with Language. So our combining of logos with both rational language and light is far from being unprecedented. But, of course, in our Western tradition of science these identifications would be sheer nonsense, for how can something so incidental as light travelling in space possibly be conceived in that way? This points to the fundamental schizophrenia of our Western society, in that its twin pillars, of Science and Christianity, are not erected on the same philosophical ground. And even today, the fundamental materialism or absolutism of classical science, far from being cleanly replaced by the relativism of Einstein’s philosophical and scientific mentor, Mach, has simply been mixed-in with its classical precursor through Einstein’s completely unnecessary interpretation of the distance-time constant c as the ‘absolute velocity of light’ relative to a universal void or vacuum. This, in the hands of Einstein’s followers, has placed modern relativistic physics far beyond the grasp of ordinary logical commonsense. In the NRIG group and in its spin-off publication, The Eye of the Beholder: the Role of the Observer in Modern Physics, 3 it has been demonstrated how all the practical consequences of Einstein’s Theory of Relativity can be deduced, far more simply and in purely commonsense terms, without that theory’s abstruse and absolutistic interpretation of the dimensional constant, c, as ‘the velocity of light in vacuo’ With that sticking-point out of the way, relativity comes into its own, as a whole philosophy, not just a bit of abstruse physics. In any event, logos is not something from outer space. It is in all of us, hence between us in all of our dealings with one another. In biblical terms, it is the light within us. By our personal exercise of it we are enlightened and we enlighten others. In similar fashion, much of the biblical language on which our society was built, but which science has made archaic, begins to make sense once more. Is this ‘Science’ we are talking about now, Physics or Theology? In this present context, what’s the difference?

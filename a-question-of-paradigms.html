ABSTRACT

There is a growing unease among science commentators concerning the traditional reductionistic, or 'atom-smashing', approach to the understanding of physical phenomena. New evidences have suggested that this needs to be supplemented by another, ~~obsolescent and that there is need of a more holistic, or~~ phenomenalistic, type of approach.

Attempts to provide a general model for this have been many and various but all, for one reason or another, have been unsatisfactory. The reasons for this lack of success are here analysed and the possibility of a new systematic ~~alternative, or~~ 'paradigm', is explored.

A QUESTION of PARADIGMS

by

N. Vivian Pope

In an article entitled 'A Clash of Paradigms in Physics', (1) E. W. Bastin argues that there may be discerned, at present, 'two paradigms' of thought which are separate and incompatible. One of these Bastin calls 'the classical paradigm'. This, he says, 'has its basis in the idea of a continuous background of space and time which are imagined as perfectly smooth, perfectly homogeneous and infinitely divisible continua.' The contents of this space are particles and fields with positions and intensities varying with time. The other paradigm, which Bastin calls 'the sequential paradigm', arises out of studies in experimental microphysics, where matter does not behave in anything like the way that is familiar to us on ordinary microphysical levels. This new paradigm has been thrust on us, says Bastin. There is no background of thinking out of which it naturally emerges.

Nor is Bastin alone in his opinion of the confused state of modern theoretical physics. Our ideas of 'the large and the small', says C.J.S. Clarke,(2) do not gel. A 'unified alternative', he argues, is required but 'unfortunately does not exist' at the present time. There have been, he admits, many attempts at producing this unified alternative, some of which he thinks are successful. But they are all, he says, either ad hoc and contrived or else requiring such a radical revision of everything that they are a long way from physical utility. In any event, simply 'glueing together' classical and quantum physics, Clarke believes, is 'doomed to failure'.

Primarily, the difference between the two paradigms is that there is no concurrence between them as to what is meant by physical interaction. According to the classical paradigm, the ways in which bodies interact is either directly, by mechanical collision, or indirectly by the quasi-mechanical exchange of waves or particles travelling across the intervening space. In Einsteinian relativity there is an absolute speed-limit on all such interaction. This is the so-called 'speed of

light', the rate at which electromagnetic field-disturbances, it is said, are propagated in vacuo (a constant, c = 3 x 10⁶ metres per second). It is a limit which is automatically obeyed because c² is the factor for converting matter into energy and vice versa. In other words, since every joule of energy divided by c² is 10⁻¹⁷ kilograms of mass, the more energy we impart to a body in giving it an acceleration, the more we add to its mass and therefore to its inertia. Nearing the speed c, every effort we might make to accelerate a body succeeds only in increasing its mass, so acceleration beyond that speed is impossible.

In classical theory, then, supported by Relativity, no physical interaction can take place instantaneously. In fact, though, as quantum physicists have discovered, energy is transacted at quantum levels, across distances small or large, on a one-to-one basis of quantum-exchange involving no question of travelling-time. Nothing travels in the quantum-domain. A quantum is a unit of action, and actions, as such, cannot travel. (To say that they do, as is said about 'photons', for example, is to say, in effect, that 'action acts', which is a semantical howler). When analysed at the quantum level, therefore, the movements of bodies are like the movements of objects on a television-screen. They consist of sequences of complete and unanalysable events, each just appearing and disappearing at a point -- hence the way of thinking which Bastin refers to as 'the sequential paradigm'.

We see, then, how different quantum-mechanics is from the mechanics of the classical tradition. In the one, bodies move in the way we ordinarily think of them moving; in the other they do not move in that sense at all. But is it really as Bastin says, that there is no connection between these two different ways of thinking? Are we not accustomed, since the invention of the cinema, to seeing how very convincingly the sensation of movement can be generated by sequences of still pictures -- or spot-events on a television screen? Why, then, should we be so confounded by the discovery, in quantum physics, that mechanical motion, on its most fundamental analytical level, has this same sequential or 'cinematographic' character?

The answer is, of course, that we tend to think of what we see on a cinema- or television-screen as no more than an illusion of mechanical motion. Between each 'still' and the next, we tend to assume, there are an infinite number of events that might have occurred in reality. But what warrant have we for extrapolating this indefinitely? If the number of stills into which a motion may be divided is infinite, then the duration of each still is zero, whence the sum of the whole is nil. This makes it logically untenable to suppose that mechanical motion is infinitely smooth and continuous -- a consequence which was well known to the Greeks (e.g., Zeno of Elea) more than two thousand years ago. A motion, to be a motion (as opposed to something entirely paradoxical) has to quantised at some stage or other. That is to say, it has to consist of finite and irreducible parts, or atomic 'stills'; and the same, of course, for motion's component dimensions, length and time. These too have to be quantised, for the same logical reasons. So why should we be so surprised, nowadays, at the discovery that motion is quantised, when the intimation that it has to be has been with us for more than two thousand years?

What makes it difficult, no doubt, to accept the quantum-sequential, or 'cinematographic' interpretation of motion is to picture how, in the sequence between one such event and another, the substance of a body which we call its mass may be sustained. A body, in such circumstances, would have to flicker in and out of existence between one such discrete event and the next. We can appreciate how the appearance of motion may be sustained in cinematographic representations but fail to see how the sense-quality momentum may be sustained in the same way. Yet it is precisely that measure, momentum (or, more precisely, angular momentum) which has proved to be quantised -- in units ('stills') h/2π, where h is Planck's constant of action, of value h = 6.5×10⁻³⁴ J s. So it is not just motion but momentum which is 'cinematographic'. And that is the idea which we find so difficult to accommodate to our classical precepts.

In the sequential paradigm, then, which Bastin says has been 'thrust on us', there is no 'material substance' (substance being, literally a

'standing under') that sustains the continuity of physical phenomena -- like the celluloid or other matrix on which a film-sequence is printed and passed through the projector. But if matter (mass) is not the substance behind the quantum microphenomena, then what is?

The assumption underlying this question is that this idea of 'substance' is somehow logically obligatory. But that is just the habit which the classical mechanistic philosophy instils in us. There is another way of thinking which makes that idea of 'substance' unnecessary. This has a tradition as long as, if not longer, than that of the mechanistic philosophy. It is the view called phenomenalism, which finds its most recent expression in the methodologies of Ernst Mach and Henri Poincaré; also, but not quite so thoroughly and systematically (see following), in the ideas of Albert Einstein and Niels Bohr. It is therefore simply not true that, as Bastin says, 'there is no background of thinking out of which it (the sequential paradigm) naturally emerges'.

What is true is that this alternative, phenomenalistic, way of thinking is incompatible with the one which, traditionally, forms the conventional mainstream of scientific thought. Its developments, therefore, have taken place in areas of academia remote from those in which scientists undergo their professional training. Scientists do, of course, sometimes avail themselves of such phenomenalistic (or, as scientists call them, 'positivistic') ideas as were subscribed to science by philosopher-scientists like Mach and Poincaré, but invariably on an ad hoc or eclectic basis. But because the two thought-systems are antithetical, whenever they are mixed in this way -- as in conventional relativity theory and quantum wave-mechanics -- they inevitably create confusion.

What characterises these admixtures is that they seek to represent quantum-events as mediated by 'waves' of one kind or another, travelling in something like classical space and time. In this we see that 'glueing together' of incompatible thought-systems which Clarke sees as doomed to failure. The concept of a wave, like that of the

momentum of a particle, is of a continuum, whereas the concept of a quantum-sequence is of a discontinuum. This makes 'Quantum Wave-Mechanics' a contradiction in terms.

To observe the logical distinctions between the two paradigms is to see very clearly that in the phenomenological paradigm there can be no question of 'waves', 'particles' 'wave-particles' nor any other such substratum, mechanical electrodynamical, geometrodynamical or whatever 'standing-under' the sequences of fundamental quantum-events. In this view, every physical quantity, quality, or dimension -- of space, time, mass or whatever -- dismantles into phenomenological 'stills' (quanta), which is the datum-level of the phenomenological interpretation. To go beyond that and suppose that the very data out of which space, time and mechanical activity consist are arranged and mediated mechanically in that same kind of space and time is to generate nonsense. It is that kind of nonsense which is so typical of the situation which Bastin and his colleagues describe as prevailing at the present time.

If Science and Philosophy had not parted company (during the early Nineteenth Century) such confusion could scarcely have occurred. It would have been plain that for logical reasons, a fundamental distinction has to be maintained between two different orders of physical existence: one at the level of physical laws, theories and formulæ, the other at the level of the data on which these laws, theories, etc., depend. The distinction which needs to be maintained, in other words, is between what Professor David Bohm (2) calls the 'explicate order' and the 'implicate order'. In this present context, Bohm's 'explicate order' is that of the world as it appears to us in ordinary physical dimensions (the scenario, as it were, of ordinary experience) whereas the 'implicate order' is that of the data out of which these dimensions are constructed. 'Waves' and 'particles', as ordinarily understood, are part of the scenario-system (the explicate order) not the data-system (implicate order), so no wave-particle or other such precept applies to events on this datum quantum-phenomenological level.

What is it, then, for the phenomenalists, if it is not waves nor particles, nor any other kind of substratum or substance, which interrelates events at this datum-level? It is evident that something makes these quantum-informational events hang together in the way they do. Is that interrelating, as Hume and Kant suggest, purely psychological on the part of the observer? That seems unlikely considering the sheer intricacy and objectivity of natural phenomena. No science of nature could ever function on the basis of such subjectivistic assumptions.

So, whatever the interrelating factor might be, it is certainly not human subjectivity. But that way of thinking does at least give a clue as to what, in phenomenalistic terms, the kind of answer should be. It is that the explanation of the coherence of the microphenomena lies, somehow, in the phenomenological whole not in the microphenomenal parts. This defines the basis of the new and revolutionary kind of thinking, called holism (from the Greek holos, meaning 'entire') which seems to be gaining ground in present-day science. The reason why it is so revolutionary is that it turns physics literally inside-out, with all those causal principles which the 'atom-smashing' tradition assumes are to be sought in the 'insides of atoms' being sought, instead, on their 'outsides' -- that is, in the phenomena themselves, not in any supposed 'internal micro-mechanisms'. For the phenomenalist, looking 'inside atoms' for the 'secrets of nature' is as absurd as assuming that what led to the works of Shakespeare was the way the letters of the alphabet are constructed, or that smashing-up chessmen is the way to find the rules of chess.

Unfortunately for modern science, this 'revolution' in thought has been attended by much confusion. This is because the revolution is, so far, only partial. We can no more accommodate it as it is than wear a coat with one sleeve turned inside-out and the other turned outside-in.

To make that revolution complete we have to drop altogether the notion of a 'substance' or quasi-material substratum underlying the phenomena, like a reel of film or tape, and express all physical laws as positivistic functions of the phenomena themselves. The existence

of phenomenological wholes is then unquestionable. Everything becomes very straightforward, for the fact that these wholes exist and are not entirely subjectively imposed is the self-evident starting-point of any science, whence to deny them (to relegate them to the level of 'epiphenomena' in the classical way) is metaphysical. No less metaphysical, however, is the tendency among some of the new scientific holists, to jump to the other unempirical extreme of assuming, automatically, that there is some Overall Universal Whole which is the inscrutable explanation of everything. As to whether or not there is such a Universal Whole, as Bohm envisages, who can say? That there are wholes to some limited extent in finite experience is, on the other hand, undeniable. What we may do, then, without taking either holism or atomism to metaphysical extremes, is to study those wholes which we may sensibly discern, either directly or by instrumental means. This puts a curb on mysticism and keeps our speculations within the bounds of empirical science.

For the phenomenalist, then, science begins and ends with these finite, self-evident and systematic wholes which we call phenomena. But what makes these cohere in the way they do if they are not to be regarded as just sense-representations of something more fundamental? Unfortunately, among the confusions to which our culture is heir is that of the history of phenomenalism, which initially assumed that this coherence was provided by the mind of God, then (in later philosophies) by the mind of man and then, in more recent philosophy, by the rules of language.

Now 'language', in these later philosophies, meant human language, the language used by the human observer or percipient. Gradually, however, with the evolution of modern communication-technology, this meaning of 'language' has broadened to include not only the language of the observer as such -- i.e., written or spoken language -- but all communication, verbal, behavioural, instrumental, or whatever. The academic meaning of language, as used by the 'linguistic' philosophers has therefore been made redundant. In its place we have communication, in which the language of human convention loses its central importance.

Communication, then, as we now understand it, takes place not only between people, at the verbal/literary level, but between organisms in general, at the behavioural level, at the biological level (i.e., both inter- and intra-cellular and molecular), and even between 'atoms' at what is traditionally called the level of 'inorganic' interaction. In this way, the meaning of 'language', as employed in the academic sense of the 'linguistic philosophers', is modified more or less out of existence, to become something for which the term 'language' is now quite inappropriate -- since that 'something' now includes just about every kind of interaction there is.

Yet the essence of what the linguistic philosophers meant by 'language' remains. In what we now understand by 'communication', applied in physical science as in science generally, the same sorts of holistic (i.e., logical or contextual) rules apply as they do in language. The revolution that has occurred in physical science is that the rules of thermodynamics as codified by mechanists like Boltzmann, and which are fundamental to physical interaction on all levels, have been replaced by rules of a contextual (e.g., statistical or stochastic) kind. These rules, far from being rules of grammar, turn out to be the basic causal rules of physics.

The 'nature' which forms the proper study of phenomenalistic science is therefore neither the fictitious 'material substance' of mechanistic science nor the academic 'language' of the linguistic philosophers. It is more in the nature of what the early Greeks called logos, a secular term signifying simply the objective rational connexity of natural phenomena. (This is to be distinguished from the later, Holistic (i.e., (religious†) meaning of logos given by the writer of St. John's Gospel, Ch. I, vv 1-14.)

Now this logos (meaning, here, simply the logical order implicit in physical, i.e, organic and inorganic, processes) requires many more dimensions for its description than does the traditionally determinate

mechanical process. The natural model for this multidimensional logical system is the one Bohm cites, the modern computer. As Bohm says $^{(4)}$, in the computer the silicone chip contains the information in 'bits' corresponding to the elements in the chip. This is the implicate system which, as Bohm says, determines how currents will flow throughout the computer as a whole, through the working together of the hardware and the software. In this artificial logos, so to speak, all the myriad alternatives for specific operation potentially exist in the chips and their wired interconnections.

However, there are no such 'wired interconnections' between the quantum-events at the ultimate microphenomenal level. So if one wants to examine the reasons why a certain sequence of microphenomenal operations takes place one has to look, not at the 'quantum microchips' (atoms) but at the programme as a whole, which is the sense in which the computer-model is applicable.

So far, then, Bohm's thinking is entirely in agreement with that of the phenomenalists. However, in thinking that at the implicate level there are 'hidden mechanisms' analogous to the workings of the microchips and wirings, Bohm is conflating two entirely incompatible systems of thought. To come properly to terms with empirical phenomenalism is, as we have said, to break entirely away from the polarised metaphysical constraints of 'underlying mechanisms' on the one hand and of 'Universe' on the other, both of which lie at the core of Bohm's analysis.

From our phenomenalistic 'inside-out' point of view, then, what are our scientists in the 'nuclear research' laboratories discovering if not these very 'hidden mechanisms' of elementary particles -- the 'internal parts and wirings', as it were, of natural phenomena? What, in holistic/phenomenalistic terms, are they 'finding' when they announce their discoveries?

The reason why scientists need to split-up matter in this way is obvious. You can split a piece of matter only so many times and so

many ways until you come to its ultimate chemical and other constituents. Rearranging these either consumes or releases physical energy, creating, in the process, all sorts of energy-systems and all sorts of different materials, both natural and artificial. If those atoms were not there all along, as things in their own right, then we would not encounter those limitations which there are on dividing things up and rearranging them. We would be able to start with any arbitrary amount of anything and divide and rearrange it indefinitely. The fact that we cannot do that is therefore testimony (we tend to suppose) to the existence of self-sufficient constituents, such as atoms and all sorts of sub-atomic 'elementary particles', with characteristic and uncompromising natures.

This same fact can, however, be seen in an entirely different way. From the phenomenalistic point of view, given that there is some whole which is analysable there are bound to be parts. Some of those parts may in themselves be isolable wholes (microphenomena) which it would be appropriate to call molecules, atoms, etc. For reasons already discussed, this process of analysis cannot be extended to infinity but must end, at some stage or other, in ultimately unanalysable or indivisible parts, as in the original Greek idea of ατῶμωτ (atomos). These 'atoms', however, (or, as we nowadays conceive them, quanta) are not bits of 'substance', as the Greeks imagined but bits of emptiness. They are, that is to say, not things but ultimate gaps in the interrelations of things, 'shaped' in accordance with those interrelations, not in accordance with anything intrinsic or internal to themselves.

When you get down to that level, questions of how large or how small, how massive or how tenuous, etc., that ultimate unit is, intrinsically, are meaningless, since such comparisons can be made only in relation to other things, of which, in this case, there are none. But since that emptiness has a certain 'shape', in terms of those external interrelations among other things, then it may be 'reshaped' by altering those external relations. The extent to which we are able or unable to alter those relations defines, for the phenomenalist, the 'nature' of the unit in question -- which is not really the nature of

the emptiness itself (which is absurd) but the nature of the relational system in which it occurs.

'Smashing atoms', then, in our analysis means no such thing. Even in an 'as it were' sense, any convenience in this phrase is far outweighed by the nonsense it serves to perpetuate. What goes on in our nuclear laboratories is in no sense the study of 'physical substance', by bombardment or otherwise. It is simply discovering what dimensions and other 'degrees of freedom' the logos allows. We are permutating, in effect, the numbers and sorts of interrelation there are between the various natural units in all the different dimensions of phenomena.

This exercise gives much facility in rearranging things to our practical advantage. But that is a technological undertaking, entirely. As a means of providing any real understanding -- providing the 'Key to the Universe', as one Science television programme typically expresses it -- it is a vainglorious enterprise. There is no single 'Key' to be sought in just one area called Physics. The 'keys' are many and various and they 'hang not at one man's girdle'. Far less are they locked away in the metaphysical insides of 'substantial atoms'.

The nearest thing we find to the Greek idea of atomos is the quantum h. This is not a unit of 'permanent and indivisible substance' but an event, a discrete and irreducible bit of phenomenological information in the relative dimensions of ordinary mass, length and time. Nothing that has ever been done in our nuclear laboratories has come even close to 'splitting h'. We may 'reshape' that unit, reducing it in one dimension at the expense of increasing it in another; and in so doing we discover certain regularities in the system of things, which physicists call 'spectroscopies'.

These regularities, however, are not the mechanistically conceived 'keys to the universe'. They are informational combinations, and the 'combination-locks' they open are in the world before us not in micro- or macro-realms of metaphysical imagination.

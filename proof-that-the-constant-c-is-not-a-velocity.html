Proof that the Constant c is Not a Velocity

N. Vivian Pope

The Road Circuit Analogy

[Figure 1: A road circuit, 90 miles long, with depot, checkout at A, and gate at B.]

In Figure 1 is a road circuit along which a vehicle travels from A to B. The road is 90 miles long. Travelling at 30 m.p.h., therefore, the vehicle takes 3 hours to complete the circuit. Let A be a checkout station recording vehicles exiting the depot. At the far end of the circuit, B, is the re-entry gate for those vehicles.

Now it is characteristic of anything having what can sensibly be called a 'velocity' of motion that it can set out from A and then travel as far as the gate at B, regardless of whether the gate at that point is open or closed. If the gate is open, then the vehicle passes through and completes the circuit into the depot. If the gate is closed, then it cannot pass but gets held up at that point. In that case, a series of vehicles exiting from A creates, at the gate B, a traffic hold-up which will back up, eventually, to the road's full capacity, right up as far as the point of entry at A. The road is then choc-a-bloc. Nothing moves. This is what happens with anything having what is truly called a 'velocity'.

The Electrical Circuit

But now let us replace that road circuit with a simple electrical circuit, as in Figure 2, below. This consists of a loop of electrical wire, a battery with a switch to one side of the battery and a detector on the other.

[Figure 2: A simple electrical circuit with battery, conductor, detector at A, switch at B. The dotted rectangle encompasses the observational locale.]

The dotted rectangle encompasses the locality of the observer under whose immediate observation and control the switch and detector are included. Now electrical engineers know that:

i) When the switch at B is in the 'off' position, no flow of energy is detected at A, and that a flow of energy will be detected as soon as that switch at B is closed.

This means that when the switch at B is open there is no detectable flow of anything analogous to that of the vehicles passing the checkout at A in the road analogy. Nor, when that switch is open at B, is there anything analogous to the build-up of traffic and backing up of vehicles behind the ones which cannot pass at that point. Despite the capacity of the circuit to accommodate the flow, with the switch at B open, the detector at A reveals nothing passing through. The circuit remains completely empty until the switch at B is closed, even if a large capacitor is inserted in the circuit at B just before the (open) switch.

This proposition i) is a fact known to most electrical engineers, and it can be confirmed by anyone in a simple experiment with ordinary components, like that carried out by the writer. But if i) is a statement of fact, this next statement is no more than an assumption, namely:

ii) The speed of the conduction of the electricity around the circuit when the switch is closed is the finite speed $c$, which is approximately 300,000,000 metres per second. This implies that the conduction takes about 3.3 nanoseconds to traverse every metre of the circuit.

In view of the simple experiment just described, these two propositions, i) and ii) plainly contradict each other. For the notion of 'velocity' to make any sense in the electrical case, the conduction must start, as in the road example, from one pole of the battery, progress gradually around the circuit, like those vehicles, and finish at the opposite pole. However, if we assume that, in accordance with ii), the current flows from A through the detector at that point and on around the circuit, at 'velocity $c$', towards B, then that proposition implies that the flow from A has to be detected at A before it reaches the switch at B. In other words, the effect detected at A, of the closing of the switch at B, would precede that event by 3.3 nanoseconds for every metre length of the conductor – bearing in mind that the circuit may be any number of metres long. This implies a reversal of the natural order of causality. Since this does not and cannot happen, it reveals the absurdity of the assumption that $c$ is a 'velocity'.1

That should be sufficient in itself to convince the ordinary intelligent, commonsense-logical thinker that any notion that $c$ is a 'velocity' is just plain nonsense. What follows may therefore be regarded by the non-mathematical layman as redundant and of relevance only to students of mathematics.

The Mathematical Argument

Without that interpretation of $c$ as a 'velocity', the situation makes perfect natural sense. The POAMS version of the relativistic time-formula is:2

$$t_R = \sqrt{(s/c)^2 + t^2} \quad \text{(1)}$$

in which $c$ is not a 'velocity' of anything but simply what Bondi (see below) calls a dimensional distance-time conversion factor. This formula tells us that in the case of $c$, as soon as the switch at B is closed, every quantum of the energy that 'starts out' from A arrives instantly at B; that is, in the proper-time (quantum time) $t = 0$ in the formula. The same formula shows that when $t = 0$, the relative time $t_R$ is the observationally delayed time $s/c$. This means that the observer will read the flow in the detector after closing the switch by an interval which is the length $s$ of the circuit in metres divided by $c$. This avoids the absurdity of reversed causality.

It also fits perfectly the requirements of both Relativity and Quantum Theory, according to which, when the proper time (quantum time) $t$ of the contact between A and B is instantaneous (i.e., $t = 0$), the observer-time (relative time) $t_R$ is the delayed time $s/c$. Nor is there any reason to suppose that things would be any different in the case of light; that is, if the electric wire in Figure 2 were replaced with an ideal light-conduit (say, a vacuum-cored optic fibre).

We may logically conclude, then, that what is customarily called 'the velocity $c$' is not truly a velocity of anything but is an instantaneous proper-time interaction $t = 0$ between A and B as in Quantum Theory, having the observationally time-delayed characteristic $t_R = s/c$ as in Relativity Theory.

But, surely, someone will say, the fact that the dimensions of $c$ are those of distance divided by time makes it a velocity by definition. However, that is not true. The fact that 'All velocities are distances divided by time' does not logically entail 'All distances divided by time are velocities' any more than 'All lunatics are people' entails 'All people are lunatics.'

So everything points to the conclusion that $c$ cannot and need not be a 'velocity'. But, someone else will say, if $c$ is not a 'velocity', then what else can it be? The answer to that was provided by the discovery, made independently by H. Bondi and N.V. Pope in the 1960s,3 that all the important practical consequences of Relativity can be derived much more simply and empirically without Einstein's Second Postulate that 'light has a constant velocity $c$ in a vacuum'. They demonstrated, concurrently, that the 'velocity of light' in the sense of 'Einstein separation' need not be thought of as a 'velocity' at all but only as a pure constant interrelating the observationally (i.e., phenomenologically) projected dimensions of distance and time. (In any case, in a vacuum, there is literally nothing to measure a velocity against, constant or otherwise, which makes nonsense of any idea that light travels at a constant speed in or relative to a vacuum.)

It is surprising, then, that this logical solution was not realised right at the start of all the historical rigmarole about 'light' and 'relativity'. The logic of it is extremely simple. For a velocity to be truly a velocity it has to obey the rule of the composition of velocities. But, as Michelson and Morley discovered, $c$ does not obey that rule, whence it follows that $c$ cannot be a velocity. So, with all due respect for the undoubted genius of Einstein, it appears that his interpretation of the constant $c$ as a 'velocity' may possibly have been one of the biggest mistakes in the history of science.

Conclusion

The perceptive reader will have noted that the above argument confirms both relativity theory and quantum theory, thereby reconciling those two notoriously divided approaches to modern physics.

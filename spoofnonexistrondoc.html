How the Universe Began For the Universe to begin, the time had to be just right. But, of course, there was no such thing as time at first, so the universe had to wait for ages and ages with nothing happening before time was created in order to get things started.

When time did eventually pop into existence it was ten past eight in the morning. Space did not yet exist; it was eleven-twenty-four and 150 milliseconds GMT in the evening before that started. This was at a point of zero dimension, which point was, by then, so packed full of Universe that it just had to explode, which it did with such an almighty bang that it could be heard just about everywhere.

This was the celebrated ‘Big Bang’. After about 2.563(46) mini-microseconds GMT from start, space became as big as nearly one and three-quarter footballs – at least that would have been its size if there had been footballs or anything else around to measure it by – which, of course, there wasn’t, so we must simply take that size for granted.

But because there was nothing for the space to expand into, there was so much pressure and friction at the interface between the expanding space and the nothing that this caused a tremendous rise in temperature at the interface (actually, 18.2546(33) billion-trillion degrees Kelvin) – and, Boy, that’s hot!

Even a million Shuttle re-entry tiles wouldn’t stand that temperature. It was even hotter than a Soho curry!) But then, suddenly, at eleven-twenty-four in the afternoon, plus about 2.563(47) minimicroseconds, the barrier between the space and the nothing melted and space with its so densely packed-in matter, burst out into the nowhere.

This consequently became a somewhere – which, by then, of course, was an everywhere, and into this somewhere the space could now expand and cool. This it did, condensing into little hot globules which we now know as stars and galaxies, and the shock-waves of that explosion reverberated around to become what we know as light.

This, we now know, travels relative to the nothing at a constant speed of something like 2.99792458 metres per second. (How can any speed be measured with respect to a nothing, you ask. But why do you ask that? You must be dim or something!) Meanwhile, the integrity of the whole was conserved by means of interactions involving tiny exotic non-particles called nonexistrons.

These were little compact bits of the nothing which whizzed to and fro, like angels and cherubs, keeping everything in contact with everything else. These non-particles are technically categorised as Hosons. (named in honour of their inventor, the illustrious train robber, Ronnie Biggs who, the clergy found, had altruistically hidden bags and bags of money in the crypt of their church for the funding of innovative extramural projects.

This was after the church, like so many others, became a bingo hall, after alterations kindly carried out by the firm of Ledstealers and Scarpers, inc.). Anyway, these nonexistrons, as bits of non-matter do not have to obey the laws of ordinary mechanics or, indeed, any other laws, including those of ordinary logic.

As products of pure twenty-seven-point three-and-a-half-dimensional mathematics, they travel everywhere at the speed of dark. This, you see, is because, obviously, dark is always there ahead of the light and is therefore much faster by always being there first.

Indeed. these non-particles are instantaneous, which means that they are everywhere all at once; but that’s okay, because being non-existent there is no danger of them banging into one another, which might otherwise be a big problem.

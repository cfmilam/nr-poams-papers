In Search of the ANPA Philosophy Spring 1995

N. Vivian Pope — 1995

In Search of the ANPA Philosophy Is nature a product of Mathematics or is mathematics a product of Nature? Rationalists believe the first; Empiricists believe the second. The Rationalist sees nature, in his mind's eye, as a unity, a Cosmos or Universe.

For the Empiricist, nature is as he actually perceives it, a polyverse or pluriverse, in which there is both harmony and conflict. Rationalists regard harmony as everywhere mathematically assured and all appearances of conflict as illusory. Empiricists see the conflict as real and the world as, at best, a balance of opposing principles which the Greeks called natural justice.

For the Rationalist, the occurrence of every event is mathematically predetermined. For the Empiricist, these events are never more than probabilistic, so that every moment provides an opportunity for either shaping history or leaving it to its own random devices.

In Rationalism the balance of everything is always mathematically perfect, so that there can be no true imbalance in nature. Philosophically speaking, therefore, nothing can be right or wrong, good or bad. It simply is, as Mathematics directs. This means that for the true Rationalist there can be no question of justice or morality in whatever transpires.

Since for him there is no pain or pleasure except in human imagination, he sees no alternative in this 'uncaring world' but to endure it bravely and resignedly, as the Stoics taught, or to 'have a ball' as the Epicureans advised. For the Empiricist, on the other hand, pain and pleasure and the issues of justice and morality these entail are all part of real natural justice, demanding, so far as man is concerned, the dedicated and continual balancing-act portrayed by the statue with the scales atop the Old Bailey.

Such, then, are the extremes of the philosophical spectrum. Where in it shall we place the 'Alternative Natural Philosophy' which is the basis of ANPA? As a member of the Association I have been asked to apply my analytical skills as a philosopher (albeit a non-mathematician), in search of an answer to that question – as a member put it, 'to dig out and articulate the ANPA philosophy'.1 Now it is not as though I have any clue, to start with, what that ANPA philosophy is.

Indeed, from discussions with founder members I have learned that there has never been any clear philosophical basis to the movement but only a heterogeneous collection of philosophy-like assumptions which seem, somehow, to serve the same end.2 As it seems to me, that end is to open up the 'secrets of the universe' by the method of juggling numbers and combinations of numbers, like a burglar trying to crack a safe.

What philosophy, then, is needed for that? The answer is, none – so long as we know that the 'lock' we are trying to open is a combination lock. With prior knowledge that the 'lock' works on the principle of combining numbers and combinations of numbers in hierarchical series, that method is assured.

We simply spin the wheel while with our stethoscope, as it were, we hear the numbers click over one by one, and carry on in that way till 'the door' swings open. But how can we know what sort of Lock, if any, conceals 'The Secrets of the Cosmos'? Is there some automatic assurance that we are getting somewhere in Big

Science, for instance, by 'cracking the combinations' of atoms and sub-atomic particles? How valid, philosophically-speaking, is the assumption that in dividing and classifying all those various constituents we are tripping the tumblers, one by one, of that Cosmic Combination-Lock?

This unspoken assumption stems from Democritus. It is, of course, that basically there is a great void, of indefinite and continuous threedimensional extension in all directions, in which bits of matter float about, and that the world as we perceive it is the product of mechanical accidents among these simple constituents.

Further contributors to that view, such as Galileo, Newton, Dalton, Boltzmann and so on have built on this assumption, so that the picture we now automatically retain at the back of our minds is of that same self-sufficient void but with the atoms in it having further constituents which, like the atoms themselves, hold together and fly apart under the influences of all sorts of 'forces' acting in vacuo after the fashion of Newton's 'gravitational force'.

The aim of the science that commands the biggest budgets in that Democritan line of enquiry is therefore, as Weisskopf, one of the founding directors of CERN put it, 'To discover the ultimate constituents of matter and the ultimate driving forces of nature'.

The ANPA methodology, it seems to me, reflects that Democritan philosophy, though its aim seems to be not so much experimentally to 'safe-crack' atoms as to 'outguess' that process by the mathematical juggling of pure numbers – on the Rationalist's assumption, of course, that the 'secrets' one is trying to unravel are of that mathematical, combinatorial kind.

But, say the Combinatorial Hierarchists, how else could it be? How could the universe possibly hold together and function other than in that perfect, Mathematical way? Now that is not a question to be tackled without prior analysis. For instance, what can be meant by 'universe' in that context if not the all-encompassing 'void' of Democritus, Galileo and Newton?

But since the advent of special relativity, that absolutely extended, God's-eye-view 'void' can no longer signify. No bits of matter tick time at the same 'universal' rate, and the distances between them are different relatively to each and every one.

So where is that self-extended 'void' of the atomistic philosophy? What 'universe' can be defined in modern quantum-relativistic terms? The logical alternative to this materialist philosophy of Democritus, Galileo and Newton is, of course, the relativistic philosophy of Berkeley, Hume, Kant and Mach.

This takes as its basis of all enquiry, not the Democritan God's-eye-view of matter and space, but the world as we actually perceive it, with all the senses and instruments at our disposal. This is an altogether different philosophical 'ball-game'. In it, for instance, there is no way we can assume that the proper times, proper lengths and proper masses of things form any permanent interrelations among themselves of the sort that defines the classical 'universe'. 3 In relativity, nothing is related except via the observer, the ultimate cognitive information-processor, without which there can be no information as such, hence no world – indeed, no anything that it makes sense to describe.

All there would be, if it could be imagined, would be ultimately dissociated relata, be these conceived as atoms, atomic sub-particles, quanta, pure numbers or whatever, with no relations whatsoever between them – what Kant called an unspeakable disordered manifold of noumena. On the other hand, in phenomena we have the relata and relations together, with

no problem whatsoever as to how they are joined, because the way they are joined or interrelated is self-evident. Without observation (cognition) there can be no informational distinction between even a simple one and nought, far less any question of combinations and hierarchical combinations (or 'chains' or 'strings') of these.

We may, of course, assume there is a God who has it all laid out before him as we might perceive it on a background continuum such as a piece of paper or a blackboard, but it makes no sense in the context of relativity and quantum theory. The nearest we get to that kind of holism is to think of an informational community of observers, replete with their observing instruments, of which every object known to them is an example.

In this case, the 'universe', such as it is, is one of informational concourse in which stochasticity takes the place of mechanical determinacy. A relativistic approach to combinatorics must therefore take account of the continually changing relations among elements in the cognitive contexts of observation.

From this relativistempiricist point of view there is no more chance of explaining the world in the old 'atom-smashing' way than of pulverising a painting, separating all its various bits of pigment into piles and explaining the painting in terms of mathematical combinations of those bits.

Any sensible person knows that intentions may be every bit as accountable as physical forces for the shaping of things, from paintings to pieces of rock. Think, for instance, of the creativity that goes into ordinary, everyday speech and action, especially in, say, commissioning and building a space-station or something like the Great Wall of China.

Unfortunately, relativity, which is the antithesis of mechanical materialism, has been introduced as though it were little more than a natural extension of that 'atomistic' philosophy, and no doubt it will be a while yet before relativistic physics and relativistic philosophy can become fully combined and articulated.

Be that as it may, it must already be sufficiently clear that the combinatorics of matter and/or number can no longer proceed in the old Democritan way. The Whole whose existence we might try to explain in quantum-relativistic physics is nothing like that of classical physics.

Insofar as it may be conceived as a whole, it consists of phenomena, and there is no single phenomenon we can sensibly think of which includes all the rest – in the way that 'the universe' of classical physics and maths was thought of as a great 'Room' containing everything simultaneously and eternally. 4 From the relativist-empiricist standpoint, therefore, it is to that pluriverse or manifold of phenomena that any 'combinatorial' method must be applied, not to the 'Blackboard Universe' of classical, absolutist physics.

How, then, shall we define the true role of combinatorics in that quantumrelativistic framework? Certainly it is not to continue trying to discover how things and the various bits of things relate 'in themselves', behind the scenes – like the vain quest for a 'proper-time GMT' of differently moving particles or for underlying 'gravitational' and other interrelations between their proper-masses.

In quantum relativity, the elements of matter have none of those sorts of interrelations that could possibly form the 'absolute and primordial' space and time of classical physics, nor what Einstein and Minkowski envisaged as an underlying space-time continuum.

Those bits of matter can interrelate only spectroscopically; that is to say, in discrete to-ings and fro-ings of action, in quantum units h, of the sort we observe in light

spectra. These interrelations are as expressed in formulae like that of Balmer and Rydberg but including all the various interactions, nuclear and gravitational, which Weisskopf called 'The Three Spectroscopies'. An axiomatic or combinatorial 'spectroscopy' of that all-embracing sort is perhaps difficult to contemplate.

But it is not impossible. Nineteen years ago I attempted such a thing myself, in a published flow-chart entitled 'Towards a New Economy of Fundamental Physical Concepts'. Now, however, I regard that as no more than just a pointer in the sort of 'combinatorial' direction which I would want to take if I were a mathematician - that is, along the lines suggested by the subtitle of that piece: 'An Axiomatic Deduction of Some Central Physical Principles from a Minimum of Phenomenological Premises.' (I still have one or two copies of that chart which I can send to anyone who might be interested.)

Now where that puts me in respect of the Combinatorial Hierarchies, I have so far been unable to determine. Whenever I enquire about that subject, among ANPA members or in textbooks, all I receive are answers which begin something like 'Take a set of numbers (points, elements, or whatever)...' and then get lost in Rationalistic labyrinths of mathematics.

Yet I do feel, if only intuitively, that ANPA and I are somehow on the same track – or at least on a track going in the same direction if not of similar gauge. My relative-empiricist 'spectroscopic' approach, for instance, is distinctly Diophantine (or so my Keele Mathematics colleague, Anthony Osborne, instructs me), insofar as it deals with integers, of the sort signified by the ns in the Balmer series.

Moreover, I am decidedly a discretist in the sense much used at ANPA by Pierre Noyes. My philosophical approach, however, is essentially Empiricist and Heracleitan, as opposed to what seems to me the distinctly Rationalistic, almost Leibnizian, emphasis of the ANPA philosophy as interpreted by Ted Bastin after the fashion of the early Wittgenstein. (In contrast to Ted, I take my departure from the work of the later 'reformed' Wittgensein.)

In some ways, then, I have much sympathy with the 'cybernetic' approach of the BCS view, as represented at ANPA by Peter Marcer and Keith Bowden. Indeed, it seems to me that just about everyone in ANPA, including, I suppose, myself, goes along with the Parker-Rhodes type of ontology, which, as Keith expresses it, is that 'information is the basic stuff of the universe' – except that for reasons which I hope are now plain, I would eschew all traditionalist talk of a 'universe', preferring my own paraphrase: 'information is the basic stuff of nature.'7.Alison Watson's relationist stance is also, I think, much in line with this information-based view of nature and physics.

But, for the reasons already stated, I reserve judgement regarding her metaphysical 'universalism'. Surely, also, everyone at ANPA more or less agrees with Emmanuel Ransford who, as reported in Clive Kilmister's summary of the last meeting, says that 'consciousness' is not reducible but is somehow immanent in matter.'

And Eddie Gery's description of a strator, as an entity analogous to a cine-film but generalised into a continuum' (a statistical, observational continuum, one supposes) must surely be interesting in any information-processing interpretation of quantum-relativity.

Mike Manthey's thesis is also, it seems to me, similar to that of the BCS, so far as I am able to follow his specialised computer-mathematical arguments. Mike and I certainly seem to agree that it is a confusion to talk 'discretist' about physical

phenomena and 'continuist' about the space-time dimensions of those phenomena, which, as Mike puts it, is like 'trying to whistle with flour in your mouth.' Tony Deakin argues that to do that is alright for reasons of ordinary everyday expediency, and I agree with that – for those ordinary everyday purposes but, as I argued at ANPA 16, not when we are trying to sort out our ideas on the close-reasoned philosophical level, which I take to be at least one of the basic aims of a statedly philosophical society like ANPA.

In these philosophical contexts you cannot say, as combinatorial and cybernetics people do, that everything reduces to discrete and ultimately irreducible informational elements (numbers or quanta or whatever) and then keep chatting on in such a way as to imply that these absolutely fundamental elements are linked together by something even more fundamental but non-discrete – such as 'light' travelling in the old absolute and continuous way in the old continuous background of space and time.

If you insist on using that sort of 'double-talk', then no commonsense person, let alone any philosopher, will ever understand you. To resolve that at least (and some members now seem to be more or less in agreement) should be a fair start on the road to 'sorting-out the ANPA philosophy'.

To decide where we go from there with our combinatorics, whether it should be along lines of relativistempiricist or rationalist-absoultist axiomatics, should be, I would say, 'no trivial pursuit'. IN SUM, THEN, I would say that there is a central contradiction in modern physics which any philosophical society like ANPA needs to address.

This is that on the one hand quantum physics reduces everything to discrete and irreducible actionelements, informational sequences of which are the basis of physical phenomena. On the other hand the relativistic arm of that same physics, as Einstein interpreted it, embeds those discrete quantum elements in the continuous vacuum of classical physics, where they are 'linked' by 'light' – or at any rate, by 'waves' of one kind or another – conceived in the old, mechanistic, 'space-travelling' way.

These two idea-systems can never logically cohere, and I would look to ANPA, as foremost in the discretist movement, to give a clear lead in removing this contradiction. This it may do by tidying up its act, lingusitically speaking, and jettisoning those vestiges of continuism in Einsteinian relativity that have been shown to be unnecessary and by coming down decisively on the side of a properly formulated and philosophically articulate discretism.

But also, in my view, the Association needs to decide, for the sake of its exponents and critics alike, just what are the elements of its 'combinatoric' processes. Are they the abstract 'pure numbers' of the Rationalists or are they the Empiricists' phenomenological irreducibles?

In this latter case, there can be no special problem concerning the reality of the system to which the method is applied since, for the empiricist, that reality is immediate and self-evident. For the Rationalists, however, since the relations between abstract numbers are essentially cognitive, then they need to state, clearly and unambiguously, just where, if not entirely in the confines of their own imaginations or in the assumed 'Mind of God' – which is the same thing – this 'combinatorial' cognition takes place.

And since relativistic and quantum physics, between them, have disposed of that absolute 'God's-eye-view' reference-frame that has been assumed by mathematicians from Pythagoras to our modern David Bohm

and Stephen Hawking, those Rationalists 'have a problem'!

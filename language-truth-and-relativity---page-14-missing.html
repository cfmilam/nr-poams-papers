LANGUAGE, TRUTH AND RELATIVITY

What is truth? To answer this question has been the aim of philosophers ever since man began think. Some might say that truth is something existing beyond human experience, something of which we can at best obtain only small glimpses or else by some mystical insight towards which the soul may be cultivated. Others might say that there is no such thing, really, as truth, whence they have the problem of trying to decide whether and in what sense that statement can be true.

But what else could truth be than what remains when you have got rid of error. That answer defines the basis of the kind of philosophy which is here being offered for the reader's consideration. Truth, we shall argue, is fully attainable; and any view which puts truth beyond the reach of the common man should, we think, be rejected in the interests of democracy and practicality. This does not mean that the only truths we seek are those having this social and practical kind of significance. That defines a doctrinaire approach to philosophy, from which kind of thing we would wish to be dissociated. Instead we might say that we seek truth purely for its own sake; but that sounds as though we have already decided, in some sense, what truth is. If, however, we say that our aim is to seek out error, then that is not nearly so pretentious. Everyone knows what an error is: it is something that fails either the test of logical consistency or that of practical sufficiency.

Truth and error, moreover, are never entirely abstract, but are ultimately connected with the way in which we perceive. But what is perception? Traditionally we think of it as a psychological reconstruction of external reality, built up out of bits of energy obtained by the senses and conveyed throughout the central nervous system by processes of essentially the same energetic kind.

This external reality, the source and sink of all those energies which underly perception, is then called physical, as opposed to our perceptions of it, which are called mental or psychological. The dimensions of this physical world are those of mass, length and time, through various combinations of which all other physical quantities and qualities are derived.

In this physical world there are deemed to be no qualities or quantities which are not expressed, ultimately, in these physical terms. In the mental or psychological world, on the other hand, there are all sorts of other qualities and quantities which cannot be expressed in purely physical terms. In these 'other dimensions' we have those subjective and evaluative (as opposed to objective and factual) characteristics which distinguish our experience of the world from that world itself.

The 'physical' and the 'mental' worlds so described are therefore mutually exclusive: that is to say, no physical quality as such is directly perceived, but only indirectly, in terms of psychological (that is, subjective and evaluative) representations, while no mental or psychological quality as such exists in the purely physical world. Even at the place where the two realms interconnect (that is, in the body and brain of the percipient) the physical and psychological manifestations of the events they share are radically and completely different.

The bits of information which are the psychic aspects of the energies impinging on the nerves are referred to as 'sensory stimuli', and the impressions of the external world which we form in perception are psychological constructs out of these stimuli. The kinds of construction depend, however, in large part, both on the psychological and physiological predisposition of the observer. The physiological predispositions include the layout of nerve-paths and the positioning of nerve-endings, also things like physical condition and state of health; and the psychological factors include things like personal predisposition, character, personality; social, aesthetic and moral attitudes, practical judge-

In the main, however, the psychological reconstruction will depend on things like the direction, strength, intensity, frequency, etc., of the physical energies impinging on the senses. The psychological wholes built up in this way then correspond to the world of ordinary and scientific experience.

To the extent that such individual experience is always, in part, psychological or subjective, as well as limited in its physical ranges, objectivity is provided by the kind of community or consensus of experience that frames itself in public language. By this means we learn to distinguish the common evaluative criteria by which the community assesses the reality of the physical situation, and without which no truly objective measure of experience is possible.

Now if that should be deemed the 'standard' or commonsense view of the relationship between Man and reality, then it is easy to see how, on any more thoughtful analysis, the view runs into difficulty, for it contains in its own basic precepts, the seeds of its own logical destruction. Just suppose, for instance, that one is asked to give a clear and non-subjective account of the 'purely physical world' that human experience is supposed to represent. How can this be done? Well, of course, it cannot, because any account we give of this 'purely physical world' is necessarily given in representational terms, and cannot therefore be 'purely physical'. This problem which is as old as the 'standard view of perception' that we have described, is identified in philosophy as The Problem of Knowledge.

Differences in attitude towards this very perplexing problem have, in effect, split traditional human thought right down the middle, creating a wound in our understanding that has wept for over two thousand years. The cleft is defined by the stances taken, respectively, by those who have taken this Problem seriously and those, on the other hand, who have not. Those who, characteristically, have chosen to ignore the Problem of Knowledge are, by and large, those scientists and technologists who follow in the tradition set by Isaac Newton, in what is now called Classical, or Newtonian, Science. According to that Science, the logical contradiction leading to the Problem may be dismissed as 'merely philosophical'. The 'purely physical world' and the world of scientific experience are then tacitly assumed to be one and the same. In other words, the external world, as it is in itself, and as it appears in the mind of the utmost qualified (Newtonian) scientist are preserved identical.

And in the minds of the utmost qualified Newtonian scientists that world consists of three absolutely autonomous ingredients: brute matter, empty space, and an absolutely universal time, which registers simultaneously throughout the whole length, breadth and depth of space. Physical interactions, according to this view, then consist either of mechanical collisions between masses, such as 'atoms' and other mass-corpuscles, or of much subtler influences commonly known as 'field-forces'. These forces, variously identified by names such as 'magnetism', 'gravitation' and 'electric force', then pervade the depths of the otherwise empty space. Like the surface of a pond, the balance of such tensions is occasionally disturbed by the movements of bodies, creating waves and ripples of electromagnetic (and gravitational) energy, which travel for perhaps eons until they wash up at random, greatly attenuated, on some unknown shore. Meanwhile, the distance they cover in this absolute space during the absolute time defines the 'absolute velocity' (c) of their motion, where c is established experimentally as approximately three hundred million metres per second.

Inclusion of this velocity c, called the 'velocity of light in vacuo', as a fundamental constant in the measuring of mass, length and time, signals the change from the science of Newton to that of Einstein. But what has taken the place of these 'purely physical' ingredients? What kind of new idea, in the minds of the utmost-qualified of modern scientists, must we now identify with the external and 'purely physical' world? That is where we encounter no small amount of confusion. According to many writers, the changes in thinking that Einstein's Relativity has brought about have been profound. Among the more philosophical writers, however, there seems to be a growing body of opinion that these changes have not been nearly so profound as their proponents would have us believe. The view which will be expressed here is that, philosophically speaking, Relativity has landed us in a mess. The dogmatic realism of Newtonian Science has crumbled into dust; but it has been replaced by nothing that can commonly be regarded as more plausible. Although we live in a world of science and technology that is formulated in terms of relativity, our commonplace modes of thinking remain those of an outmoded Science. Never, therefore, has Man felt more of a stranger to reality and truth than at the

present time. For all its material or practical sufficiency, the social effects of scientific relativism are therefore, one might say, totally disruptive.

To close the gap in understanding that Relativity has created, newer and ever more sophisticated 'models of nature' are perennially produced, mostly on the lines of General Relativity (Einstein's later work). These theories seek, typically, to replace the the old 'corpuscles in a void' interpretation by that of a cosmical continuum, or field. In this view, not only bodies and what we ordinarily conceive as their motions, but also the forces which cause these motions (e.g., gravitation) lose their reality and become, instead, distortions of a universal 'field-continuum'. Space then becomes no more than a relative cross-section of this substratum which the observer makes, according to his particular orientation — different kinds of orientation making different kinds of slices. The metrical irregularities in this substratum, encountered in accordance with the differences in orientation, then generate our experience of physical phenomena in the way that music is reproduced by the needle in the groove of a gramophone record. In this highly abstruse and metaphysical interpretation, not only the space, but also the objects it contains and the light travelling across it are the 'sounds' in a phenomenological symphony, generated out of 'grooves', or geodesics, in a universal, four-dimensional continuum. And although in its real (that is, in its total spatio-temporal) aspect, neither the beginning or the end are distinguishable from the middle, any orientation in it somehow generates phenomena which are consonant with human experience.

Now there is little point in elaborating on the varieties of theory which have been constructed around this central theme of General Relativity. Suffice it to say that they are all as remote as Andromeda from the possibility of being incorporated into commonplace experience. So long has Relativity been with us, yet so intractable is the problem of giving it a plausible general interpretation, that something seems to be wrong with the whole conception. Characteristic of the standard classical, or Newtonian, view of science was the conception of the physical world of brute matter, empty space and universal time as somehow constituting an autonomous and comprehensive whole: that is, as a universe whose integrity owes nothing to those evaluative and predicative powers supplied by human perception. This, tacitly or otherwise, endows the 'purely physical world' with para-psychological characteristics. Some scientists, like Albert Einstein and Sir James Jeans, quite unselfconsciously ascribe to this underlying physical 'reality' the characteristics of a God. Others, seeking to prevent science from becoming embroiled in religious and theological controversies, prefer to use the more agnostic term 'nature' to signify the barest essentials of this para-psychological characteristic. Few, however, have matched the agnosticism, in this sense, of the philosopher Kant who refused to see any integrity whatever in this 'purely physical world' other than what was given to it by human perception.

Now it is quite certain that in the account of physics which Relativity gives, nothing in this respect has changed. Whatever ineffable spirit, god, demon or panpsyche was implicit in the Newtonian physicist's concept of 'nature' remains firmly embedded in the 'nature' of modern physics; for the para-psychological and pan-psychological implications of Einstein's 'universe of space-time' are, of course, the same as on the classical view. This 'four-dimensional continuum' or 'substratum' is as autonomous in its integrity, as independent of human perception and predication, as the 'cosmos' of classical science. The only change (if it is a change) is that 'nature' now comprehends not only the whole of cosmical geography but also the whole of cosmical history and futurity. The 'God of the Scientists' has become distinctly four-dimensional.

What new conception of reality should mankind adopt as the basis of commonsense? How is man to conceive his relationship to the external physical world? External space and time are no longer separate and independent dimensions but are intimately related by the constant c: in other words, there is no length but the distance that light travels per unit of time, and there is no time but what it takes for light to travel the unit length. And since the measures length and time —and mass— are so totally dependent on the velocity of light, which is constant for any observer irrespective of his motion relative to other observers, then the fundamental physical measures, mass, length and time are now observer-dependent, differing from observer to observer as these move relatively to each other. This extends to matter and its duration the same kind of variations due to perspective that occur with lengths in space.

These relative aspects, or perspective variations, in the measures length, time and mass, which extend to all the derivative quantities and qualities also, might seem to suggest that Relativity provides some fundamentally new insight into the relationship of the percipient to the physical world. Certainly, some very radical changes in outlook are implied: so what prevents these changes in outlook from taking place? The answer, we think, is that Relativity is formulated in such a way as to suggest that it is concerned, not with the world of ordinary experience, but with the old metaphysical 'world beyond'. That is to say, in accordance with the bogus division that classical science created between the 'world of mind' and the 'world of matter', Relativity manages to fall, somehow, into the old Newtonian habit of distinguishing Physics as the 'Science of Matter'. And for many who, accepting the same bogus dichotomy, continue to observe the absolute distinction between 'Physics' and 'Psychology', Einstein's Relativity irrelevant. What does it matter whether physics is dominated by Einstein or by Newton if you are not a physicist?

What, then, can scientists mean by 'the relation between our ideas and physical reality', other than the relation between one set of ideas and another?

This becomes the more obvious as old and trusted conventions fail and have to be replaced by new theories of an ad hoc and generally incoherent kind — like that of Relativity, which has become a by-word for obscurity among the populace at large. As theories and interpretations of theories proliferate the consensus as to what constitutes the 'underlying realities', or the 'cold hard world of objective fact', is gradually replaced by a rash of subjectivism, which those who remain more conservative refer to as a 'loss of commonsense'. But commonsense cannot be re-established simply by fiat. Those who do not suffer this loss at first hand suffer it at second hand, at third hand, and so on; and gradually, by degrees, the altruistic conception of consensus and community breaks down into subjectivism, scepticism, solipsism and nihilism —manifest among non-intellectuals simply as a vague and general feeling of frustration and despair.

What is required, in other words, is to re-establish some commonly acceptable criterion for judging which ideas are true and which are false.

Let us now return to what, at the beginning, we identified as the Problem of Knowledge. While this remains unsolved there is always, as we said, the 'lurking suspicion' in the minds of the deeper-thinking individuals, that the 'physical world' that forms the vital bond of objectivity between men's ideas may be, in the end, just another of these ideas —or, at best, an authoritarian convention. It is by no means difficult to see that while this problem remains unsolved our present troubles are inevitable. Nor is it difficult to see that this is not the kind of problem to be solved by politicians or social theorists. You cannot simply legislate answers when things like logic and mathematics go awry.

Our suggested cure for the conceptual cancer called the Problem of Knowledge is as follows. Whereas most philosophers in the past have taken the accustomed conclusion (that 'it is all ideas') as a kind of scepticism which is to be avoided at all costs, we take this conclusion as an inevitability and then operate from there. All attempts to avoid this conclusion, we would say, are motivated by nothing more than a kind of superstition, based on a fundamental misconception of what an 'idea' means. To most people nowadays, the word 'idea' signifies something very different from what it meant prior to the bogus dichotomy that, as we have seen, was created by classical science between the 'world of matter' and the 'world of mind'. Aristotelian philosophers and scientists knew no such dichotomy. For them, an idea simply meant the form of a thing, as opposed to its analysed particulars. It meant the manner in which such particulars are related, giving to things the kind of character and organisation that is manifest at the level of ordinary perception —what the German psychologists have called Gestalten.

Now the way in which modern science departs from classical science is, as we have seen, in respect of its relativism; and relativism is concerned, by definition, with relations. Implicitly, then, modern science is concerned with what the Greeks called logos, or what we nowadays (somewhat inadequately) call language. Modern 'Linguisticism', with what so many see as no more than its 'preoccupation with words', is actually no less than philosophical first-cousin to modern scientific Relativism. The outrage they both occasion to those steeped in the old Newtonian way of thinking is on account of one and the same family characteristic.

So much for the family resemblance between modern philosophy and modern science. The differences, on the other hand, which make these hostile to one-another, as well as distinctly vulnerable in their respective ways, is that while the logos they similarly express is both qualitative and quantitative, the one tends to favour logic to the exclusion of mathematics, and the other to favour mathematics to the exclusion of logic. Thus, what can be regarded as 'mere words', with the one, can be regarded as 'mere numbers' with the other. Neither, in this case, is fully acceptable to commonsense.

Now the logical compulsion to accept the existence of logos does not entail having to go all the way with the theists. Freedom to choose and to depend upon faith remains absolute in this religious direction. On the other hand, there also remains the secular need for limiting faith only to what is rational and demonstrable, which was the original 'family business' of both science and philosophy and which used to be called the search for truth. On this level of concern, the only valid subject for study is the finite and immediate logos —which is to say no more than the logic and mathematics of human experience.

The answer is a very definite no; because, as we have seen, for the sceptical conclusion to be true, the words expressing that view can be allowed no significance, which straightaway nullifies the 'truth' of any such sceptical conclusion. The sceptical view of the insignificance of language flatly contradicts itself. There is no way whatever in which it can be true.

So that is one thing of which we can be certain: some words have practical signification, since to deny this involves one in logical contradiction. And it is this practical character that words have, in their logical interrelations with one-another, which makes it sensible to compare the practical efficiency of different uses of language. For words to have this practical signification, of course, it is essential that they are not taken merely in abstraction. To abstract the formal characteristics of a word (like its sound or its status as a bare logical symbol) is to deprive it of true meaning. By a word, therefore (and the same applies to practical mathematical symbols) we mean something which is used to frame actual experience, a logical delineation of anything which needs to be identified in any of the conventional categories like physics, geography, history, psychology, fiction, or whatever.

It may be noticed that in this context the old 'Problem of Knowledge' which, as we saw, is so inimical to the prospect of social harmony, has simply disappeared. The reason, of course, is that no fundamental distinction now occurs between 'physical' and 'psychological'. The only relation between these that we can now sensibly discuss is the logical relation between one practical convention in the use of language and another. Nor is the conventional distinction inevitable. With the continual evolution of language (if it be allowed to continue), other more logically and practically efficient ways of speaking may develop in such a way as to remove this arbitrary distinction.

And the same thing goes for any other such conventional schism in language. The advantage of having ontology under the control of convention is that there are no a priori limits to the making of adjustments. The only criterion to be

The language thus described is no 'mere language' which is to be set aside from that which language signifies, and in contrast to which it could be regarded as 'just words', or 'no more than numbers'. The language and what it signifies are inseparable. Without the language you cannot speak about —far less oppose to language— what that language customarily signifies. You cannot conceive the the possibility of anything 'outside and beyond' language, other than in terms of that language. Without those logical relations that are internal to language we could not even formulate an awareness that there are things unknown, things as yet undiscovered (the so-called intentional, or transcendental, function of language).

And this, as we say, by no means entails any of those socially enervating conclusions such as scepticism, subjectivism, solipsism and nihilism, to which the Western mind is prone. So long as we do not lose sight of the practical dimension of language as the vital criterion of meaning and efficiency, the bogey of scepticism is kept at bay. Seen as no more than a figment of the metaphysical imagination it is soon recognised as having no place in practical and scientific philosophy.

But what about the physical world of classical and modern science, the world of Newton and of Einstein? Is this now to be regarded as no more than a convention of language? Well, why not? Newton's world turned out that way, so why shouldn't Einstein's? Also, what else could these 'worlds' be, on the analysis given, but conventions of language. What are 'space', 'time', 'mass', 'force', 'charge', etc., but classificatory conventions? In a world (if such can be imagined) where there is neither human nor superhuman, nor any parallel form of awareness, are there such things as 'space', 'time', 'mass', and all those derivative concepts that we find in our textbooks of science and technology? What standpoint would we take, in imagination, with regard to these things? Would it

  • 18 -

be the finite kind of standpoint we are accustomed to as human beings, or would it be one of cosmical ubiquity, like that of a god; or would it be, perhaps, that of a totally mindless atom? The concepts that we frame are different in each case, and in trying to imagine such things we merely tie ourselves up in metaphysical knots.

Without making either implicit or explicit appeal to parahuman or superhuman agencies, therefore, our answer must be that physical science, on any interpretation, is no more than a convention of language. And what is it that we experience that it is necessary to frame in such language? The answer must be: that which the language expresses. And there it must rest if we are to avoid metaphysical conundrums.

But how, it will be asked, can a science of such conventions hang together? What can give it reality without an underlying 'physical universe', or world of 'cold, hard, uncompromising fact' to give it substance? The straight answer is that what makes these conventions hang together is logic (i.e., logic and mathematics), which is the science of relations internal to language. It is this logical function of language which gives to this set of conventions their reality — so long as the logic is applied, as we have said, to uses of language which retain their practical content. A logically consistent language of practicality is then a coherent conventional whole, answering to what is commonly called scientific understanding.

But one of the problems which modern scientists have encountered, it will be said, is that the more scientific your experience becomes, nowadays, the less it conforms to the rules of logic. Physicists, for instance, have been forced to abandon the criterion of logical coherence, or 'commonsense understanding', and to adopt instead a principle of eclecticism, or 'complementarity' — which simply means the acceptance of views which do not hang logically together. Now that is undoubtedly true; but the fault, in this case, may lie with those dimensional conventions we insist on applying. Perhaps those dimensional conventions

  • 19 -

should now be revised and some new dimensional alternatives tried that might succeed in maintaining overall coherence. Could it be that our linguistic evolution has led us up a blind alley? Then perhaps it is time we looked around for some other, a-historical, line of development. Since it is all a matter of convention the answer to the problem presented by the incoherence of modern science is, surely, to become ... Well, unconventional!

What we should do, therefore, is to look at present scientific conventions dispassionately in the light of pure practical logic. Our ability to do any really useful measure of logical rethinking, in the past, has no doubt been inhibited by the customary notion that there is but one, unique and unchangeable, reality — the one we usually associate with 'physical science'— and that when this logically falls apart there is no recourse but to accept confusion as a 'universal principle'. The advantage, however, of seeing physical science as no more than a set of conventions is that it puts a more liberal and less sceptical interpretation on things. Where conventions do not hang together we simply make more and more logical adjustments until they do. There is no reason, indeed, why such readjustments should not be of the most radical and revolutionary kind, so long as these succeed in closing the old logical gaps without opening up too many new ones. Einstein's Relativity goes a very long way in this direction, but it quite evidently does not go far enough. True, it has given us nuclear energy, but the social benefits of this, so far, have been eclipsed by the threat it has brought, of global catastrophe — not so much from the effects of The Bomb as from its deleterious effect on commonsense understanding.

What is scarcely to be doubted, therefore, is that relativity theory, as formulated at present, is far too complicated for general consumption. Nor will it help if every student of the subject, as he grows to maturity, does no more than jealously guard, against all who would seek to 'corrupt' it, his knowledge of this admirable, highly abstruse and totally esoteric absurdity. The absurdity, it seems, is not endemic to relativism but arises from its inability, so far,

  • 20 -

to release itself from the dead grip of the old Newtonian dogmatism. Relativity thus remains, impossibly, a theory of human perspectives in a world which, like that of classical science, lies essentially beyond human perception. It is not a new theory in the philosophical sense, therefore, but a conceptual hybrid, a glorified piece of modern eclecticism whose use, in the interests of achieving a new general understanding, is severely (and, we think, unnecessarily) limited.

But so long as scientists continue to imagine that what they are looking for is 'Nature's Secrets', and that in rummaging around for these it does not really matter what kind of shambles is left in the way of language, Western Science will continue to be a conceptual 'Klondike' in a rush for philosophical fool's gold. What we must realise is that language is no merely incidental means to truth and reality but the very end in which these verities, if anywhere, are to be found. Realising this we might be persuaded, all round, to treat it with a higher level of respect than that to which, of late, it is accustomed.

N. V. Pope,

April 9th, 1979

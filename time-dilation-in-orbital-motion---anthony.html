TIME DILATION IN ORBITAL MOTION AND COSMIC TIME Time Dilation in Orbital Motion We have provided a detailed explanation as to why, according to our Normal Realist approach to relativity, there is a time dilation effect associated with linear motion, just as there is in Special Relativity. We have provided an explicit formula which gives the passage of time as recorded by a relativity moving clock in uniform motion in terms of a fixed reference clock. Written in terms of standard notation, if τ denotes the time as registered on a relatively moving clock, X, and t denotes the same passage of time as registered on an observer’s clock, O, then τ = (1  v2/c2)1/2 t,

(TD)

where v is the constant speed of the clock X relative to O. Recall that in Newtonian theory, all free motion is linear so that observed orbital motion, which Newton saw as unnatural, is explained by the presence of an unseen in vacuo ‘force of gravity’, which distorts the assumed natural straight line paths into orbital trajectories. However, we maintain that uniform motion is never observed in practice. To re-iterate, according to POAMS (the Pope-Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis), in contrast to Newtonian theory, the natural path of any particle is the trajectory followed by the particle when it moves freely under the influence of nothing but its own angular momentum, angular momentum being (globally) conserved. This is without the need of supposing the existence of an unseen in vacuo ‘gravitational force’. We are therefore required to examine time-dilation in natural orbital motion and, in consequence, to revise (TD). Since, in POAMS, the trajectory of any freely moving particle is naturally orbital, such a particle moves non-uniformly, i.e., it has non-zero acceleration, so that its speed is generally not a constant. We have shown in [Ref 1] that the natural trajectory of a particle depends on the mass, M, of the body which it is orbiting. More generally, it depends on the mass of a system of bodies, taking the orbital angular momentum of the particle relative to the centre-of-mass of such a system. These

considerations indicate that, for natural orbital motion, (TD) has to be revised to provide a formula which depends on M and, in general, a variable speed v which depends on t. [Ref 1: Anthony D. Osborne and N. Vivian Pope, Light-Speed, Gravitation and Quantum Instantaneity (phi Philosophical Enterprises, Swansea, 2007) Chapter 6] In the hypothetical situation in which all influences are essentially removed, so that a particle moves in isolation, then at least on a local scale, its trajectory would follow part of a straight line so that it would move uniformly, and hence (TD) would apply. In this way, POAMS aligns itself with two of the basic premises of Einstein’s General Relativity [Ref 2]: the presence of matter causes the trajectories of freely moving particles to be non-uniform and orbital, and, at least on a local scale, (TD) applies. The second premise is essentially, Einstein’s Principle of equivalence. Both POAMS and General Relativity remove the need for assuming there to be an in vacuo ‘gravitational force’ to explain the natural trajectories of freely moving particles. Both POAMS and General Relativity maintain that the reason we feel a ‘gravitational force’ on the Earth’s surface is because we are prevented from following our natural path due to the presence of that surface. Hence, POAMS is more aligned to General Relativity than it is to Newtonian theory, although it retains some aspects of the latter. [Ref 2: R. D’Inverno, Introducing Einstein’s Relativity, (Clarendon, Oxford, 1992)] Because time is dilated in POAMS, it only makes sense to talk about events, which are specified by points in space and a specific time relative to a chosen observer. The set of all such events constitute space-time. Since the paths of freely moving particles are curved in POAMS, the geometry of this associated space-time must be non-Euclidean, unlike the Euclidean space envisaged by Newton, together with his absolute time. Hence, in POAMS, the presence of matter changes the geometry of space-time. This is also essentially the third fundamental premise of General Relativity. However, there are fundamental differences between POAMS and General Relativity. The latter provides a mathematical model in which the ‘flat’ space-time geometry of Special Relativity (in which freely moving particles move in straight lines) is ‘curved’ due to the presence of matter. In contrast, POAMS, with its Normal Realist foundations, begins with the postulate that all free motion is orbital and angular momentum is conserved, based firmly on observations. There are

also other fundamental differences: notably, the space-time manifold of General Relativity is continuous, whereas in POAMS, space-time is discrete at the quantum level. We now turn to one particular situation: the simplest case of one particle, P, orbiting a massive body, B, treated as an isolated system, that is, no external effects or interactions are taken into account. This is the only case we shall explicitly consider. It provides a simple model of a planet orbiting the Sun to a good degree of approximation and is known as the ‘two-body problem’. In this simple case, neither the body B nor the particle P are spinning. In this case, according to Newtonian theory, P’s trajectory is determined by Newton’s laws of motion and his inverse square law relating to his presumed in vacuo ‘gravitational force’ of attraction. These laws determine that the orbital angular momentum of P is conserved, so in this case is constant, and that if the (assumed unnatural) orbit of P is closed, then the trajectory of P is an ellipse [Ref 1][Ref3]. In this way, Newton was able to explain why the planets move in elliptical orbits around our Sun, as observed by Kepler. [Ref 3: S. W. McCuskey, An Introduction to Advanced Dynamics (Addison-Wesley, Reading, Massachusetts, 1959) Chapters 1 and 3] In complete contrast to Newtonian theory, POAMS begins with the premise that angular momentum is holistically conserved, so that in the case of the simple two-body problem, the orbital angular momentum of P relative to B is constant. In the case where the orbit of P around B is closed, the constancy of P’s orbital angular momentum dictates that P moves in an elliptical orbit exactly as predicted by Newtonian theory [Ref 4]. Again, in contrast to Newtonian theory, the orbit of P is the orbit that P naturally follows when not subjected to any external forces or constraints, rather than an unnatural non-linear orbit caused by an invisible in vacuo ‘gravitational’ force. [Ref 4: Anthony D. Osborne and N. Vivian Pope, Light-Speed, Gravitation and Quantum Instantaneity (phi Philosophical Enterprises, Swansea, 2007) pp. 105-117] We turn now to replacement for (TD), in relation to the orbital trajectory of the freely moving particle. Let us first of all consider the case of the particle P ‘freely moving’ in a circular orbit around the body B, of mass M, in the case of the ‘two-body’ problem. We shall only consider the case in which B is static (non-rotating etc.) and spherically symmetric. In this

case, it can be shown that the proper time, τ, recorded by a clock carried with P, is given by [Ref 5] τ = (1  3GM/(rc2))1/2 t. (TD) [Ref 5: Anthony D. Osborne and N. Vivian Pope, Light-Speed, Gravitation and Quantum Instantaneity (phi Philosophical Enterprises, Swansea, 2007) pp. 161-172] In this formula, G is a constant, the Newtonian gravitational constant, r is the radius of P’s orbit and t is the time as recorded by an ‘external observer’ far distant from the orbit of P, which in POAMS is called deep space time (DST) [Ref 6]. This revised formula provides the basis for calculations which predict that clocks in the Global Positioning Satellites in orbit around Earth should run faster relative to Earth clocks. The amount predicted agrees very closely with what is observed [Ref 7]. The time-dilation formula (TD) is the same as predicted by General Relativity, except that in Einstein’s theory, r no longer measures exactly the radius of the orbit [Ref 8]. [Ref 6: N. Vivian Pope, The Eye of the Beholder: The Role of the Observer in Modern Physics (phi Philosophical Enterprises, Swansea, 2004) p. 32] [Ref 7: C. O. Alley and T. Van Flandern, Briefing Document: Absolute GPS to better than one meter, available at http://metaresearch.org/solar %20system/gps/absolute-gps-1meter.asp] [Ref 8: J. Foster and J. D. Nightingale, A Short Course in General Relativity (Longman, New York, 1979) pp. 109-112] Hence, POAMS, as does General Relativity, predicts that time is not only dilated by speed but also in the free orbital motion relative to a massive body. The analysis in [Ref 4] shows that even if P is relatively stationary in the vicinity of B, there is a time-dilation effect given by τ = (1  2GM/(rc2))1/2 t. The mass of the Earth is not great enough to have a significant effect on the passage of time. In particular, a clock on the surface of the Earth will run more slowly than a clock which is not subjected to Earth’s influence. Specifically, the clock on Earth will loose about 10 -9 seconds in an hour [Ref 9]. However, much more massive bodies will have a significant effect on even stationary clocks.

[Ref 9: J.J. O’Connor and E. F. Robinson, A history of time: 20th century time (Internet article, www-history.mcs.st-and.ac.uk)] More generally, now let us consider the case in which the particle P moves in a non-circular orbit about the body B which is symmetrical in one space dimension. Again, B is assumed to be spherically symmetric and static. Since P’s orbit is not a circle, the radial distance, r, of P from B depends on the angle, θ, which the radial axis of P makes with some fixed axis any at point. In this general case, it can be shown that the proper time, τ, recorded on P’s clock relative to DST, t, is given by c2dτ2 = c2F(r)dt2  (1/F(r))dr2  r2dθ2

(TD)

where F(r) = 1 – 2MG/rc2 [Ref 10]. This is the same formula derived in General Relativity, being the metric for the ‘equatorial plane’ of Schwarzschild space-time [Ref 11]. As in General Relativity, the formula (TD) can be thought of as determining the space-time geometry surrounding a spherically symmetric static body. Again, as in General Relativity, it can be shown that this space-time geometry determines the fact that a ‘freely moving’ particle P following an almost closed orbit about the body B follows an elliptical orbit, the perihelion (the point of closest approach of P to B) of which rotates very slowly [Ref 12]. [Ref 10: Anthony D. Osborne and N. Vivian Pope, Light-Speed, Gravitation and Quantum Instantaneity (phi Philosophical Enterprises, Swansea, 2007) pp. 173-177] [Ref 11 R. D’Inverno, Introducing Einstein’s Relativity, (Clarendon, Oxford, 1992)] Chapters 14 and 15] [Ref 12: Anthony D. Osborne and N. Vivian Pope, Light-Speed, Gravitation and Quantum Instantaneity (phi Philosophical Enterprises, Swansea, 2007) pp. 180-183.] It can be shown that from the equations of motion for ‘freely moving’ particles associated with (TD), the orbital angular momentum of P relative to B is conserved, as dictated in POAMS. However, Newtonian theory cannot produce such an orbit since it is no longer derivable from an inverse square law involving a ‘gravitational force’ [Ref 10]. In this way, the POAMS equations of motion are more general than Newton’s and are able to derive at least one space-time produced by General Relativity.

Stellar Collapse and ‘Black Holes’ We turn now to the end point in the evolution of stars and in particular stellar collapse, which, according to certain theories, opens up the possibility of time travel. Theory and observation tell us that certain types of stars implode at the end of their life and shrink in size, to a ‘white dwarf’ state for example, whilst explode to form a supernova for example. Some theories predict that stars of a certain critical mass continue to shrink to a critical radius. In this case, General Relativity predicts that such a star will continue to collapse to form a ‘space-time singularity’. On the face of it, this is region in which the fabric of spacetime is literally torn apart by the enormous ‘gravitational’ effects caused by the collapsing star. In General Relativity, with its interpretation of light travelling as ‘photons’, it is possible for light to be bent due to gravitational effects [Ref 13]. In the scenario of a star collapsing to a point just described, the implication is that the ‘gravitational’ effects are so enormous that light inside the region determined by the critical radius is so bent that it cannot ‘escape’ to the outside region. The physical consequence of this is that the collapsed star is invisible to any external observer and hence the term ‘black hole’ was introduced to describe this final stage of stellar collapse [Ref 14]. [Ref 13: Anthony D. Osborne and N. Vivian Pope, Light-Speed, Gravitation and Quantum Instantaneity (phi Philosophical Enterprises, Swansea, 2007) pp. 183-191.] [Ref 14: J. Foster and J. D. Nightingale, A Short Course in General Relativity (Longman, New York, 1979) pp. 123-126.] We now consider one particular case which is relevant to the timedilation formula (TD) above: that of a collapsing, spherically symmetric, non-rotating, electrically neutral star of mass M. According to Birkhoff’s Theorem in General Relativity, the external space-time geometry of such a star is described by Schwarzschild space-time, the ‘equatorial plane’ of which is given by (TD) [Ref 15]. It is theoretically possible for such a star, if it has a certain critical mass, to shrink in size to its Schwarzschild radius, r = 2MG/c2 [Ref 16]. Having reached this critical radius, it is possible in General Relativity for the star to shrink to a radius of less than 2MG/c2, so that the signs of dt2 and dr2 are interchanged in (TD). In this case, mathematics tells us that r becomes a time-like variable. Since no

physical effect can halt the monotonic increase of such a variable, the implication in General Relativity is that once a star has collapsed through its Schwarzschild radius, it must continue to collapse to form a ‘spacetime’ singularity at r = 0. According to (TD), this is a point at which the time is infinitely dilated. [Ref 15: G. D. Birkhoff, Relativity and Modern Physics (Harvard University Press, Cambridge, 1923).] [Ref 16: C. W. Misner, K. S. Thorne and J. A. Wheeler, Gravitation (Freeman, San Francisco, 1973) Chapter 24, pp. 618-635. ] In the General Relativistic scenario of a ‘black hole’ just described, a freely moving space-traveller, according to his own clock, which records proper time, can reach the Schwarzschild radius surrounding the ‘black hole’ in finite time and pass through. Once through this region, the traveller suffers the same fate as the collapsed star and can never return. He will be torn apart by the enormous ‘gravitational’ effects long before he reaches the ‘space-time singularity’ at r = 0. On the other hand, according to the clock of an external observer who remains at a safe distance, the space-traveller can never actually reach the Schwarzschild radius, since from (TD), time is infinitely dilated there. The conclusion here is that the existence of a ‘black hole’ in the above sense can never be verified directly by means of any physical experiment. Once an observer passes through the Schwarzschild radius to verify the existence of the ‘space-time singularity’, he can never return to communicate his results! In this way, the ‘space-time singularity’ at r = 0 is surrounded by what is called an event horizon at the Schwarzschild radius. Before we continue, it needs to be stressed that what say concerning ‘black holes’ depends very much on how such phenomena are defined. The original meaning of the term ‘black hole’, first coined by Laplace in the eighteenth century [Ref 17] was simply a phenomenon for which light cannot escape its ‘gravitational effects’, so is effectively invisible. We shall take the term to also imply the existence of a ‘space-time singularity’ since, in our view, it is the implied existence of such a singularity which is in conflict with common sense. More formally, we take a ‘black hole’ to mean the region contained within an event horizon. [Ref 17: S. W. Hawking and G. F. R. Ellis, The Large Scale Structure of Space-Time (Cambridge University Press, 1973) Appendix A, pp. 365368.]

Although a ‘black hole’ as a theoretical consequence of mathematical theory is an interesting object to study, and indeed provokes excitement, a ‘black hole’ as a real physically meaningful object has no place in our Normal Realist philosophy, since it is a completely unempirical description of the endpoint of stellar evolution. Indeed, in the case of a collapsing non-rotating star, the existence of the ‘black hole’ predicted by General Relativity can never be verified by observation. In effect, the event horizon ‘covers’ the singular core from any possible observation. The only observable facets of such an object would be the effects it may have on its surroundings, and these effects may have other possible explanations. Schwarzschild space-time is derived in General Relativity as a specific solution of the Einstein Field Equations. It provides a good model of our solar system in that it predicts physical effects which agree with empirical and observational evidence. It must be remembered however, that the same model is produced by POAMS [Ref 10]. Although this model predicts all the observable phenomena within our solar system, and so is a good model, there is no reason at all for supposing that it will still accurately predict what may happen at the end point of stellar evolution. As explained in [Ref 10], the POAMS derivation of what is essentially the Schwarzschild metric, (TD), depends ultimately on the time-dilation formula for circular motion, (TD) , so that r is greater than 3MG/c2 in (TD). Remember that in POAMS, (TD) amounts to the general timedilation formula along the path followed by any freely moving material particle in the otherwise empty space surrounding a spherically symmetric static body. Hence, in contrast to General Relativity, in which (TD) is simply a solution to the Field Equations with no restriction on the value of r except that it cannot be negative, in POAMS, there is a built-in restriction on the values of r in (TD). In other words, in POAMS, there is no reason to suppose that the Schwarzschild radius is reached in stellar collapse, let alone the predicted ‘space-time singularity’ at r = 0. So far, we have only reported on what General Relativity predicts in the case of the collapse of a spherically symmetric, electrically neutral, nonrotating star. For a rotating star, there is a much more complicated solution of the Einstein Field equations, known as Kerr space-time [Ref 18]. In this scenario when the star collapses to form a ‘black hole’, rather than the enclosed ‘space-time singularity’ being formed as a point, there is a ring singularity of a particular radius enclosed within two event horizons. It is now generally accepted in General Relativity that any non8

rotating star possible of collapse, collapses to form a ‘black hole’ which is spherically symmetric and so described by the Schwarzschild solution. It has also been shown that, according to General Relativity, any rotating star which collapses to form a ‘black hole’ will eventually settle down to a stationary state described by the Kerr solution. Moreover, the size and shape of this ‘black hole’ only depends on its mass and rate of rotation and not the nature of the body that it had collapsed from: this claim is famously paraphrased as, ‘a back hole has no hair’ [Ref 19]. [Ref 18: R. D’Inverno, Introducing Einstein’s Relativity, (Clarendon, Oxford, 1992) Chapter 19.] [Ref 19: S. W. Hawking, The Illustrated A Brief History of Time (Bantam Press, London, 1996) Chapter 6.] There are other solutions of the Einstein Field Equations which describe space-times relating to other scenarios. For example, the RessnerNordstrøm solution describes the case of a collapsing electrically charged star which produces a ‘charged black hole’ [Ref 20]. [Ref 20: R. D’Inverno, Introducing Einstein’s Relativity, (Clarendon, Oxford, 1992) Chapter 18.] At the present time, POAMS has not produced time-dilation formulae for rotating or electrically charged bodies, for example. However, it is not necessary for us to examine every possible case in order to provide the Normal Realist arguments against the physical existence of ‘black holes’. The Normal Realist philosophy is based firmly on observation and, as we have said above, the very nature of ‘black hole’ prevents its existence from being confirmed by empirical verification. Communication of observation to an ‘external’ observer is prevented by the presence of the event horizon. So when scientists claim there is experimental evidence for the existence of a ‘black hole’ they really mean that external effects which could have been caused by the presence of a ‘black hole’ have been observed. So from the Normal Realist point of view, we claim that ‘black holes’ do not exist since there is absolutely no empirical evidence whatsoever for their existence. It must be remembered that these are only theoretical predications of General Relativity and there are many alternative theories which do not predict the presence of ‘space-time singularities’ [Ref 21].

[Ref 21: Anthony D. Osborne and N. Vivian Pope, Light-Speed, Gravitation and Quantum Instantaneity (phi Philosophical Enterprises, Swansea, 2007) p. 198] The ‘Holy Grail’ for the believers in the existence of ‘black holes’ has always been the binary star system, Cygnus X-1. This system consists of a star together with an invisible companion. If one star of such a binary system were a ‘black hole’, then it would accrete matter from its visible companion. Theory predicts that this would form a gaseous disc around the ‘black hole’, which would become hot enough to produce intense bursts of X-rays. Such bursts of X-rays were first detected coming from Cygnus X-1 in 1971[Ref 22]. However, this so-called evidence has always been controversial [Ref 23] and, besides, there are other possible explanations of the data which do not involve a ‘black hole’ [Ref 24]. The ‘dark’ companion is certainly small and very dense and could be an object with properties similar to those of a ‘black hole’. In fact there is no qualitative difference between the Cygnus X-1 system and a binary system for which the ‘dark’ object is identified as a neutron star, a collapsed star of very small radius and very large density [Ref 25]. In POAMS, we have formulated an argument to explain the phenomenon in Cygnus X-1 based on angular momentum considerations [Ref 26]. There is other claimed evidence of the existence of ‘black holes’, for example, data emanating from the radio galaxy M87. But again, this so-called evidence can be disputed [Ref 21]. [Ref 22: Thorne, K. S.: ‘The Search for Black Holes’, Scientific American, 231 (6) (1974), pp. 32-43.] [Ref 23: Rees, M. J.: ‘Observational Effects of Black Holes’, in Shariv, G. and Rosen, J. (eds.): General Relativity and Gravitation (Halsted, New York, 1975).] [Ref 24: Bahcall, J. N., Dyson, F. J., Katz, J. I. and Paczynski, B., Astrophys. J. Lett. 189 (1974) L 17.] [Ref 25: Rowlands, P.: A Revolution Too Far: The Establishment of General Relativity (PD Publications, Liverpool, 1994) pp. 197-199.] [Ref 26: Anthony D. Osborne and N. Vivian Pope, Light-Speed, Gravitation and Quantum Instantaneity (phi Philosophical Enterprises, Swansea, 2007) pp. 199-202.]

Many physicists believe that the predicted existence of ‘space-time’ singularities in General Relativity is not evidence for the existence of real ‘cosmic plugholes’ but rather an indication of the fact that General Relativity is not valid at a small enough scale; for example, at the end point of stellar collapse. In other words, General relativity is an incomplete theory in some sense. We would agree with this sentiment. In POAMS, we have argued that (TD) may not be valid for r less that 3MG/c2. Also, in POAMS, space-time is ultimately discrete on a small enough scale. This goes some way to addressing the widely held view that General Relativity and Quantum Mechanics must somehow be combined in order to produce a better model of observed physical phenomena. Many physicists argue that a quantised version of General Relativity would perhaps prevent space-time singularities from occurring [Ref 19][Ref 27]. Moreover, if Quantum Mechanical effects are taken into account, Hawking claims that any ‘black hole’ should create and emit particles in the ‘empty’ space just outside the event horizon, which contains quantum fluctuations [Ref 19]. The ‘black hole’ loses energy through this process and eventually effectively evaporates away! [Ref 27: Isham C., Penrose, R. and Sciama, D. (eds.), Quantum Gravity (Clarendon, Oxford, 1975).] To conclude our discussion of ‘black holes’, it is important to note that there is another problem which remains unsolved. Despite many attempts, to date no solution of the Einstein Field Equations has been found which provides a mathematical model for the interior of a collapsing star. Without this information, it is not realistically possible to speculate about the eventual fate of such a star. ‘Wormholes’ in Space-time and Time Travel We have shown that according to our Normal Realist approach to uniform motion, time travel into the future is at least in theory possible, according to the time-dilation formula. This presents no logical or moral difficulty. But what about the possibility of time travel in the more general setting of POAMS and General Relativity? As stated earlier, in POAMS, as in General Relativity, the space-time geometry is non-Euclidean since the path of a freely moving particle is curved. This non-standard geometry led to the mathematical possibility in General Relativity of the existence of ‘wormholes’ in space-time, which connect one part of space-time to another. The possibility occurs essentially since General Relativity maintains that space-time becomes so

warped due to the presence of matter that distinct regions may be connected. This is analogous to a plane sheet of paper being bent so that its top and bottom touch. This idea was first put forward by Einstein and Rosen in 1935 and ‘wormholes’ were originally termed ‘Einstein-Rosen bridges’ [Ref 28]. The idea is that these ‘tears’ in the fabric of space-time occur due to enormous ‘gravitational effects’, so can only occur in the neighbourhood of a ‘space-time singularity’. Since these ‘wormholes’ connect one distant event to another, which of course, involve time as well as space, their theoretical existence opens up the possibility of time travel, both into the future and the past! Such an idea has formed the basis of many good stories for science fiction writers and film makers alike! [Ref 28: S. W. Hawking, The Illustrated A Brief History of Time (Bantam Press, London, 1996) Chapter 10] Certainly, General Relativity allows for the possibility of the creation of a ‘wormhole’ in gravitational collapse when ‘space-time singularities’ are formed. In the case of the Schwarzschild model for a non-rotating ‘black hole’, it is not possible for a space-traveller to avoid the ‘space-time singularity’ once through the event horizon – so even if the ‘wormhole’ was there, the traveller would not be able to pass through. However, in the case of the Kerr model for a rotating ‘black hole’ [Ref 18] and the Ressner-Nordstrøm model for a charged ‘black hole’ [Ref 20] it is theoretically possible for a traveller to avoid the space-time singularity and to pass through a ‘wormhole’, thereby travelling in time as well as space. We have already discussed the problems associated with time travel into the past. It is important to note, however, that even if it were possible to pass through a ‘wormhole’ connected to another event in space and time, this does not necessarily imply that closed time-like curves are possible, whereby a traveller could end up at the same point in space but in the past. For example, the possible physical laws which enable a traveller to pass through a ‘wormhole’ may be such that that they always restrict the traveller to arrive at an event a sufficient distance away, so that he cannot interact with his earlier self. Some relativists have even gone so far as to suggest that thee are a multiplicity of space-times sprouting from ‘wormholes’, so that a traveller can never end up in his own past! On the other hand, General Relativity allows for the existence of closed time-like curves, so it is not incompatible with the ‘block view’ of time [Ref 29]. [Ref 29: Barry Dainton, Time and Space (Acumen, Chesham, 2001) pp. 314-319.]

Now let us consider the possibility of time travel through ‘wormholes in space-time’ from our Normal Realist point of view. Mathematical models are clearly worthy of study in their own right and have many interesting properties. The problem comes when some of these properties are entertained as physical facts. It should come as no surprise to learn that there is no place for ‘wormholes’ in POAMS, since there is no empirical evidence for there existence whatsoever. In our view, even in General Relativity, they are simply an interesting mathematical possibility of particular space-times which are solutions of the Einstein Field Equations. To interpret such a mathematical extrapolation as a physical fact is rather far-fetched to say the least. However, even this mathematical possibility does not occur in POAMS. We have previously stated there are no ‘space-time singularities’ in POAMS since the various time-dilation formulae are not valid on a small enough scale: space-time is ultimately discrete so that other considerations have to be taken into account. Even in widely supported theories, such as Quantum Gravity, the existence of ‘space-time singularities’ remains dubious. Without such ‘singularities’, there can be no ‘wormholes’. In General Relativity, the mathematical possibility of ‘wormholes’ occurs because of an extrapolation of the space-time geometry which forms the basis of the mathematical model. In contrast, in POAMS, there are no such extrapolations since POAMS relies on a model based on observation, with time-dilation playing a fundamental role: there is no physical reality assigned to a ‘curved space-time manifold’ as such. The Nature of Space and Time on the Cosmological Scale We turn now to the consideration of space and time on the very large scale – the cosmological scale. We have shown how time is dilated both in uniform motion and natural orbital motion. This leads to questions regarding the nature of time in the cosmos. But we begin with a study of the nature of space on the very large scale. In order to do this, we need to make clear the meaning of the word ‘universe’, at least from our point of view. From the time of the Eleatic cosmologists, the word ‘universe’ has been taken to mean ‘everything there is’ in what they conceived as ‘an everlasting and unchanging (i.e., overall-conserved) unity, or whole’. This meaning still persists today. Following on from this Eleatic basis, we shall take the ‘universe’ to mean the ‘all-encompassing whole’. This is what Ludwig Wittgenstein referred to as its ‘proper’ meaning. Hence, the

universe consists of all the planets, life-forms, stars, interstellar debris etc. etc. The Nature of Space Recall that before the time of Copernicus, the space in which we live was thought to have a natural boundary. This idea essentially came from Ptolemy’s model. This natural boundary was removed when Earth was no longer regarded as the centre of the universe. From that time, space was thought to be infinite and everlasting. This view was re-enforced by Newton’s absolute space: infinite three-dimensional Euclidean space. Newton initially considered the universe as being a finite amount of matter situated in finite space. However, according to his universal law of gravitational attraction, in this scenario, eventually all the matter would come together to form a single ‘clump’. Hence, he rejected this idea and instead, viewed the universe as an infinite amount of matter in infinite space. By the early part of the twentieth century, other ideas concerning the nature of the universe had emerged. For example, it was proposed that space could be finite with an eventual boundary or it could be finite with no boundary - this is analogous to travelling around the Earth and finding no natural boundary, although the surface of the Earth is finite. The latter idea was originally supported by General Relativity. Einstein showed that the average density of matter in Newton’s universe would have to be zero, so that according to Einstein, such a universe ‘ought to be a finite island in the infinite ocean of space’ [Ref 30]. The General Relativistic postulate that the universe had no boundary was supported by the predictions General Relativity made in relation to the bending of light signals by gravitational masses [Ref 30]. [Ref 30: James Coleman, Relativity for the Layman (Pelican, 1959).] From our Normal Realist point of view, it makes no sense to attempt to create suitable models of the whole universe since we do not have a God’s-eye-view. We cannot speculate as to whether the universe is finite or infinite since this cannot be ascertained directly by local observation of any sort. On the other hand, it certainly makes no sense to postulate the universe has a boundary since if it did it would have to be embedded in something else. This is illogical as far as linguistic analysis goes since the universe should encompass everything that there is.

The above arguments used against the Newtonian model of the universe and for the finite but unbounded universe formulated originally in General Relativity were based on the belief that the universe is static, that is, unchanging over time. Certainly a finite amount of matter would eventually come together in a static Newtonian universe. However, if the matter content is receding over time, then this need not happen. In 1929, a discovery was made by Edwin Hubble and his co-worker Vesto Slipher which lead to the belief by the majority of physicists that the ‘universe is expanding’ over time. This negated the original General Relativistic arguments for a finite and unbounded universe. It should be noted before we examine this claim that from the POAMS point of view, a finite amount of matter may not collapse in on itself since in POAMS, there is no in vacuo gravitational force of attraction as such – particles move in their natural orbits due to angular momentum considerations alone. What Hubble and Slipher discovered was that the light-spectra of the farthest galaxies are always shifted towards the red. Hubble found that this redshift in the light’s wavelength is proportional to the estimated distance of any galaxy to a good degree of approximation. Now it is a well-know observed fact that when a light source is receding from an observer, its light-spectrum is shifted towards the red, whereas when it is approaching the observer, its spectrum is shifted towards the blue. This is a consequence of the time-dilation formula, (TD), and is known as the (optical) Doppler Effect [Ref 31][Ref 32]. The natural interpretation of the majority of physicists was that Hubble’s redshift was due to a recession of the galaxies from us and from one another at an appreciable speed. The implication was that the ‘universe is expanding’ at the present time. [Ref 31: D. F. Lawden, Elements of Special Relativity (Wiley, 1985) pp. 32-34.] [Ref 32: Anthony D. Osborne and N. Vivian Pope, Light-Speed, Gravitation and Quantum Instantaneity (phi Philosophical Enterprises, Swansea, 2007) pp. 49-50. ] However, it must be appreciated that the observed redshift in the lightspectra emanating from galaxies do not logically imply that those galaxies must be receding. The fact that all speeds of recession cause redshifts does not imply that in observing redshifts we are observing speeds of recession. This point was appreciated by Hubble if not by his followers [Ref 33].

[Ref 33: As Mayall notes in the entry in the 1961 Encyclopaedia Britannica, Volume 16, p.187Kd, “Hubble preferred the noncommittal term redshif’ and referred to his …results as the ‘law of redshifts’”.] Many scientists argue that since, in their interpretation, the universe is expanding at the present time, it has always been expanding at the same rate. The argument is that there is no reason for supposing otherwise. Thus, extrapolating backwards into the past implies that some fifteen billion years ago, the whole universe of matter, space and everything that there is, exploded into being from an initial ‘space-time singularity’, this event being popularly known as the ‘Big Bang’. In General Relativity, there are space-times which are solutions of the Einstein Field Equations which purport to model the entire universe. These cosmological solutions lead to the Friedmann models, which predict an expanding universe originating from an initial ‘space-time singularity’ [Ref 34]. More generally, an initial ‘space-time singularity’ is predicted always to occur under reasonable physical assumptions in any cosmological model derived in General Relativity [Ref 35]. [Ref 34: J. Foster and J. D. Nightingale, A Short Course in General Relativity (Longman, New York, 1979) pp. 149-157.] [Ref 35: R. D’Inverno, Introducing Einstein’s Relativity, (Clarendon, Oxford, 1992) p. 313] From our Normal Realist viewpoint, there can be no explanation of the Hubble redshift that is more absurd than the esoteric ‘Big Bang’ theory, to which there is no logical, philosophical or commonsense meaning. Once again, in General Relativity this prediction follows from an extrapolation of the Field Equations by many orders of magnitude beyond the range for which its validity has been established. We have to be extremely dubious about a mathematical prediction based on such an extrapolation, and in no sense can anyone claim that this actually represents physical reality. However, incredibly, there have been models produced that claim to give a true picture of the universe microseconds and moments after the ‘Big Bang’. In 1948 George Gamow first proposed models for the universe, George Gamow in 1948 first proposed models for the universe, 10-43 seconds, 10-34 seconds, 10-10 seconds, 1 second, and 3 minutes after the ‘Big Bang’ [Ref 36]! It is these ‘hot universe’ models that predict the existence of a 3oK microwave background radiation at the present time, being the remnants of the ‘cosmic fireball’. However, these

models do not satisfactorily explain why this radiation is observed to be nearly the same in all directions. [Ref 36: S. W. Hawking, The Illustrated A Brief History of Time (Bantam Press, London, 1996) Chapter 8.] A large number of physicists argue that the existence of the isotropic microwave background is observational evidence which supports the ‘Big Bang’ theory. This radiation was first discovered by Penzias and Wilson in 1965 [Ref 37]. However, although the presence of this radiation is consistent with an initial explosion, it certainly does not imply that there was one. Like all observations, it is a matter of speculative interpretation. There are certainly other plausible explanations for the presence of the background radiation which do not involve a ‘Big Bang’ [Ref 38][Ref 39]. Since the ‘Big Bang’ can never be directly observable, we would argue, especially from our adopted phenomenalist perspective, that it makes no sense to discuss it as though it were a physical phenomenon. [Ref 37: Penzias, A. A. and Wilson, R. It has to be realised that this premise is based on many different assumptions, any one of which may be false. Let us first examine the claim that the Hubble redshift implies that the galaxies are receding, so that the ‘universe is expanding’. Certainly the simplest and most obvious interpretation of the Hubble redshift is the generally adopted one of a Doppler Effect due to galactic recession. However, as we have said, there is no logical reason why the redshift needs to be interpreted in this way. Admittedly, most of the alternatives to this interpretation to date have failed. However, another possible interpretation from the POAMS point of view is that the redshift is the effect of relativistic time-dilation due to increasing relative speeds with distance. Like the Doppler Effect, timedilation shifts the frequencies in a light-spectrum towards the red.

However, unlike the Doppler shift, time-dilation applies not just to receding motion but to all motions in general. But what explanation could there be for our claim that there is a timedilation effect on the frequencies of light-spectra from galaxies due to their increasing relative speeds? For an angular momentum system of normally distributed stars and galaxies, the common ‘centre of motion’ is not central but may be indefinitely distributed. In that case, the further away a body may get from one local centre of motion, the nearer it may approach some other body. It follows from consideration of conservation of angular momentum that the nearer a body is to the major centre-ofmass of a system, the greater is its orbital speed. Hence, when the major concentration of mass is spread all around a given point in the system, the speeds of bodies relative to that point increase the further away from that point they get. In a sense, the further away a body is from any chosen point of reference, such as a terrestrial observatory, the ‘nearer’ it gets to the greatest (ubiquitous) accumulation of cosmic mass. On this basis, an alternative explanation of the Hubble redshift provided by POAMS is that the so-called ‘recessional speeds’ of the galaxies are due to the fact that they are getting ‘nearer’ to the greatest accumulation of cosmic mass [Ref 40]. [Ref 40: Anthony D. Osborne and N. Vivian Pope, Light-Speed, Gravitation and Quantum Instantaneity (phi Philosophical Enterprises, Swansea, 2007) pp. 212-217.] Suppose now that the above suggested interpretation of the Hubble redshift is incorrect and the galaxies are actually receding. From a linguistic point of view, there are difficulties in interpreting this to mean that the ‘universe is expanding’. Since we have defined the ‘universe’ to be ‘all that there is’, against what may we presume to record its expansion? From our Normal Realist standpoint, it only makes sense to talk about the implied recession of the observable galaxies. Any extrapolation concerning the geometry of the universe is merely speculation. Hence, in this context, instead of referring to ‘universal expansion’ which shall refer to ‘galactic recession’. Assuming that the galaxies are receding, this implies that the magnitude of the orbital angular momentum of the galaxies is increasing. This presents POAMS with a possible problem, since in POAMS, angular momentum is holistically conserved. This would mean that the angular momentum is accordingly redistributed elsewhere in the unobservable regions of the universe, which, from a phenomenalist perspective, is difficult to explain.

One possible solution of this problem is provided by the steady state model, first proposed by Bondi and Gold [Ref 41] and independently by Hoyle [Ref 42]. In this model, it is supposed that the galaxies are receding, but that more matter is continuously created in order to maintain the constant density of the observable galactic collection. Hence, although the galaxies may be continually receding, there was no initial ‘space-time singularity’. However, the predictions of this theory do not appear to agree with empirical evidence although the results are not yet conclusive. [Ref 41: Bondi, H. and Gold, T.: ‘The steady-state theory of the expanding universe’, Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society 108 (1948), pp. 252-270. ] [Ref 42: Hoyle, F.: ‘A new model for the expanding universe’, Month. Notices Royal Astro. Soc. 108 (1948), pp. 372-382. ] Even if the galaxies are receding at the present time, there is no reason to suppose that they were receding at the same rate in the past or even receding at all. The huge extrapolation back into the past to a ‘space-time singularity’ is generally based on a very simple model which uses a relatively simple equation known as the Friedmann equation. This equation can be derived under the assumption that any finite number of observable galaxies is homogeneous, i.e., has uniform density and isotropic, i.e., the number of stars per unit solid angle is the same in all directions. The Friedmann equation implies that the galactic collection expanded from a single point in the past and that the galaxies continue to recede from one another. We have shown in an earlier work [Ref 43] how POAMS provides a simple derivation of the Friedmann equation, which also occurs as a consequence of the Robertson-Walker metric in General Relativity [Ref 44]. Newtonian theory provides the same equation but the POAMS derivation is based on considerations of angular momentum alone. [Ref 43: Anthony D. Osborne and N. Vivian Pope, Light-Speed, Gravitation and Quantum Instantaneity (phi Philosophical Enterprises, Swansea, 2007) pp. 206-209] [Ref 44: J. Foster and J. D. Nightingale, A Short Course in General Relativity (Longman, New York, 1979) pp. 149-157.] However, there is no reason to suppose that the simple Friedmann equation does provide a reliable model for structures as complex as the

observable galaxies, particularly in the case of the situation in the past. Many simplifying features are introduced in the derivation of this equation, both in POAMS and General Relativity. In particular, the galactic collection may not be homogeneous and isotropic. In fact, more recent observational evidence suggests that the galactic collection is not homogeneous but fractal [Ref 45][Ref 46]. [Ref 45: Graneau, N and Graneau, P.: ‘The Evidence and Consequences of Newtonian Instantaneous Forces’, in Pope N. V., Osborne, A. D. and Winfield, A. F. T. This discrepancy leads to the problem of the so-called ‘missing matter’ [Ref 47]. Some physicists have postulated the existence of hypothetical ‘dark matter’ to avoid any possible contradiction between the observed and predicted densities. It is suggested that this matter might take the form of an almost ethereal intergalactic gas, but searches for its existence so far have proved unsuccessful [Ref 48]. [Ref 47: J. Foster and J. D. However, it is a consequence of the space-time singularity theorems of Hawking and Penrose that any General Relativistic cosmological model satisfying reasonable physical assumptions will contain an initial ‘space20

The prediction of a ‘singularity’ implies that the theory is not valid at that point. In other words, it is a mathematical prediction of the limitation of the theory which implies that other considerations have to be taken into account. This is far removed from inferring that the initial ‘singularity’ has any sort of physical existence, a postulate which can never be empirically verified. Indeed, Hawking has now changed his mind about the existence of an initial ‘singularity’ and has argued that such a ‘singularity’ disappears when quantum effects are taken into account [Ref 50]. Also, there are many theoretical alternatives to General Relativity which do not support the presence of an initial ‘space-time singularity’. Some of these theories generalise or amend the Field Equations to erase any possible singularities. [Ref 49: S. H. Hawking and G. F. R. Hawking, The Illustrated A Brief History of Time (Bantam Press, London, 1996) Chapter 8.] Time on a Cosmological Scale We have seen that it is not only the time as a conventional label that is relative to any observer, but also the duration of any process. Hence, at first sight, it does not appear to make sense to talk about the age of the universe since there is no absolute time in the sense of Newton. For example, the information that we receive from the furthest star cluster visible from the Hubble space telescope, is several thousand million years old. However, it is possible to introduce a commonly measured time on the cosmological scale. From a mathematical point of view, the universe essentially consists of all possible events in space-time. Let us imagine each star equipped with its own internal clock, which, of course, records the star’s proper time. Remember that this proper time is the same for all observers. There is no reason to suppose that a period of proper time differs from star to star, i.e., the rate of proper time varies from region to region in the universe. This is particularly so if the universe is homogenous and isotropic, but even if it is not, this is a reasonable supposition to make. For any given proper time registered by a chosen star, consider all the events which are proper time-instantaneous with this time, in the way prescribed by our Normal realist approach to relativity earlier. We can

clearly describe all such events as occurring at the same time, t. This construction provides commonly measured time for the universe, usually referred to as cosmic time. Note however, in this approach, there is no implication of the classical God’s-eye-view of the universe, which is the case for the usual justification for a cosmic time scale. In the latter case, a coordinate system consisting of a time coordinate, t, and three spatial coordinates is introduced to describe any event in the universe in such a way that the spatial coordinates are constant with respect to any star or galaxy. Then the time t reduces to the proper time registered by any particular star, which, by above, is assumed to be invariant [Ref 51]. As far as we are concerned, cosmic time does not represent an allencompassing ‘God’s time’ as is implied in General Relativity. [Ref 51: J. Foster and J. D. Nightingale, A Short Course in General Relativity (Longman, New York, 1979) p. 150.] It follows then that despite the variations of duration among all the different observers in the universe, there is an underlying cosmic time, representing a non-relative, datum time of ‘the whole’. But if all the observational times of the various possible observers throughout the universe differ, then where, in cosmic time, is the ‘present moment’? The answer is that when a particular event such as a star exploding occurs, it is the proper time of that event which records the present moment. The relative time of that same event is then the time of that event as actually seen by an observer and recorded by his clock, which is, of course, different for different observers. The moment at which a star explodes for example, is the same moment both for the star and observer, but different relative time labels will be attached to this same moment due to timedilation caused by distance, orbital motion and so on. In other words, the cosmic present moment is the proper time instantaneous cross-section of the whole collection of events whose relative times are different. In is within the context of cosmic time that we are able to consider the question of the age of the universe. Certainly before the twentieth century, it had always been assumed that the universe was unchanging in cosmic time: it had either existed for all time in this state, or else had been created at some finite time in the past in this state. A universe being ‘created’ at some finite time in the past suggests the existence of a ‘creator’, in other words divine intervention. This was a problem for some Greek philosopher, including Aristotle, who believed that ‘the world’ and the human race had existed and would exist for ever.

The question of whether the universe had a beginning in time has been considered by philosophers through the ages. In the eighteenth century, Kant argued that there was no logical reason for supposing the universe had a beginning rather than not. He essentially argued that there is no logical difference in having an infinite period of time before any event, in the case of the universe having no beginning, and having an infinite period of time before ‘the creation’ of the universe if it did have a beginning. However, it must be remembered that the universe contains all events so that if the universe did have a beginning, time began then, since it is a property of the universe itself. This fact negates Kant’s argument [Ref 52]. [Ref 52: S. W. Hawking, The Illustrated A Brief History of Time (Bantam Press, London, 1996) Chapter 1.] With Hubble’s discovery of the redshift in the light-spectra from galaxies and the assumed implication that the galaxies are receding, came the possibility that the universe was expanding and so evolving over time. This is independent of whether or not there was an initial ‘space-time singularity’. We have already mentioned the problem of making this claim from a linguistic point of view. Now nothing in General Relativity is obviously incompatible with the static block conception of time, but the concept of universe expanding in cosmic time is compatible with a dynamic model of time, since this suggests a truly universal ‘tide of becoming’[Ref 53]. However, due to the linguistic problems stated earlier, from our point of view, a static block view of time appears more appropriate. [Ref 53: B. Dainton, Time and Space (Acumen, 2001) pp. 314-320.] As we have discussed earlier, one of the possible implications of an expanding universe is that the whole universe began with the ‘Big Bang’ from an initial ‘space-time singularity’. On this premise, knowing the present rate of expansion, it is possible to extrapolate backwards in cosmic time to the ‘Big Bang’ to determine the finite age of the universe. One of the problems associated with the universe beginning with the ‘Big Bang’ is the consideration of cause and effect. What caused this event to take place? General Relativity has no answer to this question since in this theory the universe began with the initial singularity and the theory is not valid before it. A possible answer of course, is that the ‘Big Bang’ is the creation itself caused by divine intervention. In fact, in 1951 the Catholic Church officially pronounced the ‘Big Bang’ model to be in accordance with the bible. Some people have argued that the physical laws which

hold in the universe must have been originally decreed by a creator, since there had to be precisely the right physically conditions in the ‘Big Bang’ for the creation of a universe capable of sustaining intelligent life, which, in turn, is capable of interpreting those laws. However, a model for the beginnings of the universe first proposed by Linde in 1983 indicates that wide variations in the initial conditions for the ‘Big Bang’ could still have produced the universe in its present form [Ref 50]. Since the advent of Quantum Mechanics, many highly respected theorists, including Hawking and Penrose, have argued that General Relativity in itself is not sufficient to describe the early stages of the universe, as indicated by the prediction of an ‘initial space-time singularity’. Rather, Quantum Gravity, which also takes quantum mechanical effects into consideration, has to be employed [Ref 50]. Hawking has proposed a new measure of time, known as imaginary time. This is a mathematical construct with space-like qualities. Using this idea, Hawking has argued that the universe may have had a beginning but has no boundary in imaginary time, and so no moment of creation: spacetime is finite but with no boundary [Ref 54]. [Ref 54: S. W. Hawking and R. Penrose, The Nature of Space and Time, (Princeton, New Jersey, 1996).] There have been several other attempts to unify Quantum Theory and General Relativity. For example, earlier in the twentieth century Milne developed a complicated theory of cosmology, which attempted to unify the two theories. Milne’s theory included a non-constant value for the gravitational constant, G, over cosmic time. The prediction of Milne’s cosmology was a static universe with an infinite past age which acted as a precursor to the steady-state theory. As in the case of Hawking’s Quantum Gravity, Milne’s theory led him to create two distinct time scales [Ref 9]. Once again, from our phenomenalist perspective, it makes no sense to speculate whether the universe began at some time in the past and has evolved to its present state after a finite period of cosmic time, or has no beginning in cosmic time. We can never ascertain which alternative is correct by empirical evidence and there is no advantage in speculating about such things. On the other hand, from our point of view, it is illogical to contemplate the possibility of time travel into the past as far as cosmic time is concerned. This is because it appears to be undeniable that whatever happens can never be un-happened. For instance, it is difficult – one might say impossible – to think if Earth, with all its natural and

human history were wiped out, say, by the Sun turning into a nova, all those events that happened on Earth were somehow cancelled, so that they never took place. If this premise is correct, then whatever that a human observer was and what he may have done in his life will, in a sense, forever remain. One obvious sense in which this may be conceived is that in which those events are items in the memories of surviving witnesses to those events, or some recorded history, in libraries or as fossils in rocks and so on. However, in our example of the Sun becoming a nova, all those records would certainly be annihilated, yet it is difficult to think that even this would un-happen those events in themselves, that it would somehow make them never to have occurred. This idea leads to a fundamental question: in what sense can past events or happenings be thought of as existing? Histories may be true or false, whereas, according to our premise, events that have truly happened, in this ‘immortal’ sense, have to be absolutely true, regardless of whether we know it or not. In the same way, the universe evolves in cosmic time in some absolute sense, beyond our control. Such thoughts naturally lead to the question as to whether or not there is some information-transacting deity in control. Of course, whether or not such a deity exists remains an open question. But at least, our information-based, Normal Realist approach to physics provides no valid grounds for assuming that scientific reason makes atheism obligatory. What it does is to cancel the traditional ‘God of the Scientists’, with its presumed absolutistic and ubiquitous overview of the universe as a determinate physical whole. Any ‘deity’ there might be has to exist relative to each and every observer individually, since there is no presumed ‘God’s-eye-view’ of everywhere all at once in the Normal Realist approach, as in the standard presumption of classical physics. In this scenario, there is no ‘God’s absolute time’ in the sense of Newton as such, but a cosmic time, which we have described previously. One thing is certain. In view of those wars between religious groups which now seem to threat our very existence on this planet, any ‘God’ (Allah, Jupiter, Great Sprit, Krishna, Jehovah or whatever) conceived in these terms has to be an overseer, not only of humanity but also of animals and plants, molecules and microbes, planets, stars and so on, right out beyond the most distant galaxies. This of course, includes personal experience without being confined to it or to any particular social group or sect on this extremely tiny and insignificant planet.

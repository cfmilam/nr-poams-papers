Collected Notes on Stellar Aberration Bradley’s aberration involves the motion of the earth relative to the heavens. Aberration shows that light is not propagated instantly. The apparent position of a star does not correspond to its true direction from earth. In determining the positions of stars we have to correct for aberration, arising from the fact that our actual judgement of the direction of the ray involves a combination of the earth’s motion and the motion of the light. The most elementary explanation can be given in terms of the old corpuscular theory of light, which was the theory of aberration accepted at the time aberration was discovered. We see the body not where it is now but where it was when the light left it. … we apply a correction to the time of observation antedating it by the light-time. (ASE). Since the velocity of light is known with great accuracy, the observed value of the constant of aberration determines the earth’s orbital velocity. The astronomical observations prove that the ray as it reaches the telescope is travelling in the same direction as when it started from the star.

Think of two ships in a gun battle at sea, The ships, we’ll say, are on parallel courses, a distance s apart. Ship A fires a broadside at ship B. If ship B is hit, whilst travelling in the water at speed v. then (ignoring wind resistance, etc.,) it will seem that the shot hit him from a direction somewhat forward of his sailing direction, at an angle determined by the resultant of the two components: the relative (parallel) speed between the two ships and the speed of the shot.

This deviation from the normal trajectory due to relative motion corresponds to what is called aberration. Now let us take a second scenario, in which the attacking ship A is stationary in the water and that their relative speed v is the same as previously.

Again, the shot from A will seem to B to come from the same angle as before. In fact, it doesn’t matter how the ships are travelling with respect to the sea, it is the relative motion between the two ships that matters, which gives the same result no matter which ship is sailing and which is stationary.

But now let us consider a different scenario in which A fires a blank at B. In this case, if A is, again stationary in the water – and in the air which, we shall assume is still over the calm sea – then B, travelling at the speed v will hear the shot coming from A in a direction forward of his, B’s, sailing direction, the same as in the case of a shell (many shells, we may recall, can travel at the speed of sound).

That is to say, the direction from which B hears the sound will be the angle of the resultant of his speed v and the speed of sound in air. That is, the direction of the sound is aberrated to the same extent as that of the shell. Now, however, let us suppose that it is B that is stationary in the water and that it is A who is travelling at the speed v.

This time there is a difference. For no matter what the relative speed of the two ships may be, with B remaining still in the water – and in the air – he will hear the sound of the shot coming from its true direction, that

By the same token, when an astronomer measures the change in apparent directions of stars that Bradley called aberration, due the earth’s annual motion around the sun, it seemed reasonable to assume that this was due to the light travelling in an ether, analogously to the way sound travels in air. Observing this aberration, it was assumed that the earth was moving in that ether. In other words, aberration was not simply due to the relative motion between the earth and the stars. It had to be due to motion relative to an all-pervading ether. That, at any rate, was the easy – not to say, facile – explanation. In fact, things were far more complicated. For instance, the Michelson Morley experiment was generally regarded as having disposed of the ‘ether’ as a medium for the conveyance of light. Arthur S. Eddington wrote: All observable effects of aberration are due to relative velocity, or change of velocity. On absolute velocity it is silent; and there is reason to believe that all other experiments are silent also – a conclusion which has led to the principle of relativity. [1]

Astronomical; observation provides no means of determining a uniform motion through the ether; this will be apparent from what we said as to the aberration from the motion of the solar system through space But it provides a means of determining differences of velocity, such as the differences in the earth’s orbital motion at different times of the year. The corrected direction joins the position of the earth at the instant of observation to the position of the object some time before. A question often raised is that of whether we ought not to apply a correction on account of the aberration due to the motion of the whole solar system through space towards a point in Hercules. No correction is required provided that the observation relates to the state of things when the light left the star; the aberration rather helpfully ‘puts back the sun’ to the earlier date which must in any case pertain to the star. [This account of aberration (by Eddington) implies a universal ‘present position’ of all astronomical bodies, underlying the aberrated or ‘seen’ positions. If, as Relativity asserts, the ‘speed of light’ is the same in all observer-frames, then this seems to imply (in Relativity, at any rate) that all observers, no matter what their state of relative motion relative to one another, will be able to pinpoint just where the object was at a certain cosmical instant – that is, absolutely, in a universally objective reference-frame. But shouldn’t that independence of relative motion between the observers ‘queer the pitch’ for that?]

Well, let’s replace vorb with the earth’s equatorial velocity vspin. We now know that there is no way of measuring vspin with respect to a signal coming from space directly ahead of or behind the motion of an equatorial receiver. It is only when the source lies above, below or to one side or the other of the moving observer’s line of sight that there can be any difference between c and √[c2 – vspin.2].

In the case, therefore, of a signal coming from anywhere other than in the special case of straight ahead or straight behind, vspin will go alternately from negative to positive throughout each daily cycle. (This won’t affect the velocity √[c2 – vspin.2], of course, due to the uniformly positive result of squaring of the observational velocity-term vspin.)

That is to say, the signal’s ‘speed’ relative to the receiver goes from a maximum of vspin = 464.74 m/s as the signal comes from straight overhead (in the way Bradley measured for the orbital velocity), to a minimum of zero as the receiver moves away from the source (through one quarter cycle) and then from a minimum of zero (at the three-quarters cycle) to minus vspin as the receiver moves back towards the source.

In other words, √[c2 – vspin.2] goes from 0.99998663 c to minus that amount in one full cycle. In between, of course, there may be any number of variations, depending on the geographical location of the receiver. Only in the rarest of instances will the observational parameter vspin just happen to be absolutely zero.

There are, then, all sorts of ‘variations in c’ for a meticulous observer situated at the receiver to measure. In none of these measurements is the composite vector √[ c2 – vspin.2] ever greater than c, though in many cases, it may be less. However, even at its largest, the relativistic parameter vspin is so small as to appear almost Newtonian.

For instance, the difference between a relativistic and a Newtonian vspin for the earth’s rotation is approx. $1.2 \times 10^{-12}$, or 0.0000000000012 m/s. In that case, the GPS people might well be excused for thinking that what they measure as the composite velocity is Newtonian not relativistic, with the consequence that √[c2 – vspin.2] might be extrapolated as the ‘faster-than-c’ velocity $\sqrt{c^2 + v^2} > c$ that is depicted in your diagram, Fig. 1.

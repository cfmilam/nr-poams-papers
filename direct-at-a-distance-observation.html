Direct At-a-Distance Observation

N. Vivian Pope — 2020

DIRECT AT-A-DISTANCE OBSERVATION N. V. Pope Take your mind back to the time before it was discovered that light has a speed in space. How would you have viewed the heavens? Would you have viewed the stars, in the way we do now, as objects that existed a long time ago? Obviously not, because you would have no reason to think in that way. You would have said that the star you are seeing is the star as it is right now, at whatever time it was by your observatory clock. But then, in 1676, the Danish astronomer, Olaus Römer discovered the constant relation, c, between astronomical distance and time which has since become known as the ‘speed of light in space’. This separates us from the distant stars and galaxies by times which may be aeons in some cases. Prior to this discovery, the standard commonsense view was that of phenomenalism. This was the view that unless we are looking through some medium, such as glass or water, the objects and events we observe are observed directly, regardless of distance. But, of course, that view became untenable as soon as news got around that we are separated from those distant objects and events by the time that their light takes to travel the distance. This was the first serious blow that phenomenalism received at the hand of modern science. Then along came Einstein, with his famous Theory of Relativity. According to that theory, nothing can travel at the ‘speed of light’ – except light itself, of course. This ‘Einstein separation’, as it came to be called, was the killer blow for phenomenalism as an approach to astronomy and physical science generally. Well, at least, so it seemed for a very long while. However, over the next century, phenomenalism began gradually to recover. The recovery began with three significant discoveries. First was the discovery of the quantum. This appeared in experiments conducted independently by Max Planck and Albert Einstein. These experiments revealed that the energy which is transacted between the object and the observing instrument in the form of light is quantised in amounts that are irreducible below a fundamental unit In other words, light-energy comes, not in continuous amounts but in discrete packets – more analogous to a hail of bullets than a flood of water. These units became known as quanta – or, as Einstein named them, photons. Next, in logical sequence, came a discovery described by the chemist, Gilbert Lewis, in 1927, that as a mathematical consequence of Einstein’s Theory of Relativity, despite light appearing to be something travelling in space, in it own time, or ‘proper’ time, a ‘photon’ contact between object and detector is immediate and instantaneous – in other words, it is there as soon as it starts out. This logically disposed of Einstein’s conception of the ‘photon’ as something ‘travelling in space’ and suggested more of a quantum ‘knock’ or, as Lewis presented it, a quantum touching in which the emitting and receiving atoms transact energy reciprocally in perfect accordance with Newton’s law of equal and opposite action/reaction. This restoration, at the quantum microphysical level, of the direct-observational connection, so crucial to phenomenalism, was slow to percolate through into a physics that for a whole century had been dominated by a science that was preoccupied with space-travelling light and photons.

Then, in support of Lewis’s quantum touching, there appeared. in 1987, a paper by N.V. Pope and A.D. Osborne which derived the equations of the Theory of Relativity in purely phenomenalist, direct-observation terms, without involving Einstein’s axiomatic conception of ‘light-velocity’. This was corroborated by Herman Bondi who, in 1956, had shown the non-necessity of ‘light-speed’ in Einstein’s equations. So instead, Römer’s constant distance-time relation c, which had been customarily interpreted as the ‘speed of light’ was re-interpreted, in the new relativity of Bondi and Pope-Osborne, simply as a constant, with the dimensions of a velocity but none of a velocity’s other essential characteristics (such as, for instance, conformity with the theorem of the addition and subtraction of velocities). One logical consequence of this quantisation of light-energy into discrete units is that at the quantum analytical level, all transfers of energy between atoms, classically conceived as certainties, become probabilities. That is to say, uncertainty, or randomness, becomes endemic in the nature of things at the quantum microphysical level. But probability is a definite statistical measure. In the late 1940s, an American telecommunications engineer, Claude Shannon, discovered a formula according to which the inverse of probability (that is, improbability) is a measure of information. In this way, ‘information’, previously seen as something altogether vague and abstract, had become a measure alongside other physical measures. Now add to that the consequence of Lewis’s ‘quantum touching’, namely, that the transaction of a quantum of energy between an object and an observer is, in effect, an event common to both. The logical knock-on effect of all these different stages in the quantisation of light-energy is that far from light being something that travels in space, as previously thought, light is pure information whose ultimate elements are the quanta of Planck and Einstein. This fully restores phenomenalism to its former vitality as the standard, commonsense approach to physical science. The phenomena, directly observed, analyse out into ultimate units of information analogous to the bits (binary digits) of computer technology. These quantum units have no distance or time interrelations in themselves. Distance and time on the phenomenal level are relativistic observer-projections out of information-statistical sequences and patterns of the quantum-phenomenal elements, in a manner somewhat analogous to the way in which a video viewer projects the distance and other physical dimensions of the scenario from the occurrences and arrangements of the pixels on the screen. Science, therefore, for the phenomenalist, is the method of getting phenomena, so far as possible, to ‘speak for themselves’ in empirical observation. The only use the phenomenalist has for theories is as an aid towards guessing the most efficient, most logical and conceptually economical interpretations of natural phenomena, at which point, theory as such disappears and is replaced by natural truth. For him, the pursuit of theories for their own sake – as has become prevalent in contemporary physics and cosmology – is regarded as

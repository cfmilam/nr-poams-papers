The Intellectual Problem of Understanding POAMS

N. Vivian Pope

Solving the EPR Conundrum of Quantum Instantaneity versus Relativistic Time-Delay

You thought that understanding Einstein's Theory of Relativity was a mental challenge. Well, try this.

Central to modern physics is a notorious and (as many see it, altogether scandalous) clash between two entirely different and seemingly antagonistic theoretical approaches to physics. These are Relativity and Quantum Theory. The one approach is that of the followers of Albert Einstein and the other is that of the followers of Niels Bohr. This continuing, unresolved clash is between relativistic time-delay and quantum instantaneity and is known as the 'EPR paradox'.2

In its simplest terms, this is the apparent contradiction between, on the one hand, Einstein's claim, on the basis of his famous Theory of Relativity, that no physical influence can travel faster than the speed of light and, on the other hand, the claim of the quantum theorists, after Bohr, that there are quantum interactions that have to be instantaneous regardless of distance.

The solution to this problem, which POAMS proffers, is altogether simple and logical but, as it transpires, extremely difficult to get one's mind around. (Good logic, it has been said, may be bad psychology.) This mental difficulty has been named 'the intellectual problem'. It is the same problem that has attended just about every revolutionary move that was ever made in the history of science, which is that of having to make progress against the psychological back-pressure generated by prior presumptions in favour of the more generally accepted and familiar ways of thinking. This force-of-habit backlash makes it difficult, in the process of coping with radically new ideas, of keeping one's train of thoughts on a strict logical track without being derailed by habitual assumptions and precepts.

What POAMS does is to demonstrate that in fact there is simply no problem, that a light-interaction between a distant atom in what we call the 'source' and another atom in our eye or observing instrument which we call the 'sink' is both instantaneous, as quantum theory directs, and time-delayed as Relativity requires. This raises the problem of how, mentally, to accommodate this apparently contradictory state of affairs. Not everyone, it seems, can grasp the solution. For some it remains permanently insoluble. For others, it takes a while for the conceptual 'Eureka!' to occur. They report that it is like suddenly seeing the point of a joke – when the penny drops, as it were.

In any event, the solution POAMS offers is very simple. There is nothing particularly technical, mathematical or scientific to it. It is, if only we will allow it, no more nor less than the application of a plain, unadulterated commonsense stripped of some bogus ideas and superstitions that have become established as popular precepts. The only thing that militates against this POAMS solution, therefore, is the habitual bias towards those bogus theoretical modes of thinking that have become customary and, in the uncritical popular mind, authoritative.

That simple logical solution is as follows. In Einstein's classical portrayal of nature, when we observe a distant event such as, for example, a sudden flare in the brightness of a star, what we are seeing, right now, is not that event but an after-image of it as it 'really' happened years, or perhaps millennia ago. That is to say, the image is assumed to be in our present and the 'event itself' in our past. POAMS challenges that traditional assumption.

In POAMS the reality of that event is not in our past but as it is happening right now at this present instant of observing it. In POAMS, what Einstein conceived as the 'time-delay' due to 'light travelling' is nothing of the sort. It is simply the observed difference, as measured simultaneously in the same observatory and by the same observer, between the readings of two sets of clocks, the one in the observatory and the other at the distance of the object as observed in the telescope.3

Now here is the bit that one's mind has to encompass. The measure of that difference is simply the clock-reading of the present moment as registered in our observatory, minus the observed distance $s$ (in our telescope) divided by the constant $c$. Note that there is no logical reason whatsoever to interpret that measured time-delay in distant clocks as due to Einstein's alleged 'speed of light in vacuo'. It is simply the difference in the on-the-spot observational readings of two different sets of clocks (different time-systems), one local and the other at a distance.4

The radical departure that POAMS makes, therefore, is to interpret $c$, here, not as the conventional 'speed of light in space' but simply as a constant ratio of conventional units of metres to seconds in our observational/informational projections of the distances of bodies (pace Herman Bondi5).

Note that any protestation of an inability to understand this POAMS solution is in no way tantamount to a refutation of the thesis, as many seem to assume. This clearly places the onus on those professing not to understand POAMS either to juggle the ideas in their minds until they 'click' or else study the theory's basic propositions (see following) and show some logical contradiction in the POAMS deduction. And if they cannot do either, then the problem plainly has to be one of their own, not of POAMS.

In short, the revolutionary view that POAMS offers is that whereas classical physics takes one of the two distance-separated events, putting one of them (the image-event) in our present and the other, the source-event, in our past, POAMS puts both events in our present. Thus, by removing the so-called 'Einstein separation' (i.e., light-separation) between ourselves as observers and events at a distance, POAMS solves the notorious 'EPR' conundrum of quantum instantaneity versus relativistic time-delay (or, in modern-physics-speak, the locality versus non-locality issue).

The only difficulty this presents (although undoubtedly not an inconsiderable one) is that of purging one's mind of the logically unnecessary notion of light as something travelling and settling for the entirely minimalist interpretation of the word 'light' as simply what we see. And, not the least part of this difficulty is that of, having purged the 'velocity' interpretation of $c$, of keeping it purged in all future discussions. In many of these discussions it is plain that all too often the 'velocity' interpretation of $c$ is ejected through the front door only to be let in again through the kitchen window.

In the publications of POAMS it can be seen that this truly 'Copernican', lateral-thinking alternative to Einstein's 'velocity' interpretation of the 'constant $c$' provides the point of departure for a whole new, commonsense-based philosophy of modern physics. This is not something to be 'dipped into' for useful ideas that may or may not be useful to modern physics. It is a whole new logical and philosophical departure, as the following list of propositions disclose.

The Basic Propositions of POAMS

Proposition 1: Quantum immediacy. When we look at a distant star, every quantum of the light by which we see it is a direct and instantaneous contact between an atom in the object and an atom in the observer's eye or observing instrument. This concurs with Einstein's Special Theory of Relativity, according to which the 'proper-time' of a quantum contact, regardless of distance, is zero. Let us call these observational elements quantum pixels.

Proposition 2: Space. The distance of an object is one of the dimensions that are projected, in observation, out of informational patterns of co-occurring quantum pixels in the observational field. That observational distance dimension is called range. The other two dimensions of observational projection are azimuth (or horizontal angular dimension) and elevation (angular dimension up-down).

Proposition 3: Time. Time is the observational measure of the sequences of these three-dimensional observational frames, analogous to the sequences of photographic frames in cinematography. The datum of observational time-measure is that recorded by a standard clock situated at or near the centre of the observational sphere described in Proposition 2.

Proposition 4: Motion. Motion is the change, over time (prop. 3), in the observed position of an object in the three-dimensional sphere of observation described in Proposition 2. The measure of that relative motion is speed, which is the distance moved by the object in that dimensional frame divided by the time taken as measured by the central standard clock (prop. 3).

Proposition 5: Space-time. First, it is a fact, first established by Olaus Römer, and confirmed by subsequent observers, that all observational distances are observational times in the constant ratio of conventional units, 300,000,000 metres to the second (metric units) or 186,000 miles to the second (imperial units). This constant ratio of units is signified in physics by $c$.6

Corollary 1: From Propositions 4 and 5 it follows that for every metre of distance that an object moves relatively to the observer, a time of $1 \text{ metre}/c$ (i.e., 3.3 nanoseconds) has to be added to the time of that motion as recorded by the observer's central clock.

Conclusion: As compared with the time measured by a clock at the observational centre (prop. 3), all clocks seen at a distance register times that are less than those recorded by the central clock – that is, by the interposition of the distance-time $s/c$.

Caveat 1: It is vital to remember, here and throughout, that all the time-readings of clocks, either local or at a distance, are readings seen simultaneously by the observer, the distant ones, as it were with his eye on the telescope, and the local one with his eye on the clock in his observatory.

Example of Application

Now let us apply these propositions and conclusions to a situation described by one of those attending our seminars. In his example, the time-reading of a flare-event on a distant star by that star's own time-standard – as he reads it in his telescope off the star's clock – is the time of his central clock as he sees it minus the star's distance-time $s/c$, which in his example is ten years. (This means that the distance $s$ of the star is $c$ times the ten years difference, which in this case is three parsecs or 60,000,000,000,000 miles.)

What, then, he asks, is the real time of that event? POAMS answers that it is the time registered by the standard clocks on that object when, on that object, the event actually occurs. And what is that time? It is the time that we read right now on that body's clocks – as read in our telescopes (see Caveat 1 and footnote 3).

Which, our inquisitor asks, is the real event; the one we are seeing right now or the one whose time-designation we read in our telescope? This is a bogus question; both are equally real. Indeed, they are one and the same real event (see prop. 1). It is simply that the observer sees, in the same instance, the one part of it at a distance and the other part close-to (see Caveat 1).

What, then, about the 'ten years delay'? In POAMS, that is no more nor less than the difference the observer reads between what he sees (in his telescope) as the star's own time-reading of that event and his own local or observatory reading of that same event. Both these events happen together, the one at the time one reads on one's observatory clock and the other at the time registered, in that same observational instant (e.g., in one's telescope), by the clock at the observational distance $s$, to which one retrospectively assigns the present time of the observatory minus $s/c$.

In short, the event as it occurs at that distance and as we see it right now are the same event. The two different clock-readings are just bits of the same phenomenon – the same reality – but with different time-designations in accordance with the observed distance of the one bit as distinct from the other bit.

Note that in this POAMS account of observational distance and time there has been no logical necessity whatsoever to talk about $c$ as the 'speed of light in space' or of the observed time-delay between clocks at a distance and local clocks as an effect of 'light having to traverse the distance at that speed'. It is simply a difference in observed time-readings, that's all.

Now, as if that solution were not mentally testing enough, then what about this? Let's say that it is the Copernican 'full Monty', the complete mental revolution. It has been suggested, by the way, that this should be accompanied by a 'health warning' for those of a nervous disposition. Let that warning now be taken as read.

In classical physics it was assumed that somehow, there is a 'universe' of self-sufficient space, like a kind of dimensional 'grid' consisting of three self-sufficient dimensions of extension, all enduring in a single overall 'universal' time, a time which is the same everywhere throughout. Into this dimensional receptacle there are somehow sprinkled bits of matter which move relatively to one another in those sorts of ways which Newton's and other's mechanistic theories describe. These mechanisms, it was assumed, are completely self-sufficient, having little or nothing to do with our perceptions of them.

This generates what is called the epistemological fallacy, which is the age-old conundrum as to how we can be certain of the existence of these objects – or even sensibly talk about them – other than on the basis of knowledge obtained in our perceiving them. So far as POAMS is concerned, there never was any logical or empirical justification for that assumption of the existence of these self-sufficient entities, far less as a basis for empirical science. All it ever was, in the final analysis, was pure theoretical fancy. There simply is no way – as if it needed saying – in which we can know of the existence of anything, its distance from ourselves and from other things, its mass, shape, physical composition and other qualities and characteristics, other than on the basis of some kind of direct or indirect, instrumental observation, either by ourselves or in communication with others in the observational community.

Here, then, is the 'Copernican full Monty'. As conventional modern physics describes it, on the ultimate microphysical level, atoms interact in little quantum bursts of energy called 'photons'. How they do this at a distance when, according to Einstein, the proper times of those photons are instantaneous (as they are for anything travelling at the 'speed of light') is a complete mystery. The POAMS paraphrase of that conventional statement is that on the ultimate microphenomenal level of observation there is no space and no time but only sporadic occurrences of little bursts of quantum energy called quanta. So far as POAMS is concerned, it is from informational patterns and sequences of these quantum events, occurring in observation like the pixel-events on a video-screen, that space, time and all the other defining dimensions and properties of the physical scenario exist.

To put it most succinctly, whereas the customary view of light is that it travels in space with time, in POAMS it is the space and the time that are in the light.7 Therein lies the unadulterated essence of true relativity and of the POAMS alternative. For many, of course, this mental inversion, no matter how logical it may be, is just too much to swallow. It has been proclaimed 'counter-intuitive', 'mind-bending', 'absurd' … and so on. But this, surely, is good news for POAMS, since just about every truly significant scientific advance that was ever made in science has been described in those very same terms. ■

Message to the NRIG (Normal Realist Interest Group)

Those of you who have been able to manage the sort of mental 'flip-over' that Penny Gower reported towards an understanding of this POAMS alternative might, perhaps, like to report on how it happened for you. Penny's testimony, as described at the last seminar, was that what did it for her was her reading of the beginnings of the two Physics Essays papers 'Instantaneous Relativistic Action-at-a-Distance' (Pope and Osborne, 1992) and 'Instantaneous Gravitational and Inertial Action-at-a-Distance' (Pope and Osborne, 1995). Perhaps those two pieces might help others also. (Both these papers can be accessed in the Seminal Publications section on the POAMS website www.poams.org.)

Responses to date: The difficulty of getting one's head around the difference in the clock times was solved for me by the realisation that it is the distance $s$ divided by $c$ that explains it, and that this has nothing to do with 'looking back in time'. (AMP)

1 POAMS: the acronym for the Pope-Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis, a collective of scientists whose work can be found at www.poams.org.

2 The EPR paradox gets its name from a paper published in 1935 by Einstein, Podolski and Rosen, Physical Review, Vol. 47, pp. 777–780.

3 How do we know 'the time of that star', as registered by that star's clocks? Well, of course, a star does not carry clocks which register its age. But if they did, then the age of the star they would register at that distance $s$ would be a time $s/c$ behind the time registered by the observer's clocks. The thing to note is that in POAMS, as in orthodox relativity, there is no single universal ageing-rate of all things everywhere. Things age at different rates. A mouse ages at a different rate from an elephant or a tortoise. But when a mouse dies at the end of its natural life, the fact that it has reached its equivalent of the human 'three score and ten' well before the natural demise of ourselves – or of the elephant or of the tortoise – does not mean that when it dies its demise takes place in our past – or in the past of the elephant or of the tortoise. The fact is that the ages of the mouse, the elephant, the tortoise and ourselves may differ, all in the same single act of observing them. The same applies to the star, whose age, as we register it, is less at its distance $s$ than it would be if the distance between it and ourselves were zero.

5 H. Bondi: 'Any attempt at measuring the velocity of light is … not an attempt at measuring the velocity of light but an attempt at ascertaining the length of the standard metre in Paris in terms of time-units.' Assumption and Myth in Physical Theory, Cambridge University Press (1965), p. 28.

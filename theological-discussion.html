Theological Discussion

N. Vivian Pope — 2005

The following is a discussion by e-mail that took place, intermittently, over a period of six years between Viv Pope and Penny Gower and to which a contribution was made by Alison Pope. It was not planned but evolved in a natural way into a lengthy exploration of religious belief and the implications of Normal Realism and POAMS for religious belief.

Any extraneous material has been edited out, but what remains is presented in exactly the form and order in which it was written. The first correspondence took place in 2005. 1. 3 June 2005 Dear Viv, ….... With regard to our discussion at Oxwich yesterday, it has stirred me up because I've often felt I haven't given enough serious thought to the problems which arise from the idea of a beneficent deity – bearing in mind all the pain and suffering in the world.

The fact is that I have always tended to feel that the suffering inflicted by humans on one another or on animals is an inevitable result of free will, and that without free will we would be little more than automata. (I am beginning to think more in terms of our being freely-communicating agents).

While Man's more selfish and cruel behaviour cannot be excused by this argument, it can be explained to some extent. In the context of Christian doctrine, it is explained in terms of 'fallenness', though I cannot subscribe to the doctrine of 'The Fall' without considerable reservation.

Also, with the example of such paradigmatic individuals as Christ, one sees that it is possible to face up to, and indeed overcome, suffering – though it is by no means easy for most of us. The Christian answer to our weakness in this respect is to put our faith and trust in God, but that, too, is something that does not come readily to the average person. (But having said that, am I betraying my personal leaning towards Pelagianism?)

Furthermore, if one has a sense of meaning and purpose beyond the merely mundane concerns of this world, it is perhaps a bit easier to put the human condition into perspective and acknowledge that our view of things is inevitably very limited. Yet again, as I know we've discussed before, it is a fact that one is more likely to learn and make progress in the face of hardship and challenge than if one has a relatively comfortable life.

I admit that the foregoing is somewhat simplistic, and in some respects, muddled, and does not begin to tackle the matter of illness and disease, (and as we touched on yesterday, predation), in all their forms. On one level, it could be said that the presence of illness and disease in Creation implies a less than perfect system and that it sits very uneasily with the notion of a perfect God. There are at least two possible rejoinders to that – first, that we still need to allow for our own limited perspective

which means that we can't presume to 'understand the mind of God' and secondly that it is not inconceivable that, in some way, a once perfect Creation has been corrupted by an evil influence, or indeed that there may be an embodiment of evil in the form of the Devil – a negative force which seeks to destroy all that is good. (That's a very anthropomorphic way of putting it, I confess.)

I appreciate that when one tries to take into account all the religious and cultural systems in the world which differ considerably from the one in which we have been brought up, the issue becomes even more complicated. At this point, I find myself attempting to distinguish between two approaches: one based on an intellectual stance and the other on an emotional one.

Intellectually we try to comprehend and analyse religious questions as we would problems in philosophy or science, but emotionally we consider religion as something which does, or does not, answer a deeply felt need for love and purpose in our lives.

There are dangers in both approaches. It seems to me that the danger in the first is that we can put too much reliance on our own admittedly meagre powers of thought and, at worst, take a coldly pragmatic view of the world, whilst starving ourselves of what comfort may be there for the taking; and the danger of the second is that we become prey to an over-sentimental or even superstitious outlook which can return us to a primeaeval 'dark age' of the mind.

Maybe the really important question is how to steer a course between the Scylla of Intellectualism and the Charybdis of Emotionalism. I know this is only scratching the surface, but maybe we could explore things further, particularly in the light of what Peter and Audrey have said.

I'm still struggling to work my own constantly evolving beliefs and feelings into the setting of an information-based world and will need some help!! Penny 2. 12 June 2005 Dear Penny. Thanks for your very interesting breakdown of our Oxwich discussion.

Re. Your point about Intellectualism and Emotionalism, my view on this is that the intellect is to emotion as the wheelhouse is to the propeller of a ship. In the wheelhouse are all the controlling instruments and guidance systems, whereas the propeller provides the power to put the captain's intentions into effect.

Both Intellect and Emotion are necessary but not sufficient in themselves. To be all 'propeller' and no 'helm' is as bad as all 'helm' and no 'propeller'. One is reminded here of Epictetus' saying: 'He who follows his passions without constraint of reason is the biggest slave of all.'

Given, then, that reason or 'intellect' is necessary, albeit not sufficient, I would say that all our beliefs should be constrained by reason, or as I see it, logic. My mantra, in that respect, is always to the effect that although logic in itself cannot tell us what The Truth is, it can certainly tell us what isn't. This makes truth the object of open-ended research, a process of continual logical and experimental elimination. I think that,

together with emotion and intellect, an innate sense of justice – of conscience or whatever – inheres in everyone, albeit only latently in some people, such as psychopaths, megalomaniac idealists, etc. (Pelagianism, undoubtedly!) But that, surely, is just as it should be.

If life is a test of our moral will – as how else should it be? - then it would scarcely be fitting for the celestial 'Examination Board' to allow some priveleged people, logicians, mathematicians, priests, etc., to filch the answers and distribute them among the examinees.

To begin with, I like your analogy of the wheelhouse and propeller of a ship. It avoids the more dualistic impression left by my analogy of steering a course between Scylla and Charybdis. The wheelhouse and propeller are already integrated into one system, whereas Scylla and Charybdis are two independent objects which are, and will remain, irreconcilable opposites.

Their only 'connection' in 'The Odyssey' is that they both appear to be there simply to trap the unwary passer-by. One cannot reasonably regard either the intellect or the emotions as being there simply to trap us if one believes in a rational and beneficent deity 'behind' the world.

Furthermore, I agree entirely that emotion without reason can enslave a person, and that reason without emotion can be dangerous, too. In this context, I am reminded of Portia's speech in Act IV of 'The Merchant of Venice' when she says: 'And earthly power doth then show likest God's When mercy seasons justice.

Therefore, Jew, Though justice be thy plea, consider this: That in the course of justice, none of us Should see salvation..... ' With regard to your point about life being a kind of examination, again I'm inclined to agree. It certainly feels like that very often.

However, I don't think reading the Bible, the Koran or the Bhagavadgita or any other 'holy' text can be seen as a form of cheating. To begin with, if one thinks of it like that the implication is that these texts

are, without doubt, the infallible Word of God. In that case, reading them could perhaps give one an unfair advantage in the examination. However, having said that, for me, one of the important points about an exam is that one doesn't normally go into an examination room without any prior information (that would seem a ridiculous thing to do if one had any ambition to pass the exam), but rather that, in the course of the exam, one is able to demonstrate the degree to which one has imbibed what one has learned from many sources and display an intelligent understanding of that knowledge.

Personally, I go to the Bible and other books of devotion, not because I believe them to be the infallible Word of God, but because I believe they do contain some important, valuable and uplifting ideas (and dare I say, in some instances, comforting ones too!).

I by no means claim the whole of the Bible is equally uplifting and inspiring (some of the Old Testament is brutal in the extreme, and there are contradictions between the Old and New Testaments), but there are gems there if one looks for them. Some of the Psalms I find particularly helpful and inspiring, and much contained in the Gospels is spiritually and morally challenging and sustaining.

When it comes to the Epistles, I suppose one is on more difficult ground because one is already meeting with specifically theological interpretation, which is bound to be viewed as contentious. My understanding of such books as the Bible is that they are human attempts to make sense of life through accounts of the thoughts, experiences and ideas of men and women down the ages.

Many of these people have been spiritually advanced, and frankly, I'm grateful for any insight I may be able to gain from them or their writings. That, of course, doesn't absolve me from the duty of using my own powers of thought, such as they may be.

I might add that, as with reading the ancient Greek and Roman classics, it can give me a strong sense of connection with people from the past, despite the differences in culture and so on. Now on to the matter of free will. Yes, it has to come into the picture, because without it there can be no morality, no moral progression.

But free will, as we all know, does mean we are free to do the bad as well as the good; free to make wrong judgements (albeit for the right reasons, very often) and perform wrong acts, whether wilfully or unintentionally. As I see it, one of the greatest barriers to leading the morally good life is the human tendency to put ourselves at the centre of things – to be self-centred, thereby showing a lack of true concern for others.

Obviously, not all our acts are motivated solely by self-interest (it would be unnecessarily perverse to suggest that), but so often we are hampered by too great a concern for our own benefit and welfare, and in one sense, a lack of imagination with regard to the feelings and needs of others.

Here, the Christian ideal of losing oneself in order to find oneself has relevance because it is a fact that one can feel more truly free and fulfilled in selfless service. I'm not suggesting for one minute, however, that it is easy to achieve the necessary frame of mind to do this. (There's much more to be said about this subject, but it will have to keep for another time.)

Next comes the issue that has been exercising my mind considerably of late, which is the challenge of carrying the concept of relativity into the field of religion and morality. Relativity, after all, stresses that each of us has his/her own experience of time and space and the world which can never be conveyed adequately to other people in speech, let alone shared by them in practice.

How does one fit a rational concept of morality into this picture; a concept which includes a communicating God and beings who have more free will than classical science has ever allowed for, and who are able to make more difference in the world – positive or negative – than classical science could ever have envisaged?

Actually, the more I've thought about this, the more it has disturbed me. It has come home to me that, at one and the same time, we are both the weakest and the most powerful of creatures, the most ignorant and yet the most potentially knowledgeable.

Oh dear. I'm diving headlong into dualism again! Maybe you can come up with another apt analogy to save me! To summarise, the matter of faith in a God is something with which I have no real problem. But if there's something you have taught me over the past couple of years, it is that one cannot speak with any certainty about the nature of God, and attempting to do so just results in diminishing that which one is trying to describe.

Like you, I have a strong sense of the numinous but an increasing aversion to fixed doctrine and dogma because of its confining effect. I appreciate that the Church exists to keep at least the idea of God alive, but whilst I can accept the basic moral precepts contained in its teaching, I find things like the doctrine of the Trinity, and arguments about Transubstantiation and the 'filioque' clause increasingly pointless.

As is the case with classical physics, it seems to me that it's simply an indulgence in intellectual acrobatics about purely human ideas. At this point, I'd better call a halt, as I can see several more complex problems coming up over the horizon which had better be left for another occasion!

As I see it, this logos is the common factor that links all our relativities together in a manner that is conceivably the way of an ultimately rational, just and moral...well, call it 'deity' for want of a better word. This to me, is what provides the linking factor, being, in short, the logos, whatever that might be.

It seems we agree, at least, that sullying this inner sense of logos with doctrinaire teachings and stylised terminology militates against any true personal contact we may have with 'It'. Indeed, I would go so far as to say that any reading of the scriptures, of science, religion or whatever, should be based on a firm grounding in logic – the simple commonsense logic codified by Aristotle as rendered in those books on the subject which you and I studied, written by the likes of that excellent scholar we both admire, Susan Stebbing.

Your suggestion that POAMS and Normal Realism could be the life's work of a committed theologian tickles me.

People like your good self have perceived that the Normal Realist approach to modern science removes the traditional barrier between science and the humanities – which, of course, does not exclude religion. Yet when I started out on all this with my first attempt at writing on the subject from a pure science point of view, I was advised to send it to the local vicar for evaluation.

He said he would be glad to read and comment upon what one of his parishioners had written. Some short time later, the vicar reported back that the work was not scholarly enough to be taken seriously because it had not mentioned the Holy Trinity. So he advised me to leave out all the formulae and stuff like that and do a proper study of the Bible!

Now, as for this 'Holy Trinity' thing, I regard it as an intellectual over-elaboration of something that is perhaps altogether profound. Allowing that the natural rational principle of nature is logos, the question arises as to what might be the distinguishable facets, or subdivsions of that logos.

One of these, I guess, would be its Wholeness, or cosmical interrelatedness. Another might be its expression in man (in the Holy Scriptures, for instance, based on the historical account of the man called Christ). A third might be the workings of the logos in nature.

One wonders, then, whether this, or something like it, was what was meant originally by 'The Father, The Son and the Holy Ghost'? But however useful these appellations might have been at the time, it is a language which is now surely inept when applied literally, especially nowadays, within our modern vernacular.

The trouble is that in rejecting this ancient language as naïve, or even ridiculous, we might well be throwing the baby out with the bath water, might now be doing more harm than good. So perhaps what is needed as the basis of a new 'life's work in theology', based on Normal Realism, may be a wholesale linguistic revision, or Aufklarung, along the lines pointed by those Linguistic philosophers whose works were the original inspiration leading to Normal Realism and POAMS.

To undertake that linguistic review would be a far more rational approach to modern theology than to make like a POAMS 'Billy Graham', and if any of my students were to go along that route, then that former, rational course is what I would advise. Allied to that sort of venture would be my insistence that whereas theological questions are usually aimed at finding out 'what God is', any theological thesis based on NR and POAMS wold be dedicated to not only what that entity is not but also to finding what, logically, it cannot be.

As a start then, in that direction, I would say that, whatever that entity is, It is not what so many people variously imagine It/Him/Her to be. A deity of those dimensions would be far too small to 'fit the bill'. All the words used to denote this numinous entity are, in the last analysis, just noises we are conditioned to make by whatever society it is in which we happen to be born and raised.

Yet people fight to the death, squandering not only their own lives but also those of others in their thousands, over what noises to make personifying whatever they think of as 'God', 'Jahweh', 'Allah', 'Deus', 'Theos', 'Duw', 'Dieu', 'Dios', 'Gott'... or whatever.

To assume that it is precisely the same entity that is identified by those different noises ignores the relativities there always are in language and the sometimes vast differences in thinking that go along with them. In view, then, of these noisy, conflicting and dangerous conceptual differences, my POAMS approach to theology would advocate a deep and reverent silence in the face of such an Object of Contemplation.

Even the appellation 'Logos' might be too constraining and prejudicial. As you have probably experienced yourself, the word 'logos' often comes with a prepackaged set of associations which spells mysticism in some people's minds. The theological problem, then, as I see it, is that of how to identify and name this Object of Discourse without instantly reducing it to a finite, social personal and subjective figment of imagination which, in its cosmic fullness, it cannot possibly be.

This means that it cannot be something confined within the Bible, the Koran or any other Book or reliquary held sacred by them. Too many people, in my view, make 'God' in their own image, the image of man. This, to me is ridiculous. I find the splendid portraits of Jesus and his mother that one sees, especially in the High Churches and Temples no more impressive than the juju dolls, dancing dolls and straw effigies in the mud huts of the jungle witchdoctors. Indeed, the more splendidly

these images and idols are made, the more ridiculous, to me, they seem. (I am well aware that in an earlier age, I would have been tortured and killed by the Establishment for saying this.) So much organised 'religion' is therefore, I would say, no more than idolatry, relics of pagan beliefs and mysticisms, being more of anthropological than theological significance.

On what, then, alternatively, might any imagined 'POAMS theology' be based? The only solid basis I can conceive is the rationality that one sees, not only in nature but also, if one searches enough for it, in one's own rational dealings with the world at large.

If we think of this latter, personal logos as an infra-logos, that is, the world as we perceive it, then what remains, and which we will surely never perceive far less in its totality - might be called the Ultra Logos. Anyone steadfastly denying the existence of that undisclosed remnant of rationality would surely be clinically insane.

Of course, there is one class of objects, namely crystals, which are able to grow by accretion and can both 'determine' their own shape and reproduce that shape. This reflection puts me in mind of the debate several years ago as to whether meteorites from Mars showed signs of containing fossilised bacteria. Normal Realism is what might be classified as a monorealism in which matter and mind, science and the humanities, practicality and spirituality – whatever that may be – are all seamlessly joined. But if Normal Realism is not a form of solipsism, then what happens to that reality when I am not observing it, when I fall asleep, become unconscious or, say, comatose – or even die?

Doesn't this, then smack of solipsism? No, because, quite obviously, that reality is still there relative to hosts of other observers and interagents. As for ourselves, while we are in that non-observing state, our autonomic processes still function, our blood still circulates and our genetic constitutions remain intact and our brains are still ticking-over, albeit in some ways at depths beyond our ken.

But what happens when one's brain is anaesthetised or, say, cryogenically cooled? Where are we then, in the real world and what can be our relation to it? In such a profoundly unconscious state one is no longer functioning, except as a purely physical object, like the aforementioned pebble, a chair or a table. And what if we never emerge from

that suspended animation, when we die and our bodies disintegrate? Is that the end of everything for us? Have we and the World parted company for ever, leaving it to others to bury us, with our existence in this real world only to be remembered by others – or not, as the case may be?

In a talk I gave to an adult group of philosophy students at Swansea University, a woman declared: “When I die, that will be it, kaput, kabluey, finis, end of story!” To this I found myself replying: “Well you hope so, Madam, you hope so.” Now what made me say that?

Does my scepticism really stretch so far that I can doubt even that? There was a highly loved and respected uncle of mine who was a Humanist. When he died, recently, at the age of ninety, his own prepared epitaph began with: 'When you read this I will be dead.

I won't have “passed on”, I won't have “gone before”. I will simply have died.' That was his well-thought-out philosophical view. Am I sceptical even of that Humanist view in the same way as I am sceptical of atheism? I once asked a Philosophy class how many of them were atheists?

Practically all of them held up their hands. 'What does “atheism” mean?' I asked them. 'It means,' said a spokesman, 'that we do not believe in God.' To this I replied: 'Can you, then, describe to me this “God” that you don't believe in?' This was followed by complete consternation.

In the end these people admitted that they were stumped, for, again, how can anyone sensibly know the non-existence of something of which they know nothing! So much, then, for Humanism and Atheism which, on this analysis I have decided are every bit as much belief-systems as outright religion.

What then, of this charactercomplex one knows as oneself – or 'soul' in the old parlance? Plato likened the body to clothes the soul wears. While, during our lives, from time to time our clothes wear out and are discarded, our body and our awareness of things persist throughout.

Does the soul, then, Plato wondered, continue on in that same way, naked, as it were, after our last garment is shed? There is some support for this Platonic view in modern science, according to which our bodies are renewed every seven years or so, with no component living cell we are born with surviving for more than about seven years.

This is in the same way that older generations of human beings die out and are replaced by younger ones, while the race as a whole persists. At the age of thirteen, I had a cycling accident in which I was profoundly unconscious for three days. Where was I then?

This incident also involved retrograde amnesia in which the memory of a whole sunny day prior to the accident was completely wiped out and irrecoverable. During that day I must have been as intensely aware of the world as any fit and healthy thirteen-year-old can be.

What and where was I on that very real day that had now gone into oblivion? How would it have been if I had never recovered but had ended up dead and buried? Would my whole life, vivid as it had been, be consigned to oblivion in that same way? In view of these things it is difficult, to say the least, for me to contemplate the eternal

survival, or salvation of the soul. Moreover, having seen the pains and agonies, and the attendant horrors that so many people – and animals - have to endure, my bias, I must confess is towards complete annihilation of my soul along with that of my body at death.

Once around is quite sufficient for me. Some people have ended-up in such depths of pain and misery that they must surely have wished they'd never been born. On the other hand, I recall that in the first few moments in waking from a heart-bypass operation I experienced something which I can only describe as complete and utter bliss.

This was a complete awareness of myself with no awareness whatsoever of my body and its usual discomforts. This lasted only for a few moments but for that few moments I felt literally disembodied. However, it was not the same sort of out-of-body experience Mary and so many others report, of floating above the scene, looking down on their body with the medical staff attending them, and seeking to re-unite with the body in trying to wake.

I have no reason whatever to doubt Mary's honesty in reporting that experience. She has always been honest to a fault hence, by extrapolation so, also, must be those who report similar experiences. However, I, personally, have had no such experiences.With others, including sometimes my wife and family, and sometimes with friends in the RAF, I have experimented with Ouija boards, hypnotism, also attempts at telepathic communication and teleportation.

However, by far the most part of my experiences in this direction have been negative. All my personal experiences of what might have been called 'ghosts' and the alleged 'paranormal' I have checked-out and rationally explained. Now I say 'for the most part' because there have been one or two incidents that make me sceptical of any final conclusion on that score.

One of these incidents was during my afore-mentioned National Service in the RAF. I was on duty as a ground-control DF (Direction-Finding) operator in a small hexagonal building called the Homer, situated on high ground in line with the main runway but remote from it, right out in the country.

This duty usually involved sitting alone, wearing headphones with which to listen-out for direction requests and emergencies from the pilots of aircraft amidst the ethereal hiss called 'white noise'. One night, a group of fellow National Service airmen asked me if they could hold a 'séance' in the Homer with an Ouija board and a glass.

I couldn't be involved in this because, of course, I had to remain vigilant at my post. However, in the long boring silences between signals on the late-night airwaves, even with my headphones on, I could hear what was going on in the room behind me.

Then one of the group members informed me that the glass had spelled-out, as he put it, a 'message for Taff'. It seemed to be from my very Welsh maternal grandfather, Tom Jones, and it read 'Finish killing yourself.' This turned out to be very apt because I was then running a weight-lifting club which, as it transpired much later, was aggravating the damage to my spine that had been caused by the above-mentioned thirteen-year old cycling accident.

Despite the unlikelihood of my grandfather referring to me as 'Taff', the kind of Welsh idiom conveyed in the message was entirely foreign to anyone in that group except me, which has given me pause for thought ever since. At least it has served as another caution against my being too

dogmatic in any rejection of soul-survival and things paranormal. Another incident of a similar sort was in our big house in Burton when, in the small hours I was wandering about my study on the top floor contemplating suicide. (I am clinically bi-polar.)

On the bookshelf I spotted a small pocket bible. As I took it down to riffle through the pages, a piece of paper fell out and fluttered to the floor. It turned out to be a piece of text in my grandmother Jones' very neat handwriting. It read: 'We can trust Him still.'

Whatever might be made of that? The plain fact is that it had a calming influence on me. Somewhat spooky, I thought. Then there was an incident in our bungalow in Bishopston, when Mary and I, with my sister, Ursula, my brother-in-law Roy and Mary's sister Claire, were experimenting with an Ouija board.

At one stage, Mary, sitting apart without her finger on the glass asked if the glass would spell out her maternal grandmother's Christian name. Now Claire, with her finger on the glass, might conceivably have known their grandmother's name, and might have subconsciously influenced the movement of the glass towards writing the name.

However, instead of spelling-out her grandmother's name, which Mary and Claire knew was Alice, the glass spelled out 'Maud'. This, it turned out, was their grandmother's second name, which was news to Claire – and was certainly not known to the others – including me.

For me there was no mystery concerning the movement of the glass. This, I discerned, was due to the natural action-tremor of the fingers on the glass, which, after a few moments was bound to predominate in one direction or another, overcoming the friction between the glass and the table.

Feeling that slight movement of the glass, the tendency of the partakers in the experiment was to follow that incipient movement in some random direction and continue according to the general anticipation of stopping at some letter or another on the board.

In other words, the glass served as a medium for averaging-out and co-ordinating the expectations of the sitters, so that the momentum given to the glass was in proportion to the amount of concurrence among those expectations. For me, there was nothing 'paranormal' about this.

It was a matter of just plain physics. Not so, however of the messages the glass sometimes spelledout. Some of these were quite definitely statistically significant, or improbable, impossible to explain in terms of the laws of physics. What, for instance were the chances of the glass spelling-out the name 'Maud' known to no-one in the experiment but the one whose fingers were nowhere near the glass?

To me that defies explanation, although that of sheer coincidence cannot be entirely ruled out. Now to the last and, for me, the most significant cautionary incident, which takes somewhat longer to describe. I am told that as part of my character I have a low tolerance for ambiguity. (As I see it, this tolerance of ambiguity is for politicians, not scientists and philosophers.)

This means that, as a researcher in those two latter disciplines I have a volatile aversion to dishonesty, lying, cheating, injustice and chicanery in general. Bearing this in mind, the second incident I am talking about was during my work as a well-paid telephone lineman, or trouble-shooter for the GPO engineering Department, covering Swansea and the beautiful Gower Peninsula. One

night or, rather at about two o'clock in the morning, I was on a call-out in the City – or Town, as it then was. It was a clear and calm night and at that time the streets, although well illuminated, were deserted. At a junction I pulled out from a minor road onto a main carriageway and was surprised to feel a slight bump on the left offside wing of my van.

The van was fitted with rubber wings and when I got out to see what had happened I saw a rather old and shabby motorbike had run into the left offside front of the vehicle. It was a rubber-to-rubber contact between the tyre of the motorbike and the wing of the van.

The rider and I joked and exchanged pleasantries, after which he and I, quite amicably, went our separate ways. A week later, I learned that this motorcyclist had reported the accident to my employers, claiming severe injuries and a total write-off of his machine.

This was due, I was told, to my negligence as a driver in what a witness described as 'dashing out' of the side-street and crashing into him'. I was then taken to court and convicted of 'driving without due care and attention', being fired accordingly.

It transpired, long after the event, that this motorcyclist had been advised by someone that in an accident with a 'Crown Vehicle', such as my Post Office Van, that Government Department would pay out handsomely. So it seems he had dropped his shabby old motorbike over the cliff of a quarry near his home and claimed, with the testimony of a paid witness, that the damage was caused by the 'crash' with my van.

Strange, then, that there had been no damage to the van. Strange, also, that there had been no sign of an ambulance nor anyone to witness the alleged 'crash'. The whole thing had been a complete fabrication on his part. The Union solicitor who was supposed to defend me in court didn't even speak to me before the hearing, let alone try to elicit the truth of the incident which should have been for him an open-and-shut case in my favour.

The case was over before I was aware and the blatant injustice of it left me gasping. It now appears that this guy had paid a well-known 'expert witness' to swear on oath that he had seen me 'dash out' from the side road, hit the motor cycle and drive away hastily.

The result was that my name was in the local newspaper as a 'hit-and-run' driver. No-one who knows me would think that was true. Nevertheless, there it was, in black and white to be read by all my colleague, friends and family. I was devastated and, to say the very least, angered by such an obvious false accusation from someone who had seemed so friendly.

Some weeks before this happened I was working on the lines in Gower. For a long time I had been severely stressed by the acrimony caused, in general, by the appearance in the local press of my letter from Einstein. This time I had been called 'on the carpet', by the Area Engineeer who told me that the article which the paper had entitled 'A Lineman's Lines from Einstein' was an embarrassment to the Department. This, he said, was because 'Readers would wonder how it could be that a

man who could correspond with Einstein was employed by the GPO in the capacity of a mere telephone lineman.' I should have consulted him, he said, before I allowed the article to appear in the press. I was simmering with a sense of injustice over this and the general acrimony that the article had created among some friends and family members, which tension had recently come to a head, and whilst trying to open a tin of sardines that Mary had packed for my lunch, in trying to open the tin, using a pliers (the key had snapped) the oil squirted in my eye.

At this, the tension within me erupted. I flung the tin so hard that it must surely have gone into orbit. I danced around kicking and punching things, and anyone who might have seen me at that moment would surely think I should be certified and locked up.

During this time my van was parked up a narrow lane by the sea. This was outside a small, very old and very picturesque grey stone church. The very sight of this ancient church had a strangely calming effect on me. Somehow, I'm not sure how, I found myself opening the Church gate and walking up the path between old lichened gravestones towards the church door.

I tried the latch on that heavy studded wooden door and, to my surprise, it swung open. There was no-one there, the church was empty. Inside, was a grey-green stillness, with sunlight filtering through the ancient windows as it shone through the new, transparent green leaves of the ancient trees on the landside of the church.

The only sound heard through the open door was from the susurration of the breeze-blown leaves and the pulsing wash of the surf, joined by the echoes of my footsteps reverberating between the bare stone walls. I sat down in one of the long wooden pews and became steeped in the surrounding stillness.

This lulled me into an almost comatose state somewhere between being asleep and awake. After a while, languishing in this peaceful state there gradually formed in my mind a resolve which, if I had expressed it in language, would be something like 'I am here, with such talents as I have, to search for philsosophical truth and reality and to carry it through to wherever it leads, regardless of whatever it takes and however long it takes'.

After a while in that comfortable, zombie-like state I got up, left the church and returned to my van. The difference in my mood between leaving the church and returning to the van was remarkable. The anger was now replaced with a complete calmness.

Some time after the report in the paper of my alleged 'hit and run' incident, I was informed that as a result of the accident, I was to be demoted from my job as a lineman. The job I enjoyed so much and which I was good at was to finish. I was told that my immediate superior had objected to my demotion on the grounds that I was 'the best lineman they had', to which the area engineer replied, 'Well, we can't afford him.'

I had clashed many times with this Area Engineer over issues largely unconnected with my work. For instance, on one occasion, I had been recommended by a surgeon

who was on call from the hospital for emergencies for what the doctor had thought was my dedication in untangling wires by climbing on his roof on a wet and windy night. For this I had been called on the carpet, again, to be told I was 'setting an unnatural precedent' for my kind of work.

The fact was that this medical consultant was the only one on call that night, and his two emergency telephones were dead due to the lines being blown into contact by the wind. I simply could not have lived with myself if it had happened that some patient had died because I had refused to climb that night, which was why I climbed on that roof to untangle the wires and restore service.

Other incidents like this had set me at odds with this man with whom I had clashed on many occasions, and this accident of mine reported – or, rather, misreported – in the press was evidently an opportunity for him to get rid of me. So I was now working as a low-paid 'jointer's mate', handing string and tape to the jointer down a damp and dingy cable-chamber.

After my free-ranging job as a lineman in the scenic beauty of Gower, this demotion was more than I could stand. In the meantime, I had continued my after-hours work on my philosophical ideas and presented my writings to two physicists at the university.

After much discussion they said I should 'come in from the cold' and that I should apply, on their recommendation, for a place to study at the Workers' Education College, Coleg Harlech. This was in the scenic setting near Snowdon in North Wales. Now this is where that 'other incident' comes in, which makes me sceptical of my disbelief in 'miracles'.

Following my demotion, Mary, calculating the finances involved, worked out that my pay as a jointer's mate was now on a par with the size of the grant I had been offered to attend Coleg Harlech. As a well-paid lineman I had not been able to accept this offer because it would have meant a large drop in income.

However, the situation had now changed, so I was free to follow the course of action offered me. With Mary's encouragement and on the recommendation from Swansea University, I was immediately accepted at the Coleg. From this there followed in short order, my matriculation at the college and, subsequently, my unconditional acceptance at the University of North Wales, Bangor to study Logic and the Philosophy of Science.

After graduating I became a lecturer in Burton-on-Trent College of Further Education, teaching these subjects to adult students. This was followed by an invitation to teach for the, then newly formed, Open University where I was employed as a Tutor, Counsellor and Study-Centre Co-ordinator at the Burton College.

Call this coincidence. But if so, then it was one hell of a coincidence – a whole cascade of coincidences, in fact, all leading from my being a 'jointer's mate', albeit through many trials and tribulations, to what I am now, in my eighties, an Honorary Professor of Relativistic Quantum Physics and member of the Scientific Advisory Board of the International Institute of Theoretical Physics and Mathematics in Prato, Italy.

More than that it has led to the founding of the natural philosophy of Normal Realism which has proved so successful in explaining many anomalies in physics. If

it hadn't been for that scurrilous bit of dishonesty on the part of the motorcyclist and the haste of the Area Engineer in demoting me, I would have undoubtedly remained a telephone lineman right up to my retirement and the usual 'Well done, thou faithful servant', to be immediately forgotten thereafter, of course, by my employers.

Now, regardless of what might be said of this train of 'coincidences', the fact remains that the desperate sublingual pact – or 'prayer' if it may be so called – that I made to whatever Powers there may be, in that little grey church by the sea, has since been answered – quite spectacularly, one might say.

Even the grievously unjust incident with the motorbike had its vital part to play in this seemingly miraculous sequence. It had provided the point of departure for a whole new way of life for myself and my family in a different environment entirely, a way which put my talents to use in precisely the way I might have 'prayed' for.

Now, recalling what I decided earlier, these 'Powers' had to be within the Realm of Introspection – that is, within myself, not floating about in the church nor anywhere else in public space. That is to say, they had to be from somewhere way down below, in the hidden depths of one's soul, so to speak.

Now my aim here is not to write my biography. That has been done by someone else in far more detail than would be relevant here. Rather, my aim is to juggle these random thoughts and somehow or other put them together to see if they form any kind of logically coherent picture.

So far, then, on the one hand there is the seemingly inescapable conclusion that there can be no such thing as the eternal soul, while on the other hand, there were these very strange incidents to consider. How do I get these two things logically together?

As a scientist and logician, I should plump for what seems the most logical conclusion that all talk of 'soul' and its cosmical survival, 'resurrection', 'salvation' or whatever it might be called, is just nonsense. This would cetainly be my preferred conclusion, that at death, all this tizzwaz ends...

And yet … and yet …? In view of all that has happened I cannot see certainty in that direction, however desirable it might seem. So where do I go from here? What does seem certain is that whatever the solution may be, it has to be sought within oneself, not 'out there' in space.

Nevertheless, that exogenous situation does have profound influences upon one's consciousness. As if it needed saying, volcanic eruptions, tornados, tsunamis and the like, whether or not one is directly involved in them, definitely impact on one's consciousness, in the same way as being hit by a truck or meteorite – or by the road in my cycling accident.

No less certain, therefore, is that in introspective exploration there is no way of shutting out, entirely, one's intimate connection with the continually intrusive, outside material world. But what about when, at death, that intimate physical interconnection is irrevocably severed, when, at cremation, say, one's molecular constituents are disassembled and thrown to the four winds to revert to their primitive state from which life originally sprang? In this case, it is certain that as a living, conscious organism we no longer

exist. But this cannot mean that we never existed. We happened and who would say that whatever has happened can ever be unhappened? Even if our sun turned into a supernova, as it must do in time, consuming our earth and all the other planets of the solar system, wiping out all traces of our ever having existed, it is difficult to think that in the system of things, even this would unhappen all that has happened, involving ourselves and our connections with earth and our society.

In what sense, then, might we and that society still 'exist', or 'persist' in that backwards extrapolated time dimension? And who or what does the extrapolating, since, in that scenario, we and our fellow beings are no longer around? Does it matter anyway, whether or not we existed, how we lived our lives, morally, immorally or whatever?

Who or what is there to assess and evaluate it? If nothing, then what point can there be, when we are alive, in worrying over the moral rightness or wrongness of our actions if, in the end, it all amounts to nothing? What could possibly be the function of all that conscientious goal-directed activity if, ultimately, there is no point to it?

So why do so many of us worry about the morality of our actions? Why, for instance, am I so dedicatedly driven towards determining scientific and philosophical truth? Why do the weeds in my drive keep thrusting up through the tarmac, annually, only to be destroyed as soon as possible by my weedkiller?

Why do we and other animals and plants seek to survive and proliferate with all the associated pain and effort if there is no purpose served at the end of it? But, then, the very fact that we do all these things seems to imply that there must be some purpose to it all, some moral purpose and evaluation which, as human animals, we somehow naturally intuit, else why ever do we do it?

What function could it possibly serve if it was entirely purposeless? So it seems to me that there must be a purpose to and beyond our lives by the way we live. Switching back, then, to the introspective view, that purpose, so far as I, personally am concerned, seems very clear-cut.

I hope that my expereince in this direction is not altogether unique. The fact is that for so long as I can remember I have been driven by an insatiable curiosity regarding just about everything. In exasperation, my parents sometimes called me 'Mister why, what and when?'

And now, in my eighties, that curiosity is far from abated. If anything it has increased. The view in my life's rear-view mirror makes much more sense to me than it ever did through the windscreen. The tracks I laid are now clearly discernible, the moral decisions I made, the mistakes I made and, mercifully, recovered, the trials faced, the discouragements, the fights physical and moral for justice, won and lost, the eventual prevalence of reason over stupidity; in all of this, the far more-than-nine lives survived in dangerous situations now seem little short of miraculous.

For instance, I suppose I could have met and married just about any girl. The fact that I married the one I did was, to say the least, propitious. She always was and is a stickler for honesty, a natural scholar with an almost eidetic (photographic) memory. She saw in me much that I couldn't recognise in myself. From her I received, by

tender persuasion, the education I had so arrogantly rejected and without which I could never have pursued my philosophical aims to the extent I have. Despite the incipient moral guidance I had received originally, in particular, from my uniquely liberally-minded and highly intelligent father, throughout all this time I ricocheted blindly around with no view ahead as from a mountain or even a tall tree.

Yet, looking back on it, all the while it was as though I was guided by some invisible hand. I have already mentioned the cascade of 'coincidences' that changed my life. Added to this are the number of times I nearly lost that life on account of my 'macho' competitiveness and sheer stupidity, in which, as I shudder to recall, I risked the lives of others along with my own.

However, in my training for Ground Combat during my National Service, I balked at the prospect of killing anyone, and I cannot imagine how, in their respective view of things, those who atom-bombed Hiroshima and Nagasaki could live with themselves, or even those who, in World -war Two, bombed thousands of women, children and old defenceless folks out of their beds in places like Hamburg and Berlin – and in particular Dresden.

Of course, those who did these things have to speak for themselves. For myself I am inordinately glad that I never had to face these things. I was taught all the skills of shooting, bayoneting and garroting fellow humans and finished my basic training as Top Recruit.

With the Korean war over, I missed by only months the possibility of having to put these skills into effect. So now I am left wondering how I would have dealt with that challenge if I'd had to. This brings me to what is, perhaps my most vexing problem.

It could, I suppose, be classified as a case of what psychologists call 'survivor guilt'. If I have been protected and guided, in the way I seem to have been, towards consummating the sublingual 'pact' I made in the leafy-green gloom of that little church by the bay, then why don't those same powers that guided and protected me, guide and protect others?

Why do some folks come into the world physically screwed-up and mentally others? Why do so many have to suffer unspeakable horrors, or are snuffed out before they are wise? Anyone who thinks there is some universal, benign Deity, or Creator, behind it all, must surely wonder how that can possibly be – as, indeed I do.

However, apart from some puny attempts, on occasions, to help and protect some of my fellow beings, as the finite creature I am, in no way can I be expected to solve that paradox as a whole. All I know for sure is that I came into the world, as the poet Omar Khayyam put it, “Why not knowing, Nor whence, like Water willy-nilly flowing.”

And yet, this was like that newborn calf with its innate knowledge of how to stand, breathe and suckle. In my case, I seem to have been born miraculously replete with such abilities as I possess to consummate my philosophcal aims to the extent I have, against all the very considerable odds. As far as the exogenous situation is concerned, there may be others like ourselves acting in terms of their own unique internal promptings of good or ill will towards us,

or natural disasters such as war, disease, earthquakes, tsunamis etc., in which we may be shot, infected, buried or drowned. Of course, it might well be that any optimism regarding soul-survival is like that of the guy who fell off the roof of the Empire State Building and, as he passed every window was heard to say 'I'm alright so far!'

Or it might be that what seems to be 'the light at the end of the tunnel' is an oncoming train. What is it, then that keeps one going? I suppose it is something like what religious people call 'faith'. Well, if that is what it is, then so be it. But faith in what?

Gee, I don't know! Just, faith, I suppose, just faith. What seems certain is that when we die, the material world's time stops for us. Existence implies persistence in time. Logically, then, it follows that where there is no time there is no existence.

The conclusion from this seems to be that when we die we cease to exist – as self, or consciousness, that is. Does one's soul then float away from one's body into public objective space, as some people claim? From the scientific point of view, that seems absurd.

But, then, what about those out-of-body experiences which people like Mary have reported? Does one simply reject those honest reports because they don't fit one's own narrow experience or scientific mindset? The question of dimensions. After ruminating indecisively on this for a while I thought I might try a different tack.

Sometimes people are heard to say that when we die the soul goes off to 'another dimension'. What, exactly, can this mean? If we assume that our souls persist after bodily death, then we have to assume that when the world's time ceases for us there is some other kind of time that takes over.

What kind of 'other time' might that be? Well, it was once thought space had only three dimensions, but then science discovered that it has four dimensions, defining what is called four-dimensional space-time. Might it not be, then, that there are more than four – a fifth, say, or a sixth, or who knows how many?

Before proceeding along these lines, let us be sure that we know what we mean by dimensions. A dimension is nothing spooky. It is simply a measure of something like, say, the length of a rug or a carpet. To distinguish this length-dimension from another dimension, say the width of that carpet, those two dimensions have to be projected at right-angles to each other.

Then there is the thickness, or depth of the carpet, for which another dimension is projected, so that the object is measured in three rectangular dimensions, namely length, breadth and depth. This is the only way in which those three cubic, or Cartesian measures may be projected without encroaching on each other's domains.

However, there is at least another Cartesian dimension which is missing. This is the dimension of duration, or time, without which the object has no existence. This is how time is projected in physics, as a fourth dimension at right-angles to the other three – the creepy-sounding 'fourth dimension'.

But there is nothing 'creepy' or supernatural about this dimension; it is every bit as physical a measure as the length, breadth and depth of a carpet. (Whoever heard of any piece of matter with length, breadth and depth but no duration – an instantaneous carpet, to

coin a phrase invented by H.G.Wells in his novel, The Time Machine.) Let's take another look at this fourth physical dimension. If the usual three dimensions may be regarded as measures of geometry, then the fourth is the dimension of history which, of course includes the dimensions of geometry.

Events in our history may be extrapolated along a line, like the geographical map of a journey that we may either have taken or intended to take. That is the fourth dimension which as portrayed, is entirely static. So that is not time. Time is the measure of what is actually happening right now at this present moment.

It involves motion and change, whereas the fourth dimension, extrapolated in that historical way is static. To represent the time that is actually passing, right now, we need a further, fifth, dimension at right angles to the other four in the same way that a time-dimension has to be added to the ordinary geometrical three to represent action.

In other words, time, as it is actually advancing – at its cutting edge, so to speak – has at least five dimensions, as contrasted to the fourth, historical, dimension which, without that fifth dimension, would be completely inert and changeless. This same reasoning might be taken to apply to that fifth dimension, creating a knock-on series of dimensions upon dimensions towards infinity.

Once, when I was discussing this idea with someone I was informed that it was the same as what was known as J.W.Dunne's Theory of Time, which was that for time to be real it must have an infinite series of dimensions – Dunne called it serial time. However, be that as it may, all we may glean from this argument, if true, is simply that time as we know it must have more than just four dimensions. (Recall, by the way, that in relativist physics, all these dimensions are fundamentally polar, that is, relative, or internal to the observer.

None of them exists in itself any more than space exists as 'space itself' independently of matter). That being the case, when, for us, the world's time ceases – when we no longer exist in that time – do all the other dimensions cease also? Certainly, we are no longer processing information from outside agencies nor are we transacting information with other agencies.

Nor, indeed, is one's brain processing information internally since it is no longer functioning. My trouble is that my own life's experience has been devoid of those incidents which others report, of seeing ghosts, spirits and so on, on what is called 'the other side', such as poltergeists, teleportation, tipping tables, out-of-body experiences and so on.

Anything in that 'paranormal' category I may have witnessed I have been able to explain on rational analysis. I have dreams, of course, but not of anything significant, and on those occasions when I have been unconscious, due to accident or under anaesthetic, I have been profoundly so, with no recall whatsoever of anything having gone on in the interim.

So, what can it possibly mean to say that when we die our souls go off into another dimension? Is that idea no more than just a noise, something parroted around without meaning? Let's check it out.

The question to be addressed, then, is that of whether, when one's connection with the outside world ceases – that is, the first in the whole time-dimensional series - that whole panoply of other internal dimensions ceases as well. This theory of serial time has been likened to seeing a mirror reflected in another mirror.

Anyone who has witnessed this phenomenon sees it as a long tunnel of reflections-of-reflections stretching away to infinity. If this is truly analogous to time as an infinte series of dimensions, then it is plain that when the first dimension in the series finshes, like taking away one of the mirrors, the whole series of further time-dimensions collapses into nothing.

The only logical conclusion to be drawn from this analogy is that, when, for us, the world's time ceases, so inexorably, does our soul. In other words, when we die, there is just nowhere for the soul to go – end of story. Is this, then, to be one's final conclusion, that all talk of 'life after death' is no more than just words in the language category of illusion, superstition, etc.?

Or is there, perhaps, some logical fallacy to be found in that nihilistic argument? Scepticism demands that one has to be sure before settling to that conclusion. One argument against that nihilstic conclusion might be that there is no direct logical connection between the mirror analogy and the serial character of time, that the mirror analogy is, in fact, inept.

It serves as an illustration, certainly, and if it proves anything it is that such a series of images or dimensions cannot be infinite, as Dunne suggested. This is because in both cases, mirrors and the time series, the ascending series starts with a finite beginning.

So the question becomes: When that finite beginning ends, does the whole series of dimensions end also, that is to say, collapse into nothing? Let's see if we could prove that. We seem to have proved, at least, that there have to be more than just the usual four dimensions of time, that there has to be a whole series of them ascending to some indefinite extent, and that there is a finite starting-point to the whole series. With this

conclusion in mind, let us re-examine the meaning of the statement that when we die our soul goes into 'another dimension'. Does this mean that, at death, when, for us, the first or 'ground-level' of the time series finishes, all the other, dependent, dimensions somehow persist, and that one's soul survives in this other-dimensional hierarchy? Is this, perhaps, the 'higher domain' into which our souls go at death? Is it the realm in which those people who report out-of-body experiences, of hovering above their bodies and looking down on their bodies on the operation table, observing the operation and whatever, experience? Who is to say? All I can glean at the moment is that given the fact that time necessarily has more than just one dimension, there seems to be no logical contradiction in those reports. The fact that I, myself, have had no such experiences has no logical bearing on this conclusion. This lack of logical resolution leaves one free to speculate further along these lines. One thing seems plain, which is that a belief, or faith in this afterlife of the soul, explains why people almost universally feel there is to be some final judgement on their actions throughout life. Without some such belief there could be no such thing

as religion or even, perhaps, as society. The fact, then, that there is so much religion seems to imply that our lives – perhaps all life – is pointed towards something beyond life, else why would there be any life at all? The trouble with religion, as I have found it, is that, as already discussed, there are so many conflicting presumptions as to what that afterlife might entail.

For this reason, in search of enlightenment, I have been inclined to prefer the more quietistic, more purely contemplative religions. There was a time when Mary and I used to explore the various avenues of religion – or, perhaps anti-religions – of Quakerism, Yoga, Buddhism, Zen and so on, to say nothing of visiting the various chapels and churches of various denominations.

At one time I attended a meditation session at a Maharishi school in a mansion in Leeke, Staffordshire and even considered joining the group. In general, however, I have developed a detestation of the rantings of religious leaders such as the famous Billy Graham, and I can scarcely imagine the mindset of those who have followed the likes of Paul Jones, whose subjects slavishly poisoned themselves and their chidren, on a whim of their leader.

Nor can I imagine the depths of depravity of so many so-called 'priests' and others, like Pol Pot, Hitler and Idi Amin, who tortured and killed people on a massive scale on the basis of nothing but some kooky inspiration conjured up from within their own id.

Not least can I fathom the intentions of those who sought to transport themselves spiritually to the comet Hale-Bopp in its last spectacular visit towards Earth. How these people command the following that they do defeats me utterly. In short, then, in seeking enlightenment, I have found myself looking more and more into myself for answers, ignoring all forms of organised thought involving icons, rituals, bells, gongs, whistles and the like.

My only true belief, when I come right down to it is in the power of reason, logical reason, that is – the logos, let's say - of the whole universe of discourse, which has to apply, not only within oneself but also, albeit in an entirely unspoken way, throughout the whole of the stellar and galactic firmament.

By 'logic', here, I mean, of course, logic conducted in accordance with the strict mathematical rules of the subject as laid down, originally, by Aristotle and honed by his followers over the centuries. This is nothing like what so many people call 'logic', which is not logic at all but simply, at best, pure rhetoric – or else just noise.

Some of the arguments it has been my displeasure to witness have been just words used with no more meaning than the sound of bubbles bursting in a swamp. Where, then, do I stand at this late stage in my life? All I can say is that in my mind I keep going back to that nameless, most peaceful condition in which I found myself in the stillness of that seaside church.

On balance, then, I am inclined to say that I should maintain trust in whatever it might have been that has guided and protected me up till now, to guide and protect me from here on. At the risk of losing anyone who might be reading this, I cannot think of anything more appropriate, in summing it all up, than to quote something I learned during my childhood days in my enforced attendance at Sunday School. This, as I recall, is the Twenty-Third Psalm, I have no

idea who 'The Lord' is in this, or where 'His House' is but, anyway, here it goes as we used to chant it in school and church: The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want. He maketh me to lie down in green pastures: he leadeth me beside the still waters. He restoreth my soul: he leadeth me in the paths of righteousness for his name's sake. Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me; thy rod and thy staff they comfort me. Thou preparest a table before me in the presence of mine enemies: thou anointest my head with oil; my cup runneth over. Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me all the days of my life: and I will dwell in the house of the LORD for ever.

From a purely disinterested – i.e., non-religious – point of view this ancient text seems to provide an almost perfect template for what I am trying to sum up about my life's experiences. I don't know how I could express it better in modern-day language. In fine, then, these are my current convictions. Now, I can't speak for anyone else, but for myself I am convinced that my life has been a test, to what end I cannot presume to know. All I am sure of is that it has been, for me, a test of will and integrity, a test of honesty, a test of intelligence, resolve and commitment, with good will towards others – even, sometimes, of enemies and detractors, and I can't think of any purely forensic formula for social harmony better than the Christian directive to 'Love thy neighbour as thyself', 'Love thine enemy', 'Bless those that curse you...' and so on. My own views are definitiely aligned with that, albeit in a purely secular way. In general, I feel that my life, so far, has been a test, almost continually, of what in the modern vernacular, is called 'bottle', and I'm glad that, so far, at least, I have not been tested to destruction. In any case, I cannot see what purpose there could be in pursuing these goals as naturally and dedicatedly, almost automatically, as I have if, in the end, it leads nowhere. Moreover, if, as it seems, this life is a test, then it must be as in an exam, one has to guess the answers to the questions as best as one can, without knowing for sure what the 'model answers' are or the intentions of the examiners. To know these answers ahead of time would short-circuit the purpose of the test and annul the operation, making the whole thing completely pointless. As for any notion of a deity, I wouldn't presume to provide it with a name, far less to live and act in its name, which would surely be disastrous. I have no special regard for

ayatollahs or priests of any kind. Vicars who assume the role of mediators between me and heaven are, to me, just like anyone else, and I cannot regard the man Jesus of Nazareth as any more than a very good and courageous man, like, say, Fred of Liverpool, but perhaps a superior human being, an exemplar, in that same department of moral integrity that contains us all, sweaty and stinky as we are.

The same goes, so far as I am concerned for saints and celebrities, Sirs, Doctors and Professors of all description. We are all, ultimately and inexorably, in the same boat. Whether or to what extent the same moral code applies to us all, who can say.

People are different, sometimes very different. Anyway, that is how I honestly feel, my quest continues – for what? I have no preconceived idea. My father, on his death-bed said to my mother, 'Lilly, I am going, and I don't know where.' This will be the same for all of us.

Where, if anywhere, do we go? When it comes right down to it, no-one knows the answer to that, and no-one has come back to tell us. For me, it remains an ineffable paradox that we are designed as we are, goal-directed animals, towards what ultimate goal, no-one really knows – except, of course, those loons who tell us they have God-given pre-knowledge of that end-aim of all existence but cannot give us any rational explanation of how dinosaurs, mosquitoes, bed-bugs, earth-worms and so on fit into that scheme.

Is that end-aim there exclusively for man? I find that difficult to square with the plain fact that all life-forms everywhere have that same goalseeking disposition, and I can't see any good reason to exclude the immensely largest section of life on this planet from the same end-aim that we so arbitrarily project for ourselves.

So, in the end analysis we simply do what we do, without really knowing why. As the actor Fred MacMurray put it, in the film 'The Rains of Ranchipur' (1955): 'A man's gotta do what a man's got to do.' For me that puts it in a nutshell, although I know that there are 'beautiful people' out there who would condemn me for voicing that sentiment.

When I was studying at Coleg Harlech, the lecturer, a former Oxford don, read to us the well-known poem by Rudyard Kipling, called 'If'. I have always admired this poem, so much indeed that I presented to all my grandsons a copy of that poem in illuminated script. Here it is:

If IF you can keep your head when all about you Are losing theirs and blaming it on you, If you can trust yourself when all men doubt you, But make allowance for their doubting too; If you can wait and not be tired by waiting, Or being lied about, don't deal I lies, Or being hated, don't give way to hating, And yet don't look too good, nor talk too wise:

If you can dream - and not make dreams your master; If you can think and not make thoughts your aim; If you can meet with Triumph and Disaster And treat those two impostors just the same; If you can bear to hear the truth you've spoken Twisted by knaves to make a trap for fools, Or watch the things you gave your life to, broken, And stoop and build 'em up with worn-out tools: If you can make one heap of all your winnings And risk it on one turn of pitch-and-toss, And lose, and start again at your beginnings And never breathe a word about your loss; If you can force your heart and nerve and sinew To serve your turn long after they are gone And so hold on when there is nothing in you Except the Will which says to them: 'Hold on!' If you can talk with crowds and keep your virtue, Or walk with Kings – nor lose the common touch, If neither foes nor loving friends can hurt you, If all men count with you, but none too much; If you can fill the unforgiving minute With sixty seconds' worth of distance run, Yours is the Earth and everything that's in it, And – which is more – you'll be a Man, my son!

After the lecturer read to us this poem I was stunned when he tried to tell us that from a scholarly point of view, it was the 'worst poem ever written'. It was he that was then stunned when I jumped up and said to him 'I disagree', and told him that so far as I was concerned it was one of the most inspired poems ever written. This, I later learned, cost me marks in my overall student assessment. Well, that's just about it. It's me to the bone. Let anyone make of it what they will and I may be damned for it. Here is a piece from another poem. It is by William Ernest Henley: Invictus “Out of the night that covers me, Black as the Pit from pole to pole, I thank whatever gods may be For my unconquerable soul. In the fell clutch of circumstance I have not winced or cried aloud. Under the bludgeonings of chance My head is bloody but unbowed.

Beyond this place of wrath and tears Looms but the horror of the shade, And yet the menace of the years Finds and shall find me, unafraid. It matters not how strait the gate, How charged with punishments the scroll, I am the master of my fate: I am the captain of my soul.”

Well, that's it, I reckon, finis.

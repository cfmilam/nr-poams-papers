Mirrors and Things

N. Vivian Pope

Here is a riddle. Describe what a mirror looks like in an unoccupied room. Does it reflect everything in the room, from every possible angle or from none at all? If so, then what does that all-over reflection look like? Is it silver all over, or just blank?

This is, of course, a conundrum, because 'look like' in this context makes no sense if no-one is looking.

Now the surface of a lake is a mirror. So is that of a polished table. All objects are mirrors in this sense. How we see them depends on how they reflect light, what surfaces they present to us, and so on. Try to describe a scene as it appears from everywhere at once. Even worse, try to describe that scene as it appears to no observer at all, that is, just as it is 'in itself', independently of perception and language.

Describe a vacuum, out there in space, as it is in and of itself. How big or small is it, how old or young? What time is it in that vacuum? What date? What is its length, breadth, depth and volume? Is it stationary or moving … and so on? Is there, then, such a thing as a vacuum in and of itself? But how can there be when such a situation is impossible to describe?

And what about the light by which we see things? Does that travel in the vacuum, as alleged? But at what speed does anything travel relative to a vacuum, constant or otherwise? These questions are impossible to answer.

And what about material objects? What about their hardness and solidity, their weight, the sound they make when they strike one another, their colour, their chemical composition, their taste, their smell? Let us take these properties in turn.

i) Hardness and solidity: An object is hard and solid only from some point of view in which its atomic constituents are in rapid motion. From a point of view, relative to which those constituents are motionless – that is, from the viewpoint of something moving with those constituents – there is no such hardness or solidity. It is easy to toss a coin between the spokes of a bicycle wheel when that wheel is stationary, but impossible when that wheel is spinning at a very fast speed. With its extremely fast reflexes, a bat may fly through a fan that is rotating at a fast rate, whereas at higher speeds of rotation the bat treats the fan as a solid object. All objects are like that; whether they are hard and solid, or fluid or gaseous, depends, like the mirror, on what viewpoint in space and time is taken.

ii) Weight: A body has weight only in relation to other bodies. No body has weight in itself. The weight of an astronaut depends on whether the surface that is supporting him is that of the earth, the moon or the floor of a spacecraft. The same goes for a body's mass. Both weight and mass are manifestations of how bodies interact in some specified frame of reference. The mass that a body has in one frame of reference is not the same as it has in another frame if the frames are in relative motion. So there is no mass nor weight that can be manifest in the same way in all possible observer-frames everywhere at once.

iii) Sound: What is the sound of a falling tree heard from every possible place and distance from it? Sounds that characterise a falling tree, a voice, a trumpet, an orchestra, the cry of a bird, or whatever, are impossible to identify or describe other than from some specific location by an organism capable of such identification.

iv) Colour: Is the colour of a flower the same for an artist as it is for a bee or an ant, a moth or a bat? Colour is something that organisms specifically react to in their own characteristic ways. A snake can see in infrared in what is, to us, complete darkness, while a bee sees in ultraviolet, to which colour we humans are blind. Colour refers always to some particular spectral line. There is no colour of the spectrum as a whole in its full range from infrared and radio to ultraviolet, X-ray and gamma.

v) Taste and smell: It hardly needs saying that these qualities and properties of things are observer-dependent. There is no smell or taste that is describable other than in terms of the olfactory and gustatory sensations of creatures possessed of those senses, far less in terms of tastes and scents 'in themselves' separate from those creatures and their reactions.

What about remote viewing, by cameras (still or video), mirrors or whatever? But what instrument could possibly be devised to view from all positions and angles at the same time? What sort of scene could that represent? How would any landscape painter depict it? If he tried, who would understand it?

But what about a map? Doesn't that present a view of things and places at the same time? No. What a map does is to provide instructions as to where one should position oneself in order to view something in the ordinary way from different places at different times.

What, then, about a view of the earth and its continents from the moon, as photographed by astronauts? Well, certainly, the higher you go above the earth, the more of it you see. But, again, there is no view that can be taken from all these different heights and distances all at once, far less showing everywhere around the globe.

How is it, then, that we can presume to speak of things as objects and situations in themselves if, in themselves, they are impossible to describe? Every statement we make about the world – the only world we can know about, the real world – has to be made implicitly from some specified viewpoint or other. There simply is no way in which we can assume the overview of some all-over observer who sees everything (or anything) from all possible viewpoints and angles, at all times and all places, all at once, far less from no viewpoint at all.

The only way in which we can describe anything from all possible viewpoints at once, whether in ordinary language, geometry, mathematics, cosmology or whatever, is in metaphysical imagination – that is, by implicitly presuming what is, in effect, a 'God's eye view'.

What does all this tell us? It tells us that there is no sense whatsoever, in secular science contexts, in talking about things, positions, processes, measures or whatever, other than relatively to some finite and specifiable point of observation. This, no more and no less, is what is meant by relativity. So Relativity is not some abstruse mathematic 'theory', as so many people are disposed to imagine. It lies right at the very heart of our dealings with the world in commonsense as in science.

It stresses not only that there is no reality in things-in-themselves, but also that all reality, physical and practical, is essentially relational. No 'atoms', 'molecules', 'quarks', 'bosons', 'fermions' … or whatever, come to us 'out of the blue' labelled exactly and permanently for what they are and how they are distinguished from one another. They do not exist in any self-sufficiently 'real' sense. The only definable existence they have is in informational interrelations with other objects and, ultimately, with some observer who classifies, divides and labels (names) these things, according to their interrelations, in a finite system of language devised for the tongues of men not of gods.

This is what the philosopher Wittgenstein meant by his massively misunderstood statement, 'The World is Language'. It does not mean that nothing is real, that everything we are accustomed to talk about is something which, as observers, we either individually or corporately dream up. What it means is that whatever we think of in normal circumstances as real is conditional on being a) observable, b) commonly communicable, and c) remaining logically coherent and consistent with other experiences generally, our own and those of others. This is at different places and times as expressed within a common language system which is never entirely fixed and finished but is – or at least, should be – always evolving, adjusting and readjusting to ever newer experiences.

Nothing, then, is plainer, than what physical science ought to be, namely, the ongoing, categorising, classifying and dividing of physical phenomena, discovering and describing how, in nature, one thing leads to another and encapsulating this knowledge in a logically coherent and consistent, developing system of language.

Is this what Modern Physics is? Decidedly not. Nothing is more logically incoherent and logically inconsistent than the language of Modern Physics. Not even to its most expert exponents has Physics ever been more incomprehensible than at this present time.

Why is this? There should be no good reason for it, since there can be no built-in principle of incoherence or logical inconsistency in nature itself. It follows, then, that insofar as we find it incomprehensible, it has to be our fault entirely. So why have we made it so incomprehensible?

The reason is not hard to find. It is all a matter of the language that physicists use to describe things. The fact is that it is the language of physics, not the nature it seeks to represent, that has become incomprehensible. And why is that? The answer is obvious when we think about it.

There are three main causes. One is that physicists forget that the things they talk and write about, such as 'photons', 'field-forces', 'electric charges', 'electrons', 'quarks', 'superstrings', 'black holes', 'wormholes', the 'Big Bang' … and so on, are all, in the very last analysis, items of language – human language, that is, not of some imagined cosmical deity. Moreover, due to the arbitrary Arts/Science academic divide, physicists are professionally unaware of what philosophers know, that all items of language, especially at that language's expanding scientific interface with nature, can never be regarded as fixed and permanent, that it is always revisable in principle in all its various categories and distinctions, even down to its most familiar words and definitions.

The second cause is the syndrome that has long been known as scholasticism. This is the over-reverence for traditional language that leads to long periods of stultification, particularly in science. It is the sort of reverence that the Educational Establishment fosters in its science students as a measure of their competence in the subject. All emphasis is placed on maintaining this process of language-building, more or less by accretion, without disturbing its traditional norms and standards. This tends to perpetuate the process without disruption, leaving scientific genius, as someone has said, to do what the hell it likes – in other words, to grow, like a cancer cell, free of logical control.

Students are not encouraged to realise that much of this traditional language, subjected to proper logical analysis, may transpire to be mere jargon. Only in the rarest of instances therefore, have items of physics language had to be completely withdrawn. Just two examples of this are 'phlogiston' and 'caloric', which are now no more than archaisms. This, however, has failed to alert physicists to the fact that all other appellations are, in principle, equally tentative and conditional, subject to that need for them to fit logically with the body of language as a whole, which is called understanding.

The third and far from the least reason is the premium that is placed, in modern physics, on theory, as opposed to plain observation. Theories involved in the continual reviewing and revising of traditional interpretations of natural phenomena are one thing. Theories aimed at solving puzzles that are created by the language of physics itself are something else entirely. Although it is obvious that there is never any guarantee that the traditional language that physicists have devised in interpreting phenomena can be taken as forever fixed and oracular, it is nevertheless taken by some physicists as sacrosanct, hence as a firm basis on which to construct their theories.

Take, for instance, the phenomenon traditionally interpreted as 'electric charge'. During a certain era, this was thought to be a measure of static energy, in arbitrary units called 'coulombs', whereby particles of matter attract and repel one another by invisible field-forces in vacuo. It was thought that ultimate particles possessed of this mysterious 'charge' might be usefully called 'electrons' after the Greek word for 'amber', the substance that attracts bits of paper and so on when rubbed.

The distinction between this sort of 'electrical' energy, measured in coulombs, and ordinary mechanical energy measured in joules is a purely arbitrary one, since joules and coulombs can be inter-converted. Indeed, it can be shown[1] that the mysterious static 'charge' ascribed to the particle called the 'electron' can be more sensibly and economically reinterpreted in mechanical units of 'spin', which makes unnecessary the traditional distinction, in physics language, between the 'electron' and other mass-particles generally.

This, logically, should remove from physics language altogether, all particular references to the 'electron' and all other words beginning with the prefix 'electro'. However, now that the altogether illustrious tradition of 'electronics' has been set, these words have become fixed in the language to the extent that they are taken to be as definitive of physical objects as words like 'cow' and 'cannon ball'. This leads to theories, and theories upon esoteric theories, as to how and why the entity named the 'electron' and its accompanying 'electrostatic field' behaves in ways that bits of ordinary matter don't.

Thus, the word 'electron' is conceived as something that exists 'in itself', as some deity might be presumed to perceive it. The possibility that the original interpretation of the phenomenon at the time, hence the connotations involved in the naming of it, might not have been the best or most propitious for the future of theoretical physics can now scarcely be considered.

In sum, then, it is proposed that the only way in which theoretical physics can emerge from its present state of stagnation is to restore a sensible balance between theory and plain observation. Theories as to what happens to one's image in a mirror when one leaves the room may be all very well for purposes of confounding and entertaining an audience. The same goes for theories about what 'electrons', 'photons' and so on do when they are all alone in the vacuum.

Physicists need to ask themselves whether it makes sense to wonder whether 'God' sees images in the mirror in an unoccupied room? Obviously not, so why talk as though he does? And, by the same token, why should we presume that 'electrons', 'force-fields', 'quarks', 'wave-functions' and so on exist, exactly in the way we conceive and name them, when no-one is around?

Nature cannot create conundrums. Only our uses and misuses of language can do that.

POAMS in a Nutshell

N. Vivian Pope & Anthony D. Osborne — 2001

POAMS, the acronym coined by Jon Blay for the Pope Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis, is a theory about space, time and motion which takes its historical departure from the mechanics of Aristotle rather than Newton and is therefore, as George Galeczki puts it, theoretically imiscible, not only with classical theory but also with modern theories such as QED and SCR. Newton stated, in the face of all evidence to the contrary, that the natural state of free, uninterrupted motion is rectilinear. For Aristotle, spatial motion is naturally cyclic, or orbital. Motion in a straight line, he argued, must either start somewhere and end somewhere, in which case it is interrupted, or else its trajectory is infinitely long, which is unreal. Only in a closed loop, or cycle, he concluded, can motion be truly force-free or un-interrupted. Aristotle’s argument is supported by the fact that in free space all momentum is implicitly angular momentum, which is an automatically paired and balanced relation between bodies. In such a relation, for a mass to travel in a straight line with respect to its angular momentum countermass, the radius r in its angular momentum mvr has to be infinite (a straight line and a circle of infinite radius being one and the same). But if r is infinite, then so is the angular momentum mvr, so that for any finite amount of angular momentum, with finite mass and velocity, the radius r must also be finite, hence the motion-trajectory is closed, in the way Aristotle described. Taking cyclic motion as the naturally force-free state of motion turns Newtonian mechanics on its head. The traditional ‘forces’ of gravitation, magnetostatics and electrostatics are no longer those invisible, in vacuo forces which are usually thought of as preventing bodies from travelling in straight lines and forcing them into orbits. They are those real, that is, felt and measured, forces which are manifest in reaction to anything preventing those bodies from travelling in their natural orbital trajectories. This makes the ‘gravitational’ factor G different for spinning and for non-spinning bodies. An electron, whose spin kinetic energy is much larger than its orbital kinetic energy orbits in a far smaller radius than does a particle for which the reverse is the case. These spins, in an overall angular momentum equation supplied by the theory, account for the differences in the strengths of the true forces required to oppose the different natural orbits of ‘gravity’, ‘electrostatics’ and ‘magnetism’. Everything in free space thus naturally tends, in one way or another, to orbit everything else, and the only thing that prevents one body from maintaining a regular orbit around another is its tendency to orbit each and every other body within range, which is what makes the spatial motions of bodies as complicated as we actually observe, and never rectilinear in the ideal way Newton envisaged. Moreover, since angular momentum is an overall conserved quantity, all the different, incipiently cyclic, paired and balanced motion-systems are instantly correlated. By the same token, any change in the angular momentum of one cyclic system at one place must immediately (i.e., non-locally) affect others at other places, so long as the cycles in those distant systems are ‘resonant’ with those in the original location. (If they are not, then no interaction can take place.) And since angular momentum is quantised (that is to say, its ultimate units are irreducible and unsharable) there cannot, in any single quantum instance, be more than two mutually orbiting particles involved. These are the cyclic microsystems we call atoms. When that particle we call the ‘electron’ changes its angular momentum around its countermass (say the ‘proton’ or another ‘electron’) a compensatory change, by exactly that same amount has to take place at another atom somewhere else, where the conditions of resonance are just right. The time-sequences of those instantaneous distance correlations, at the distance-time rate c are what we call light, every quantum of which therefore consists of those two instantaneous and time-delayed components. This removes the notorious ‘either instantaneous or time-delayed’ (EPR) paradox by combining rather than separating the two and leads to a very simple Pythagorean formulation of relativistic time-dilation, from which the usual formula of Special Relativity (STR) follows immediately. A significant difference, however, between POAMS and STR is that although c remains the same for all observers, the reference-zero for all time-dilation is the overall angular momentum (Machian) frame of the ‘fixed stars’, not simply of one observer relative to another, as in the theory of Einstein. Also, the Hubble redshift is interpreted not as evidence of an ‘expanding universe’ (for which angular momentum would have to be continually created) but as a time-dilational effect due to statistical increases in omnidirectional velocity in proportion to observational distance. ■

Viv Pope Monday, 15 October 2001

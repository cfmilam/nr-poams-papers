I. Introduction

There are many sciences and scientific theories but there is only one nature. Unlike our theories about it, nature is a Whole. Why, then, during its more than two thousand years of evolution, have our Science’s representations of nature become so increasingly fragmented?

Something is surely wrong, and seriously wrong, somewhere. Explanations may abound as to what it is, exactly, that has gone wrong with our Science. In these pages we leave these sorts of explanations to the historical analyst and ask ourselves the more fundamental question as to what is the most basic, most non-theoretical, most ahistorical and most common definition of a science of nature that is possible.

And what else can that most parsimonious definition be but that it is the rational decoding of sensory and instrumental data? And what, other than ourselves, both directly and in communication with others like ourselves, is the ultimate decoder of that natural information?

Right at its very start, therefore, science is relativistic, which is to say that whatever it describes as existing in nature is described and measured relatively to observation. That is to say, observational empiricism and relativism go, inseparably, hand-in-hand.

Right at that same root lies also the fact that scientific information is quantised. This follows from the fact that for information to be information, its ultimate elements, or informational bits, have to be discrete and unconnected. In other words, the informational content of a continuum of connected elements, is zero.

For any sequence of such elements to be informational, those elements have to be discrete and unconnected. In other words, those sequences have to be probabilistic, not determinate. In the jargon of Information-Technology, they have to be stochastic [from the Greek stokhazesthai, to aim at, or conjecture (stokhos meaning a target)].

And indeed, this is precisely what we find on the most basic level of our informational contacts with nature, where all observational information is quantised in this way into discrete events of this indeterminate nature. Each such event is a complete and irreducible packet, or quantum, of energy times time, or action, in units of Planck’s constant $h$.

But if we, as observers, are the decoders of this information, then what are the encoders? What else can these be but those objects whose informational ‘bar-codes’ we read with instruments such as the spectrometer. Together with these and other instruments such as the telescope and microscope we can extrapolate, from the information presented in observation – that is, from natural phenomena – all that we know about the distance and direction of objects, their positions and motion in relation to ourselves and to one another, their relative masses, their chemical characteristics – in fact, everything that is known to Science about what we perceive as physical objects and processes.

Now so far, nothing has been said about time. But what is time? What else can time be but what we read with those instruments we call clocks? Some of those objects we extrapolate from observational information are clocks. A clock is a physical process which we arbitrarily choose as a time-standard by which to measure the rates of other processes.

Such a standard is, historically, the period of the earth’s daily rotation or that of the moon around the earth, the earth around the sun, and so on. And, of course, we have regulated mechanisms, constructed in the way that John Harrison originally did, in the 18th century, for the express purpose of fixing an arbitrary non-local time-standard such as Greenwich Mean Time for purposes of navigation and

so on. Such a clock, or regular periodic process, is that of the eclipses of Jupiter’s principal satellite, Io, as it orbits its parent planet. Olaus Römer, the Danish astronomer, in 1676, was the first to observe that as this clock moves away from the terrestrial observer it loses time and that as it moves towards the observer it gains time.

This ratio of distance-change to time-change Römer found to be a constant, c, the refined value of which is now $299{,}792{,}458$ metres to the second. It is at this point in our scientific history, with Römer’s discovery of c, that classical physics ceases and modern theoretical physics begins.

The first move in this theoretical direction, on the basis that c has the dimensions of a velocity, was to assume that it is a velocity. But a velocity of what? In this way there immediately arises the theoretical question of what it is, exactly, that is supposed to be moving at this ‘velocity $c$’.

And, as everyone knows, what was assumed to be moving was light, conceived as the conveyor, or retailer, of the information by which we observe things. But of course, conceived in this way, ‘light’ is purely theoretical. We never see light leave an object and wing its way towards us in order for us to see the object.

Nor do we ever see light travelling between one object and another in a pure vacuum. What is commonly regarded, therefore, as the ‘fact’ that light travels in the vacuum is not a fact at all but is, in the last analysis, just pure supposition. It is from this pure supposition that practically all our modern theories stem as to what it is that is travelling in that completely invisible way and by what invisible means.

The quantised character of the observed spectral energies suggests particles, whereas their periodic and resonant character suggests waves. But waves in what? What is there to wave in a vacuum? Theories abound, therefore, as to what it is that conducts these luminous energies from one place to another.

Is it some invisible quasi-substance such as a ‘wave-particle’, whatever that might be? Do the ‘waves’ travel in some sort of ‘field’, or ‘ether’? Speculative theories still vie with one another as to what may be the defining properties and characteristics of this assumed light-conveying ‘medium’.

But despite all attempts to detect and measure these properties, even with the very best instruments that modern technology can supply, empirical science has drawn a complete blank. In modern physics, all conception of the information we call light as consisting of either waves or particles have ended, as they began, as bits of pure theoretical speculation.

However, apart from any question of the sheer pragmatical value of these theories in the development of our scientific history, it is a plain fact that this interpretation of c as a ‘velocity’, which led to this whole theoretical extravaganza, was never logically obligatory.

For instance, it does not automatically follow that because all velocities are distances divided by time, all distances divided by time are velocities. I can divide the length of my tabletop by the length of time it has stood there since I purchased it, without that signifying any kind of ‘velocity’.

The difference, of course, is that the essence of a true velocity is that it applies to something travelling, which, as we have seen, is eminently not the case with c. The logical alternative to interpreting c as a ‘velocity’ is as described by Herman Bondi in his book Assumption and Myth in Physical Theory :

Any attempt to measure the velocity of light is … not an attempt at measuring the velocity of light but an attempt at ascertaining the length of the standard metre in Paris in terms of time-units.

This logical alternative to our historical interpretation of c as a ‘velocity’ marks the point of

departure for a whole new, ahistorical approach to physics which has been scarcely more than just hinted at in the chronicles of academic Philosophy. In this alternative, ahistorical approach, the traditional model of the universe as a Great Machine, whose ultimate parts are self-sufficient atoms separated by a self-sufficient vacuum and causally connected by an incidental travelling intermediary called ‘light’, is replaced by a very different model. This is the model of the informational hologram. The distinguishing features and implications of this paradigmically divergent approach to physics are as described in the following pages.

II. The Universal ‘Hologram’ In light, taking its full spectral range from deepest infrared to farthest ultraviolet and Gamma, is contained all the information there is about the world and our place in it. What we do not have in light is any direct information as to what we suppose is the existence and movement of light in the voids between objects that we extrapolate from the information contained in the light. Indeed, there is a profound fallacy in supposing that we can ever have knowledge of anything beyond or underlying the information itself, anything of which we can have knowledge that is not based on information in the same way. This means that there are no gaps, voids or spaces which are not observational projections or extrapolations made from information contained in the light as actually seen. This reverses the traditional priority of conception which puts light in space by putting space – and time – in the light. There is then no question of light ‘travelling at the rate $c$ in vacuo’. The ratio c is simply a dimensional constant in Bondi’s sense of a ‘conversion factor’ for converting our observational measures in conventional metres into conventional measures in seconds, in the way that one might convert measures in pounds into measures in kilograms, or measures in yards into measures in metres. This signifies that distance was never pure distance, as traditionally imagined, but was always, if only we could have been aware of it, distance-time, as Römer eventually discovered.

II.2 A Non-Theoretical Account of Quantum Instantaneity

From our pristine relativistic formula (1) it can be seen straightaway that what has been traditionally conceived as a delayed time s/c between one light-transacting body and another is intrinsically instantaneous (i.e., when $t_R = s/c$, t = 0). In other words, at the basic level of observational information which we customarily call light, there is nothing taking place between objects and observers of the sort that we normally conceive as ‘interaction’ involving mechanical corpuscles in a primordial vacuum or waves in ‘ethers’, ‘fields’ or whatever.

All that theoretical paraphernalia is removed in favour of a straightforward, non-theoretical, purely observational approach to physics in which the ultimate informational elements (quanta) are as much an integral part of an object as of one’s observation of it.

All the usual basic dimensions of space, time, mass, etc. – including those of so-called ‘light-velocity’ – are then no more nor less than informational extrapolations made by the decoder (the observer) from directly perceived patterns and sequences of the informational bits, in the way that all the breadth, depth and duration of a video scenario are projected by the viewer out of patterns and sequences of the informational elements.

In the video case, these elements are the sporadically lighted screen pixels; in the real case they are the ultimate action-quanta. It is in this sense that the universe of perception and experience resembles a ‘hologram’ rather than the implicit ‘machine’ of traditional ‘theory’.

Devoid of any determinate mechanical linkages, the ultimate informational elements (quantum bits) are entirely probabilistic in their observational appearances and sequences. All the causality that disappears on this ultimate microphysical level reappears on the ordinary macrophenomenal level, where it is replaced by purely information-based processes.

These processes are governed by statistical laws like that of Claude Shannon’s information-technology rewrite of Ludwig Boltzmann´s probabilistic rendering of the Second Law of Thermodynamics – or, as it is more popularly known, the Law of Entropy’.

To visualise what this means, think of what we see on a television screen when the aerial is unplugged. The ‘snow’ we observe on the screen when this happens represents a state of maximum probability and minimum information. In this situation, what we would normally recognise as a programme appearing by pure chance would be the extreme of improbability – in fact, a miracle.

The measure of improbability that we observe when the programme is running is therefore proportional to the measure of information that the programme contains. And from that informational improbability we project all the various observational properties and qualities of the programme scenario. And so it is, by analogy, with ourselves as ‘viewers’ of nature. The flat TV screen is

replaced by a panoramic presentation in which, with other observers, we are interactively immersed. It is a presentation so fine-grained that the ‘pixels’ cannot normally be discerned, being of quantum magnitude . And what more suitable description of such a system can there be than that of a ‘hologram’.

III. A Unified Non-Field Theory

When we look into space, at the planets, stars and so on, what do we see? We see objects separated from one another in different directions and dimensions. What we do not see is light ‘travelling in vacuo’ from these objects towards us or towards one another.

Nor do we see the ‘gravitational forces’ that are supposed to pull these objects together into orbital configurations like those of the solar system. What we actually see are bodies freely orbiting one another in systems of angular momentum. This is the quantity $mvr$, where m is the mass of the orbiting body, v is its orbital velocity and r is the radius of orbit – conceived, here, as ideally circular, for simplicity.

All this is a matter of plain perception, these parameters m, v and r being projected into the physical scenario from direct observational information in the way that has already been described. In historical hindsight, therefore, it seems strange that the famous Isaac Newton, viewing the motions of the heavens, conceived it as a ‘Law of Nature’ that natural force-free motion takes place in straight lines.

Newton’s so-called ‘First Law of Motion’ is therefore profoundly unempirical, since nowhere can bodies be observed to travel in accordance with that ‘Law’. Nothing in space travels in that way. Even balls on billiard tables and pucks on ice would be seen to travel in curves if the surfaces on which they travel are long enough.

Nowhere, then, is there any truly straight-line momentum $mv$ of the sort that Newton envisaged. All momentum is angular momentum $mvr$. Newton’s linear momentum is thus an ideal special case of an angular momentum with r at theoretical infinity – a straight line and a circle of infinite radius being one and the same.

When I drive my car, it stays on the road rather than travelling through the air because it does not have sufficient angular momentum to become a satellite. If it had, it would orbit the earth as naturally as the moon does around the earth and the earth-and-moon around the sun.

As it so happens, at whatever speed it is possible or legal for my car to do, its natural force-free orbit with regard to the earth (if we imagine the earth’s mass to be confined within the volume of, say, a football) would be somewhere way down below where we ordinarily stand .

However, since the earth’s surface normally prevents the car from following that natural force-free orbit, it presses upwards on the wheels of the car with a reactionary force of 9.78 newtons of force for every kilogram of the car’s mass. Why, then, did Newton’s apple fall from the tree?

Not because it was pulled towards the earth by some invisible ‘force of gravity’, as our traditional textbooks describe it. It was because with the angular momentum it possessed due to the earth’s rotation at Woolsthorpe, the apple’s natural force-free orbit would be 289/290ths of the distance from the soles of our feet to the earth's centre .

The only force it makes sense to speak of, therefore, is not the traditional invisible one acting in vacuo but the real, sensible and measurable force that is exerted by whatever it is that prevents the object from orbiting freely. In the case of Newton’s apple this was the twig on which the apple hung before falling. None of this, of course, conflicts with what, in commonsense contexts, we feel as the

force preventing us from falling and which we measure on a weighing-scale or weighbridge as the ‘force of gravity’. What it does dispense with is the theoretical notion of a ‘gravitational field’ surrounding bits of matter and pervading space. Indeed, it makes redundant all the other sorts of ‘field’ conceptions such as ‘electric fields’, ‘magnetic fields’ and so on, which have been constructed on the same theoretical lines.

For instance, converting the so-called ‘charge’ on an electron, in units of coulombs ($1,602 \times 10^{-19}$ C) into a mechanical spin kinetic energy in joules ($2.18 \times 10^{-18}$ J), and taking the electron’s mass ($9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg) as no more than just a pure mass, gives an angular momentum figure for that mass, of mvr = h/2 (= $1.054 \times 10^{-34}$ kg m2/s).

These are precisely the parameters which Niels Bohr deduced for the electron in the hydrogen atom, on the alternative basis of classical electrodynamical theory . In any event, the measurable force required to remove the ‘electron’ from its lowest, or ‘ground-level’ orbit around the nucleus would be in the immensely larger order of $10^{39}$ times the force required for a pair of non-spinning masses equivalent to those of the ‘electron’ and ‘proton’.

For these much tighter orbits the so-called ‘gravitational constant’, which is normally $6,726 \times 10^{-11}$ Nm3/kg s for macrophysical bodies in ordinary astronomical and engineering situations, is hugely increased to 1.5 × 1029 m3/kg s for these particles with their hugely greater proportion of spin to orbital angular momentum.

This is far more than would be possible for macrophysical bodies. Such macroscopic bodies would disintegrate long before such spin-energies could be reached, which is why, at the macrolevel, these spin-effects cannot normally be noticed. In this way, by replacing ‘gravity’ with angular momentum, the notorious problem of seeking the ‘unified field’ is solved automatically, by getting rid of these ‘field’ concepts altogether, along with all the theoretical paraphernalia that goes with them.

Nor are these logical consequences to be judged trivial, or ‘merely academic’. On the basis of this angular momentum synthesis it is predicted that a ball of mass 2.5 kg and radius 5 cm, rotating at 2000 revolutions per second (it is just possible to spin such a ball at such a speed without disintegration) would increase or decrease in weight by one hundredth of a gram, depending on the direction of the spin with regard to that of the earth .

So small is this predicted change in weight for such large amounts of spin that it is little wonder that, assuming it exists, it has never been noticed in ordinary macrophysical experience. Experiments to prove this predicted effect have not so far been carried out by our POAMS (Pope-Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis) group for various reasons, not least due to a failure to obtain the necessary funding.

Comparisons with other experiments, such as that carried out by Hideo Hayasaka and his Japanese team at Tokai University , suggest that the changes in weight that they claimed for their disk of 0.175 kg, spinning at 18,000 rpm were not only much larger than we would have expected but were carried out in conditions not ideal for our prediction, which would be at the equator and therefore with an axis parallel (or anti-parallel) to that of the spinning earth.

In the Hayasaka experiments, the disc was not dropped at or near the equator and was not spun in the same direction as the earth's rotation, so our theory predicts that the effects they recorded should have been even smaller than reported. This accords with the findings of Quinn and Picard of the International Bureau of Weights and Measures, who judged that the Hayasaka results were spurious .

It should be mentioned, in passing, that ours is not the first attempt to dispose of Newton’s Law of Universal Gravitation. As is well known, in his General Theory of Relativity, Einstein pointed out that the felt ‘force’ we call ‘gravity’ disappears when we are in free fall and that it can be reproduced in free space by standing on an accelerated surface such as the inside of a centrifuge or a rocket-propelled vehicle. This he called the ‘principle of equivalence’ between the felt force of acceleration and gravitational force. As a consequence, General Relativity

geometrises the vacuum in such a way that when standing on the earth’s surface we are being accelerated upwards at a rate of 32 feet or (9.75 metres) per second per second. Unfortunately for any chance of commonsense understanding, this is difficult to square with the rather obvious fact that the earth remains the same size and is not swelling at anything like that prodigious, exponential rate. Moreover, General Relativity does not, in and of itself, explain the existence of the other in vacuo ‘forces’, such as electrostatic and magnetostatic force which were conceived by Newton’s successors along the same lines as his ‘universal gravitational force’. This left Einstein unable to solve the problem of the unification of the various field-forces, which plagued him for the rest of his life and, in the end, defeated him. This implies no disrespect for his genius. Like the tasks that the gods of the Greek pantheon sometimes set for testing men, this task of solving the problem of the ‘unified field’ was not a real problem but an illusory one, hence impossible to solve. It was what the philosopher Wittgenstein would have called a ‘Scheinproblem’ – not a problem to be solved so much as dissolved..

IV.1 The Historical Route

But why do these bar-code-like patterns form in the way they do? It was not until 1913 that a theoretical explanation of this emerged. This was tendered by Niels Bohr, who based his explanation on a mixture of classical mechanics and electrodynamical theory, plus an ad hoc inclusion of the quantisation of action. This latter had been introduced in 1900 by Max Planck in his formulation of the distribution of the radiation emitted by a blackbody, or perfect absorber of

radiant energy. The lines, Bohr supposed, were energies released by the elementary charged particles called ‘electrons’ orbiting like planets around the nuclear ‘protons’. But, unlike the continuous motions of planets, these electrons changed orbits in discrete quantum jumps of energy e = h, (where  is the frequency of radiation and h is Planck’s constant).

In Bohr’s model, the jumps take place between orbit-levels denoted by the $n$ numbers in the above equation (i.e., $n = 1$ being the lowest orbit, or the one nearest the nucleus, and $n = 2, 3, 4$, … etc. the outer orbit-levels in ascending order). The fixed term $n_1$ then designates the orbit-level from which the electron ‘starts’ its jump and the running term $n_2$ denotes the orbit-level at which it ‘lands’.

The atom thus loses (emits) or gains (absorbs) energy in whole and irreducible quantum amounts of Planck’s constant $h$. Modifications of this classical theory followed in quick succession. Finer structures of spectral lines in the spectrum of hydrogen were discovered and these were explained by Arnold Sommerfeld in terms of elliptical orbits of the electrons.

Then Pieter Zeeman showed that further spectral lines appeared when the sources of the spectrum were placed near a magnet. These newer orbital parameters were given newer quantum numbers, and these were rationalised by Bohr on the basis of three quantum numbers relating to the size, shape and direction of the electron’s orbit.

Transitions between these various orbital states sufficed to explain Sommerfeld’s multiple lines, but they did not explain those extra lines that were produced by magnetism. These were eventually explained by Wolfgang Pauli as due to a hidden rotation, or spin, of the orbiting electron, and this same idea appeared in the work of the two Dutch physicists, George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit.

With these and other theoretical modifications, the full informational contents of spectra were more or less adequately explained on the basis of the hidden motions of these hidden particles. Even the periodic table of the elements which the Russian chemist, Dimitri Mendeleev, had constructed in the 1890s seemed satisfactorily explained on this same basis of invisible, theoretical mechanisms of charged particles.

In this and in other ways, theories burgeoned and things got more and more complicated. For instance, a controversy had raged, since the days of Newton and Huygens, as to whether light consists of particles or waves. The problem with particles is that it seems inconceivable that particles can create interference patterns in the way that Thomas Young had demonstrated in 1801 with a beam of light split in two and then recombined, as in his famous two-slit experiment.

However, other experiments with light, such as the photoelectric effect in which electrons are ejected from the surface of a metal suggested that light consisted of particles. Reverting to Newton’s corpuscular theory of light, Einstein explained this photoelectric effect as due to a kind of ‘bombardment’ by light-corpuscles, which he called ‘photons’.

But why did these particles strike with wave-like characteristics? The answer, it seemed, to Einstein and his follower, Louis de Broglie, was provided by a forcing together of the incommensurable wave and particle theories. And so the photon was conceived to be a ‘wave particle’.

The fact that an entity with such opposite characteristics is inconceivable was dismissed as inconsequential, so physicists went ahead with that concept, on the grounds that it was a ‘fact of nature’, regardless of how commonsensically inconceivable it might be that these photons had the dual nature of particles and waves.

This rejection of commonsense logic and intuition was graced with the name of a principle, the principle of so-called ‘complementarity’. It created the fashion, from then on, of allowing a special dispensation for physicists to think and teach in this completely nonsensical way.

As de Broglie conceived it, this ‘wave-particle duality’ of matter extends throughout the sub-physical world, being associated not only with Einstein’s ‘photons’ but with the motions of particles of any sort, protons, electrons or whatever. These waves de Broglie thought of as ‘pilot waves’.

In atoms, they form stationary, or standing waves, forming harmonic patterns around the nucleus at distances from the centre where the beginning and end of the wave-cycle are perfectly in phase. This concept of the underlying standing-wave was further developed by Erwin Schrödinger who, in January of 1926, introduced into theoretical physics a wave-equation that satisfactorily described all the quantum aspects of physical systems.

Individual states, or manifestations of these physical systems were then regarded as solutions of this underlying wave-function involving the superposition of harmonic states. Later, in the summer of 1926, Max Born subjected Schrödinger’s wave-function to a probability-statistical interpretation, describing it not as a continuous ‘wave’ but as a pure probability amplitude.

In this interpretation, exactness of position and motion of elementary particles had disappeared at the quantum level. All that was left were pure wave-like ‘probability functions’. The traditional notion of a ‘particle’ as a hard material entity was thus fast disappearing, to be replaced by its manifestation in a definite physical state as the ‘collapsing’ of a continuous, all-pervading ‘wave-function’ by the very act of observing it.

And in this way, quantum physics slid further and further into the mystery we now commonly associate with it. In 1927, Heisenberg made another discovery. This was what he called his ‘principle of uncertainty’. Observing the state of a fundamental particle requires an energy-interaction which it, which alters that particle’s state.

So the only way to determine the exact state of the particle would be not to observe or detect it in any way. This means, of course, that so far as observation is concerned, the existence and state of particles on the ultimate quantum level is impossible to describe.

In that case, how can these ultimate ‘particles’ be said to exist? Heisenberg concluded that they can’t, so that particles as such do not exist at the ultimate microphysical level. In fact, with this principle of uncertainty (or ‘indeterminacy principle’ as it is sometimes called) Heisenberg had plumbed the depths of all knowledge of our world, right down to its ultimate epistemological elements.

In the sort of theoretical physics which sees itself as seeking to describe realities ‘behind the veil of perception’, things were getting fraught, to say the very least. Theories began to burgeon at an exponential rate. Anyway, as already stated, to produce a chronicle of the precise details of the historical slide into the current mystique is not our concern here.

V.1 The ‘EPR’ Controversy In the so-called ‘Copenhagen’ interpretation of quantum physics, after Bohr, a particle and its observation form a single angular momentum system. This is based on the principle of the conservation of angular momentum, according to which angular momentum has to be conserved instantly between particles regardless of distance, a condition called ‘non-locality’.

In May, 1935 a paper appeared in Physical Review (Vol. 47, 777-780) written by Einstein in collaboration with Boris Podolski and Nathan Rosen, challenging Bohr and his followers. This

was on the grounds of Einstein’s classical assumption of ‘locality’, or separateness, according to which it is nonsense to expect that measuring the state of a particle at one place is tantamount to determining, immediately, the state of another particle somewhere else, which may be perhaps billions of kilometres distant.

Such an instantaneous ‘action-at-a-distance’ was forbidden by his theory of relativity, according to which (as Einstein and his colleagues conceived it) nothing can travel faster than the speed of light. So something, they judged, had to be wrong, or at least ‘incomplete’, with Bohr’s theory of inseparability, or non-locality.

For thirty years, this clash between Bohr and Einstein, which burdened students of modern physics, continued unresolved, becoming known as the notorious ‘EPR’ (Einstein, Podolski, Rosen) controversy. Then, in 1964, John Bell, of CERN (the European Centre for Research in Geneva), suggested a way of deciding, on experimental grounds, whether or not Bohr’s instantaneous action-at-a-distance, or ‘non-locality’, existed.

These experiments were carried out in 1978, at Berkeley, USA, by John Clauser and his team, also in 1982 in Paris, by Alain Aspect and his group. The results of these experiments were regarded as having proved Bohr’s contention that, despite Einstein’s objections, instantaneous action-at-a-distance really is an underlying fact of nature.

However, despite the heady enthusiasm this created at the time, controversy continues to rage, even now, over what is still known as the ‘EPR paradox’. The ‘paradox’ is that both Bohr’s and Einstein’s positions seem to be confirmed experimentally. But it seems a complete contradiction that, on the one hand, relativistic physics proves that nothing can travel faster than light, whereas on the other hand, quantum physics proves that distant interactions can be instantaneous.

Not even Bohr’s complementarity principle has been able to cope with that. However, what sustains this ‘paradox’ is nothing more than the persistence of Einstein’s classical interpretation of c as ‘the speed of light’. In the Bondian interpretation of c as a plain constant, or distance-time conversion factor, the ‘paradox’ simply vanishes.

V.2 Angular Momentum and Inertia

When we are riding in a bus and the driver suddenly applies his brakes, what throws us forward? Customarily, we think of it as the force of momentum, or inertia. But is this a force? No, there is no force throwing us forward. The force is what’s holding us back.

It is the force exerted by the seat belt, the hanging-strap, the friction of the seat or whatever, which is preventing our bodies from moving smoothly forwards in the way they were before the driver braked. The same applies if we are orbiting freely inside a space vehicle.

When the pilot operates the rocket-motor we are thrown towards the frame of the ship in whichever direction is opposite to that of the motor’s thrust, where some surface or other prevents our bodies from orbiting freely, with respect to the earth, the sun, the stars, etc., in the way they did before the thrust was applied.

It is only in such situations that we can, so to speak, ‘feel’ the presence of those distant bodies. As the physics philosopher, Ernst Mach, is reported to have said, ‘When the subway train jerks it is the fixed stars that throw you down.’ This angular momentum connection with distant bodies is instantaneous in the sense that nothing has to travel at any kind of speed between one’s body and those distant bodies to connect the events of the driver applying his brakes and one’s body being thrown forward.

The two events are absolutely simultaneous. The same applies to those distant bodies in relation to one another. As in the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum physics, the law of conservation of angular momentum demands that any jolt, or change in angular momentum, that any one of these bodies receives instantly affects all the others to some extent or other in the angular momentum nexus of which their correlated orbital motions are an inseparable part.

These angular momentum connections may be described as ‘felt’ connections, insofar as those changes in them which we call ‘inertia’ may be accompanied by the sort of visceral sensations which sometimes cause travel sickness. But how do these felt connections compare with their seen connections?

Suppose we see the jolt that in the one body affects all the others in that instantaneous way. When a piece of the Shoemaker-Levy comet struck Jupiter in July 1994, did our eyes see that event at the same instant that our seismographs might have recorded it?

As a soldier in charge of that gun I may be faced with the decision of whether to let the tower stand or to obey a communicated order to fire on it, killing the enemy but reducing the historical building to rubble.

That decision, either on my part or that of my commanders, is not part of the ‘material’ world, yet it has very definite material consequences, so that the classical separation between ‘mind’ and ‘matter’ no longer applies. Faced, then, with the reality of these sorts of situations, the old philosophical issue of whether or not there is such a propensity as free will becomes academic.

Everyone knows that deliberation, volition and responsibility are realities, in no way reducible to the mechanical motions of atoms. Recognising this is something that distinguishes radically between the old Democritan, atomic approach to science and the new information-based, relationist approach in which the material sciences join seamlessly with the humanities.

And of course, with real responsibility and real decision-making come unavoidable questions of real morality and, in its wake, unavoidable questions of real religion. This is why, in those talks and seminars which have led to this treatise, questions of this kind that have arisen have been altogether natural and refreshingly naïve.

They have not been confined, in the usual stylised and stilted way, within the voodoo chalk circles of academic propriety that are so typical of Physics conferences and publications. What would normally be regarded as separate issues of Science and Theology have therefore been run together most naturally and uninhibitedly, just as and when curiosity directs.

VI.2 The Quantum-Informational logos One of these interesting questions was that of whether or not life, consciousness and its associated spiritual values can evolve. What, the questioner asked, does modern science say about this conflict between creation and evolution? But why, one may also ask, is it so generally assumed that creation and evolution contradict each other when, logically, they don’t? For example, my wife can create a birthday cake by choosing the ingredients and mixing them according to her own recipe. She can then put that mix in the oven and leave it to the purely random processes of heat and chemical action to

evolve that cake in the way she intended for the party. Now is there any contradiction between her intention to create the cake according to her purpose and then trusting its evolution to the eminently random processes that take place inside an oven?

Surely not! So what’s wrong with thinking of a creator purposefully choosing the ingredients for life on this planet and then trusting it to evolve in the ‘oven’ of the sun’s radiation? One need not, as some do, claim to have a personal and intimate knowledge of such a creator.

What is clear to commonsense, without involving religion in any way, is that without some premium placed, at its start, on the evolution of life, it would be as unlikely that life would have appeared in this universe by chance as that a birthday cake could be produced in an oven without the intention of the cook.

Indeed, any idea that the cake, the birthday party and all the extraneous events leading to it were accidental spin-offs of causal atomic mechanisms is plainly ridiculous. Obviously, nothing can evolve where there is no logical possibility of its doing so.

Under no circumstances can a random pile of rubble ever, of itself, evolve into a building, or a billiard ball into a frog. On the other hand, under the right conditions, a chestnut will almost certainly develop into a tree. Anything that evolution produces was therefore always potentially present in the system of things, otherwise nothing whatever would have happened.

The Greek philosopher, Aristotle, called these potentials ‘forms’. Without these forms, he said, nothing can become anything other than whatever it already is. A state of chaos would remain in that state forever. And since there is no information in chaos, then even to think about such a universal state is a contradiction and is therefore an extremely unscientific notion.

Moreover, it is plain that, being prerequisite for evolution, while many forms are accidental, many are ‘teleological’, that is, exhibiting design or purpose in nature. This teleology is an everyday assumption of the life sciences and has steadfastly resisted reduction – other than in pure belief of course – to the laws of classical physics (hence the disparity between these sciences that prevents unification, as commented at the beginning of this book).

A modern replacement for the archaic word ‘teleological’ is function, as used by biologists to describe the obvious presence of a goal-seeking propensity among organisms – that is, forms of constitution and behaviour that are clearly designed for future survival of the organism and its descendants.

How, then, do these forms and substance interrelate? That’s easy. There is no having the one without the other, any more than there can be an inside without an outside or an up without a down. Form and substance are correlative – no form without substance and no substance without form.

But although form and substance cannot be separated, they can be distinguished. Also, a form can be transferred from one substance to another, as in organic reproduction and replication. What’s more, forms can be abstracted. The abstraction and fitting together of pure forms is a propensity of animal foresight.

In man, this leads to logic and mathematics, painting and sculpture, to literature and art generally. An architect anticipates, in his mind and on paper, the form of a building; and a bird no less plainly thinks out how best to solve problems of accessing food and deciding where best to build its nest.

Naturalists provide many glowing examples of this kind of goal-anticipating behaviour right throughout both the animal and vegetable kingdom, which in no way can be explained in terms of classical physical laws. Forms, in this abstracted or purified sense, are what the Greeks called logos, as opposed to physis, which they distinguished as ‘material substance’.

Indeed, ‘forms’, in a completely abstracted, or idealised sense (which Plato had named ‘Ideas’ – a move which Aristotle rejected) have been used as a basis for religion. For instance, in the 13th century the Christian Church deified these Forms, or Ideas, in the concept of a disembodied ‘Logos’, which it identified as the

‘Holy Trinity’. Now whether or not one accepts that particular religious doctrine, the fact remains that modern information-based science is by no means as hostile to the Christian religion – indeed, any religion – as classical science was. In that case, it has to be said that continuing to oppose religion in the name of ‘Science’, nowadays, is simply ‘Old Hat’.

For to postulate the existence of an information-transacting deity, to which one may make supplication (in ‘prayer’ or whatever) is by no means as nonsensical to contemplate in an informational system of things as it was in the traditionally materialistic one.

However, regardless of whether or not a Logos (capital el) of forms can be idealised or personified in that way, the existence of a logos (small el) of forms in a purely secular sense cannot be doubted. An information-based science, then, is fundamentally different from classical science in that it cannot be used as a basis for atheism – other than as a belief that is on a par with the most irrational of religious beliefs and superstitions.

According to classical science everything begins in a random clattering of atoms which somehow, by sheer luck, produces life in all its variety of construction and interlinking of forms. In a science based on laws of probability instead of laws of mechanics, the calculated probability of life evolving in this way from purely random happenings is zero.

The fact, then, that there obviously is life, consciousness and those associated values we call ‘spiritual’, testifies to the built-in probability that all this would sooner or later emerge, on the basis that given enough time, anything that can happen will.

These probabilities that modern science calls ‘quantum potentials’ are virtually the forms of Aristotle. These (secular) forms, or potentials are, of course, not linked in any mechanical or in any other causally determined way. In a logical argument, for instance, the sequence of antecedent to consequence, as signified by the word ‘ergo’ (or ‘therefore’) is not a time-sequence as it is in material processes. Unlike ordinary cause-effect sequences,

abstract logical antecedent-consequent relations – which, as we have seen, may precipitate material effects – can proceed either forward or backwards in our minds, which means that they can be retrospectively reviewed and changed without any of the absurdities that this would imply regarding temporal processes. In other words, unlike the category of material substance, the category of forms, or logos, as abstracted in human reason, is not a ‘universe’ but a pluriverse of independent and often competing processes, involving agents with wills and intentions of their own. And the knock-on effect of this can be physically real, as we have seen in the example of the tower. But if instead of a universe we have a pluriverse, and instead of a continuum a discretum, then in what sense can we describe this information-based approach as ‘holistic’, as resembling a hologram? The word ‘hologram’ derives from the Greek, holos, meaning complete, or whole. So in what sense, then, can this informational discretum be suitably described as a hologram? The answer is that insofar as a hologram is a whole, comprised of variously interacting parts and of which there are various independent and relative perspectives, it provides an appropriate model of nature as modern relativistic quantum physics describes it. This wholeness of nature lies in its principles of overall conservation, as applied to measures of angular momentum, action, and so on, according to which these quantities can neither emerge from nothing nor lapse into nothing. However, while it is true to say that these quantities are ‘universally’ conserved, we must remember that ‘universal’ has two meanings. For instance, one may say that angular momentum is conserved in every action that takes place, so that when a pair of atoms interact, the one atom loses a quantum of angular momentum and the other gains precisely that same amount. In that case, in the system involving just those two atoms, the

angular momentum is absolutely conserved. And if this is the case with every other atomic interaction that takes place, then one might say that angular momentum is conserved ‘universally’ according to one meaning of that word, namely, ‘applicable to all cases’.

However, that is not the same as saying that the angular momentum is conserved simultaneously between all atoms everywhere, according to the other definition of ‘universal’, meaning ‘present or occurring everywhere simultaneously’. If that were the case, then for one atom to interact with another would be to interact with all atoms everywhere.

To move one thing would be to move everything, the consequence of which would be an Eleatic stasis . This distinction is subtle but significant. In saying that angular momentum is conserved ‘universally’ we do not mean that it is conserved ‘monolithically’, or everywhere at the same instant.

We mean that angular momentum is conserved in every single case of atomic interaction, which is not quite the same thing (such being the nuances of the language we are constrained to use). The same goes for the wholeness of a hologram, all parts of which are connected individually without necessarily being seen as a whole.

But of course, in a hologram as ordinarily conceived, there is an overall design to the system, hence a designer, an overseer, a creator or ‘presiding intelligence’ which is outside the system. (a Deus ex Logica rather than a Deus ex Machina) Now whether there is the same sort of overall designer and ‘presiding intelligence’ in the World at large is a question that, in seminars on this subject, has been raised in that same uninhibitedly philosophical and logical, non-academic manner that has been described.

Some feel that the notion of an overall cosmic will and intelligence is inconsistent with any idea of moral freedom and autonomy. Others prefer the idea of a bifurcation of intelligences into good and evil, personified in Christian theology as ‘God versus Satan’.

Others again prefer to think of these evaluations and personifications as no more than conventionalised reflections of human likes and dislikes. There is, they say, no overall presiding intelligence but merely a conflict of wills and intentions between multifarious agencies, in a Darwinian ‘survival-of-the-fittest’ scenario.

But be that as it may, the revolutionary trends in modern physics, away from mechanistic, or atomic, ways of thinking towards these more holistic and relativistic, information-based modes puts an altogether different complexion on things. It places these humane, moral and spiritual issues – or, rather, replaces them – on a different footing from that of those long eras when human values remained in conflict with mechanistic science, the conflict which has undoubtedly led to the morally confused society we have today.

With that old source of antagonism between ‘mental’ and ‘material’ finally removed in favour of a commonsense-logical merger of science and the humanities, perhaps the current wildest excesses of irrationality in both sectors may be curbed. We end at this stage by saying that although it is the aim of this present small volume to explore the logical implications of the holistic paradigm as far as logic takes us, we should remember that, as has well been said, there are truths that without being contrary to logic are nevertheless beyond logic.

As Hamlet says, in Shakespeare’s play: ‘There are more things ‘twixt heaven and earth, Horatio, than are dreamed of in your philosophy.’ Perhaps, then, the best we can expect of even the most meticulously logical argument, is that although logic can never be taken as an oracle of truth, at least it is the only thing that can be safely relied upon to eliminate error. And what else but to eliminate error can be the common interest of society in all sectors of natural science and human reason?

The ‘Naked Emperor’

Something which it is all too easy for a theoretical physicist or cosmologist to do is to draw, on a blackboard, say, a large circle or square to represent ‘the universe’. In this enclosed domain, the audience, like the theoretician, can fancy that they are some kind of ‘God’ who can see everything all over, everywhere within those chalk boundaries.

The theoretician may then produce mathematical equations, working inwards from that all-encompassing figure towards some sort of logical and/or mathematical ‘explanation’ of what he presents as a true scientific representation of ‘the universe’. But how can that possibly represent ‘the universe’.

To be truly a universe, that chalk boundary would have to include the man with the chalk, the audience, the room … in fact everything in the world at large. But, of course, it doesn’t. So in no way can that chalked enclosure represent – or even resemble – a universe.

Nevertheless, theoreticians, in their minds, if not on blackboards, draw some sort of boundary which, they fancy, encompasses everything and then work inwards from there as though solving an algorithm . Well, alright, so working in that way, in what has been called the hypothetico-deductive method has, in the past, definitely proved useful to science and, especially, to technology.

Unfortunately, as science has advanced, these hypothetical ‘universes’ have proliferated to an extent that, in the place of nature, students are confronted by an educational curriculum in which it is de rigueur for them, in order to graduate, to satisfy their examiners that they have studied as many as possible of these revered theoretical precedents.

All their studies are therefore ‘focussed’ towards conventional scholarship before they are allowed the privilege of examining nature for themselves. But by then, any innocent curiosity they might have had for studying nature and thinking about it for themselves may well have been all but completely sidelined by a concern for seeking scholarly excellence, according to the arbitrary standards of a competition-orientated Educational system.

However, to study the ideas of famous people is not the same thing as studying the world of nature. So, despite its early successes, especially in areas of technology, the emphasis in modern Physical Science has become so over-weighted in favour of theory that the subject has now virtually turned turtle, leaving not only its student passengers but also its crew of teachers floundering.

Fewer and fewer students are now attracted to the subject, and departments of Physics are being shut down all over, so that universities are directed towards funding ‘more sensible’ and ‘cost-effective’ subjects, such as Business studies, Management and Computer Science.

In more and more universities, even Media Studies and Sports Science have ousted Theoretical Physics. Why has all this happened? Our analysis has been that it is mainly because two of our most revered scientists, Newton and Einstein, each with his own overplus of theoretical genius, made some very big mistakes.

Newton’s was to declare, in the face of all observations to the contrary, that natural force-free motion takes place in straight lines; and the plain fact that they don’t was then explained as due to an invisible, universal in vacuo field-force called ‘gravity’.

But why did Newton give us those two laws when only one would do? And why did so many generations of students not notice that angular momentum is sufficient in itself to explain the

orbital nature of free-moving bodies without having to create these metaphysical ‘force-fields’? The answer is that they were not encouraged to think for themselves but to keep their eyes reverentially glued to the writings of these great intellectuals.

So, what was Einstein’s comparable Big Mistake? It was to follow in the theoretical tracks of Newton, whose theory of a ‘gravitational field’ had been added to by similar theories of electrostatic and magnetostatic ‘fields’. This made him think of Römer’s discovery of the constant relation $c$ between units of metres and seconds as ‘the speed of light in a vacuum’.

The fact that his Theory of Relativity, which was based on that assumption, removed any question of the existence of a medium for that conveyance then created the theoretical convolutions and conundrums that have become modern Theoretical Physics, which, in order to save face with a reverential public, boldly demanded a complete and authoritative abandonment of commonsense logic.

The Emperor was plainly naked, but who was going to admit it? So, what does nature look like when we admit the illusoriness of all this metaphysical regalia? It takes us right back to where modern science became ‘pear-shaped’. Relativity can be derived from plain observation, without dressing it up with Newton’s metaphysic of invisible ‘fields’ and without its associated interpretation of Römer’s constant $c$ as the invisible ‘velocity of light in a vacuum’.

Seeing quantum physics bared of this same sort of ‘field’ metaphysic, together with its associated notion of invisible ‘waves’ leaves us, in a similar way, with the plain observational fact of the ultimate discreteness of the informational elements from which we obtain our knowledge of the world.

Newton once said: ‘If I have seen further, it is from standing on the shoulders of the Giants.’ That may well have been the case, at that time. However, now that our views are obscured on all sides by so many of these intellectual giants, all standing on one another’s shoulders, perhaps our best recourse, in glimpsing nature as it really is would be to escape, on our knees, between these gigantic legs. This, in effect is what we have tried to do in those extramural talks and seminars which have led to the production of this monograph.

VIII. Summary

The essence of any true understanding of relativity lies in the radical shift from thinking of nature in the way that an all-seeing presence might be presumed to see it towards thinking of it as a vast manifold of finite observational, point-centred perspectives. Science is no mystically inspired ‘God’s-eye-view’ of things. All our knowledge of the world is obtained via our senses and instrument-systems. And this knowledge is what we construct by means of our faculty of reason, in communication with other observers and their instrument-systems. This is all focussed inside our own heads, not something ‘hanging in the sky’. Science is an essentially human undertaking, expressed in human language and processed by human logic and human mathematics. Losing sight of this fundamental epistemological principle has led classical physicists and cosmologists to assume that, somehow, they were studying ‘the universe’ when, in fact, they were no more than studying their own sensations and ideas. Relativity disabuses us of that long-inculcated, ‘God’s-eye-view’ presumption of classical science. For some of us this loss can be traumatic. We are so used to thinking of the

time by which we live our lives as the same everywhere. We never actually say it, but the tacit assumption is that the time by which we measure our living-times, between our birthdays, between our heartbeats, our cell-division rates and so on – our age, in effect – is the same everywhere, not just throughout our planet but also throughout the stars and the whole galactic beyond.

But what warrant did we ever have for such an assumption? Time is what we measure with clocks. What else can it possibly be? Normally, we don’t think of these clock-rates as varying with motion, but Römer showed that they do. He discovered that as clocks travel away from or towards us (in his case, the clock-movement being that of Jupiter’s satellite) they lose or gain time in the ratio of one second for every three-hundred million metres.

All clocks do the same, although, until Römer, no-one had noticed this. Einstein was the first to realise what this meant for physical measures in general. Time is one of the basic physical dimensions, and so are length and mass. Moreover, these measures are all inter-dependent in defining the existence of a physical object.

So if time varies observationally, then so do those other dimensions of physical objects. This relativised our whole conception of matter and material objectivity. (Undoubtedly, this is why so many people have a phobia against accepting the theory of relativity, because they feel, deep down, that this ‘loss of objectivity’ in science would be traumatic.)

However, as we have seen, there is no real loss of objectivity implied by relativity. More serious as a barrier to accepting relativity is an amazing mistake made by Einstein. Having established, mathematically, the relativity of length, time and mass, he over-interpreted Römer’s length-time constant $c$ as ‘the speed of light in space’.

So light, which is the information from which all our knowledge of space and time stems was made to travel in that very same space and time that is projected out of it. In other words, space-time was made to travel in itself, which is logically incoherent – a fallacy, like saying ‘Whiteness is white’.

If only Einstein had settled for accepting c as an pure observational constant, modern science would have been disabused of its classical, God’s-eye-view presumption of ubiquity and re-established, cleanly and clearly on a truly relativistic, observational basis .

The result would undoubtedly have been socially traumatic, as all revolutions inevitably are, but at least we would have known where we stand. We would have been relieved of all the tortuous, mind-bending jiggery-pokery that goes with ‘relativity’ as now commonly understood. ‘Relative’ would have simply meant what it always meant: ‘relative to’, or ‘in relation to’, as opposed to ‘absolute’, or ‘self-sufficient’.

The conceptual switch from absolutism to observationism made by Einstein’s mentor, Mach, in the phenomenalist tradition of Berkeley, Hume and Kant, would then have been achieved. Light would have been observational information, nothing more nor less.

Space would be what we project out of that information, and so would time, with all other physical dimensions and measures following suit. Science would eventually have come ‘down to earth’ from its theoretical realms apart, to become re-united with commonsense and the humanities generally.

The ‘Great Machine’ of classical physics would have disappeared and been replaced by an Informational System answering more to the description of a vast computer or hologram. The classical ‘Universe’ would have been replaced by a Manifold of interacting and intercommunicating agents, and any overall intelligence would have to be conceived as an immanent Communicator rather than a transcendent Machine-Designer. Any such superhuman agency would have to work in and through Man and his fellow organisms. As Voltaire wrote :

God is present everywhere … but everywhere where there is something, not where there is not. To

be present in nothing seems to me a contradiction in terms, an absurdity. … I will not set up the throne of God in imaginary spaces.

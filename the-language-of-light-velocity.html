The Language of 'Light Velocity'

N. Vivian Pope — ANPA 16, Wesley College, Cambridge, September 1994
Transcript of a talk and discussion. The subject was to have been 'The Lingering Death of Western Dualism'. However, due to an exigency to be described, that talk, delivered extempore, involved much discussion and is now re-titled as above.

[Note: This is a transcript of a live conference talk with extensive audience discussion. The original document suffered severe OCR corruption. What follows is reconstructed from the recoverable portions of the text.]

Opening Remarks

Viv Pope: Ladies and Gentlemen — surprising how few ladies, in fact, are here! [He stands before the rostrum, takes the paper out of his briefcase and, as he prepares to read it, suddenly crumples it and tosses it aside.]

'To hell with it! I've just come from London after reading a paper on this subject at Imperial College, and I have to confess that it has made me feel very angry. I had a wild thought, just now, that I might avail myself of the precedent set by our founder-member here, the illustrious Professor Kilmister, for relaxing into the vernacular in order to describe that anger and the reason for it. But I have talked to too many nice people over coffee and my intentions in that respect have softened somewhat.'

'Those of you who have heard me before know that my sole contribution to science is a logical one and it is dead simple. What I say is that to talk of the constant $c$ as the "speed of light in space" is logically unnecessary, and that removing that unnecessarily loaded interpretation of $c$ from relativistic physics language removes all of the notorious mysteries and paradoxes of modern physics, lock, stock and barrel. That's all I say, and I'll say it again and again till it sinks in, or until I fall off my perch — whichever comes first.'

'What happened at Imperial College is this. After I had said what I just said, and the other speakers had said their pieces, a young man from Cambridge got up and said that what I was saying about the "speed of light" was all very well, but what about this, what about that? And in each case, the "this" and the "that" was something which involved the "speed of light" interpretation of $c$.'

'In other words, having accepted my argument that you don't need the "speed of light" he was trying to refute that argument by shoving back all the "speed of light" paraphernalia which my argument had removed. And this fellow wasn't just any old fellow, either! He was some kind of doctor of something or other, with a string of degrees from Cambridge.'

The Central Argument

'Look, as I have demonstrated here at ANPA, perhaps ad nauseam, this is all "relativity" is.' [Draws diagram — Figure 2.]

'As I say, this is all Relativity is! It's child's play! It was tried out on an average fourth-form and the teachers reported that everybody got it. This [indicating the vertical side of the triangle] is the observed distance a body moves in space. That [the base of the triangle] is the time you read off the body. And that [the hypotenuse] is the time-dilation. End of story! That's no more and no less than what relativity says. If you link time and space by the scale-constant $c$ — thinking of it as no more than a conversion factor — then it follows that if you travel in space you travel in time.'

'That diagram is perfectly simple. All you need in order to measure that time-dilation is a ruler. Otherwise, if you want to be mathematical about it, you can use the theorem of Pythagoras. Einstein's formula, the barbarously entitled "Lorentz transformation", can then be deduced from the Pythagorean equation for the hypotenuse of that triangle in a few lines of simple algebra. But doing it the other, the standard, way around has made a tremendous mess of this! You try to draw that diagram in that mode and, boy, you get some complicated and puzzling drawings! Unnecessary!'

'So all I'm saying is, if you insist that light travels in space, that it shines in space, if you think like that and talk like that, that between here and there [points on the paper] … You have, say, an "information-wave". Now, what the hell is an information wave when it's between there and there? That's a metaphysical line — a null geodesic — that I've now drawn there between the two points. How can information, as such, travel in a vacuum, far less a metaphysical one? A Schrödinger probability wave-function then? But how the hell can a "probability-function" — or, indeed, a wave of anything — travel in a vacuum? What is it when it's on its way between the one place and the other, travelling at the "speed of light"? It's utter nonsense! There is no way, no way at all, in which you can make philosophical sense of language like that!'

Discussion

Questioner: 'Suddenly, you have a sort of discontinuity. As soon as the velocity is actually $c$ then you mustn't talk about travelling. I mean, doesn't it worry you that there is this peculiar discontinuity in description?'

Pope: 'No, it doesn't. There is no discontinuity because you can never reach $c$.'

Manthey: 'You've used half an hour. I want to ask a question. It has always made sense to me that one can take this argument about the speed of light and say, actually, well, it has to do with creating conceptually consistent descriptions. You want to introduce this idea of a maximum velocity for the transfer of information in order that you do not introduce causal inconsistencies in any frame of reference. And then you get out of this whole business of things travelling in space … Really, what you are doing is introducing a conceptual limitation which, in certain respects, looks like the speed of something travelling through space, and in other respects looks like a conceptual requirement to maintain the consistency of separate events. I have no problem with that!'

Pope: 'Sure! Look, the other day, my grandson asked me how does a radio work. I gave the usual simple explanation of a transmitter there and a receiver here … and so on and so forth, in the way I was taught as a communications engineer. I mean, I learned all that stuff. Maxwell's left-hand rule, the lag and lead of a current, the bumpety-bump model of how dielectric displacement-currents are propagated in vacuo, via media — the whole damned thing. I earned my bread by it! So, yes, you can do that! But if you're in a society where everybody starts off counting horses by counting their legs and tails and dividing by five, then you can always get ways of adjusting that to deal with odd occurrences of horses whose tails have been docked, or who have legs missing or whatever. Those are "paradoxes" of the system. So you say, well, okay; and you generate new theories to take care of them. But at the end of the day, two thousand years of that would get you into the state we're in now!'

Pope: 'In this organisation, ANPA, therefore, I would be much happier to be a member if it were consistent; that is, if we were to eschew references such as yours to the "Big Bang", to "light-velocity", "fields", "energy-waves", "gravity-waves" and all those other continuistic conceptions members talk about. These things simply scramble my mind, and my aim as a professional ignoramus is to analyse that perplexity. I'm not in competition, here, with anyone to write papers on erudite physics or metaphysics, of that speculative sort presented by members of this or any other society!'

'Is that quite clear? I simply cannot understand why we have to remain committed to the continuistic "speed of light" conception. It's not just a question of usefulness (pragmatism) because if you're committed to talking about the "speed of light" you are also committed — as I say, syllogistically — to adopting a localistic view. Then you are lumbered with things like the "EPR" or action-at-a-distance paradox, the paradoxical instantaneity of gravitation and inertia.'

Noyes: 'I'd like to point out a way of thinking about the velocity of light which I think is useful. If you have an event happening here and an event happening there, then in order to talk about a velocity between those two events you have to have a clock here and a clock there and you have to know how those clocks are related to each other. Once you've related the clocks in the way Einstein and Bridgman tell you to do it, then you're going to get the formulas of relativity. And in that it is important to think about the signal going at the velocity of light, when you are using that rather than a material particle. But then you get a potential experiment that you can do, which is to determine whether or not you can transfer information at greater than the speed of light. You can perform such an experiment, and so far, nobody has succeeded — but that doesn't mean one could not succeed — in sending information at greater than the speed of light. If you can do that, if you can make what Redhead has called the "Bell telephone", that is, if you can use the Aspect-experiment type of set-up to transfer information greater than the speed of light, then one could recognise by using that language that one had done so. There are experiments which, if that happened, would tell you that this concept of the speed of light not only is meaningful but demonstrated.'

Pope: 'Can I just answer that, because we're running out of time. Experimental information that's not yet in can't be brought into the equation, can it? But I'm not talking about experiments. I'm talking about the meaning of the "speed of light".'

Pope: 'Look, I think that what Keith says is right. But, perhaps, not all the time — not when you're speaking to your grandmother, for instance. But certainly I'm with you insofar as when we are here at ANPA we should stick to this discretist language-game. Otherwise, if you use the light-velocity language it creates nonsense in the context of what we are doing. I'm simply suggesting that we should clean up our act, that is, that we tidy up our language, making sure that it makes sense, that we're not mixing up continuistic concepts with discontinuistic concepts and just chucking them around ad lib.'

Noyes: 'I'd like to recommend Viv's point of view in the following way. It leads you to an interesting question. The way I usually put it is, if you're thinking about a photon going from here to there in the way that I was just recommending, it is a peculiarity of a massless quantum that it can only have two values of angular momentum, call it spin — and those two values are $+\hbar$ and $-\hbar$. That is, a photon either has angular momentum $+\hbar$ (right-handed) or $-\hbar$ (left-handed). Now Viv thinks of $c$ as a conversion factor, and his basic unit of angular momentum is $\hbar$. If you put those two things together, which is what Viv does in his theory, I think there are interesting physical consequences. So, all I wanted to say is, let's not dismiss Viv's programme out of hand, because there are potentially interesting physical consequences of his point of view.' [Applause]

Pope: 'Thank you, Pierre! The points I have made, then, may be summarised as follows. In ANPA, which is committed to a discretist philosophy, the distinction between continuous and discrete descriptions of nature is paramount. The "speed of light" interpretation of $c$ is inherently continuistic. It implies continuous propagation of something through continuous space. This is inconsistent with the discretist, quantum-informational foundations which ANPA professes. The alternative interpretation of $c$ as a pure dimensional conversion factor is both simpler and more consistent with ANPA's philosophical commitments. I commend it to you.' ■

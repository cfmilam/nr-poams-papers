REFRACTION in optic fibres

N. Vivian Pope

OUT-TAKE (from Proof 6) on Refraction in Optic Fibres

3. The ‘speed of light’ in optical fibres. This raises the question as to whether the same effect we have described in relation to the electric wire might also take place in an optic fibre. Now it is well known that according to standard relativity, all processes in a system travelling at a speed v relative to the observer are time-dilated, or slowed, in comparison with the observer’s local time. An electrical conductor or an optic fire is such a system in which, as Feynman has said, ‘everything is jiggling’. The difference is that in the electrical conductor the electrons involved in the current ‘jiggle’ at very slow speeds as compared with those in optic fibre, in whose atoms the electrons vibrate at frequencies consistent with the relayed light-energy. [1] From this it follows that if c is the distance-time rate of light-signals in vacuo and c1 the rate of light/electrical signals in the medium, then the ratio of the slower process of lightconduction in the medium to that in the vacuum is: c1/c = tR/tR1 where tR and tR1 are observer times according to the time-dilation equation (1), namely,

(2),

$$t_R = \sqrt{(s/c)^2 + t^2} \quad \text{(1)}$$

(1)

. It is scarcely coincidental that this ratio is the same as the refractive index, μ, ordinarily given as $\mu = \sin i / \sin r = c/c_1$, where c is, again, in conventional language, the ‘speed in the vacuum’, and c1 is comparative ‘speed in the medium'. Now in an electrical conductor the vibration speeds of the (unbound, or ‘free’) electrons are, as we have seen, extremely small, so that according to the usual relativistic equation, where $t_R = s/c$, we have $t_{R1} \approx t_R$, whence, according to (1), $c_1 \approx c$. This answers to the ‘speed of electricity’ in an electrical conductor, which is said to be c (ref. FN 3.). By contrast, in an optic fibre, the vibrations of the (bound) electrons are very large, depending on the density of the medium and the frequency of the incident light, this 'speed' being upwards of 106 metres per second. In that case, the fibre can be likened to a system moving as a whole relatively to the observer at an effective (rms) speed v, due to which the dilated time tR1 is greater than tR, (where tR is, again, the minimum time s/c according to the Pythagorean timeequation, (1). In consequence, for the fibre, $c_1$ is smaller than $c$ [2].

‘Vibrating’, in this context (or ‘jiggling’), means the bound electron jumping from one quantum level in that atom to a higher level and then reverting to its original level in a relaying sequence throughout the medium. The energy of these vibrations, in the fibre, is many orders of magnitude greater than those in the copper wire.

This relation between refractive index and the speed of the moving medium was established by Armand Fizeau, in 1859. In this experiment a transparent medium (i.e., water) was made to flow at great speed around a u-tube. It was discovered, in effect, that the refractive index of the liquid increased with its speed v. In other words, the ‘speed’c1 of the transport of light-energy in the liquid increased in the direction of the flow but in diminishing amounts with the increase in v precisely in accordance with the (later) relativistic time-dilation formula. That is to say, the process of light conduction, like all other processes in the medium, was time-dilated relatively to the observer.

In an optic fibre, then, the ‘speed’ of energy conduction is predictably ‘slower’ than that of ‘electricity’ in a wire. In the wire the energy is transported at virtually the distancetime rate c, while in the fibre it is conducted at the refracted rate c1.

Otherwise, the conduction in the fibre and in the wire are, in principle, the same; that is, they are both conduits for disembodied energy. This follows from the Newtonian mass-energy equation $e = \frac{1}{2}mV^2$, where V is the classical, or proper-time velocity (in relativity, the relative velocity v multiplied by $\sqrt{1 - (v^2/c^2)}$].

In other words, if m in the energy equation is zero, then $V = \sqrt{2e/m}$ is infinite, which, for any source-energy e, makes the relative velocity in the vacuum $v = c$, and, in the medium, $v_1 = c_1$ (see above). It is a moot point, then, as to whether or not in the case of light, a fibre-optic circuit might yield results consistent with those of our electrical example.

What is certain, however, is that in the electrical example, for those reasons already discussed, the constant c cannot, logically, be interpreted as a ‘speed, and it is difficult to see how that same constant c can be a ‘speed’ in one instance and a non-speed in another.

Both light and ‘electricity’ are disembodied energies (i.e., of zero mass), whence their behaviours in respect of their distancetime rates of conduction are, in essence the same but for the difference in degree, for refracted light. But, of course, this does not mean that 'electricity’ can be identified as a form of light.

An electric current cannot flow without a completed circuit, whereas light flows in a fibre towards an open end. The reason for this particular difference is not clear, but that does not logically contradict our conclusion that both electricity in a wire and light-in-vacuo are conducted at the same rate c, hence, to prove that c is not a ‘speed’ in the one case is to prove also that it is not a ‘speed’ in the other.

Experiments with optic-fibre circuits are, of course, not so easily carried out as the very simple one with the battery, switch etc., described in Figure 2. However, on the basis of our ‘electrical’ demonstration, plus what is observed in the Thomas Young two-slit experiment, we may predict that what we have called ‘teleblocking’ of the signal would be the same for electricity as for light.

In the wire, the teleblocking is achieved by the use of a circuit-disconnecting switch. In the fibre it would have to be by means of some interference condition similar to what occurs in the two-slit phenomenon. This seems feasible in principle, with the only proviso being the purely technical one of containing the energy within the fibre to an extent similar to that of an electrical wire.

In experiments carried out, so far, by this author and a colleague, a failure to ensure this latter condition has made the results inconclusive [3]. However, this does not annul either the practical result of the ‘electrical’ experiment or the logic of the argument arising from it, that by its very nature, the constant c, in whatever context it appears, cannot be a ‘velocity’. _________________

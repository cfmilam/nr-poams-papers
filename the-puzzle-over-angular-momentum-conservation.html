The Puzzle over Angular Momentum Conservation

The Puzzle over Angular Momentum Conservation

Viv Pope

I'm writing this mostly for my own benefit, just to get things right in my own head about conservation of angular momentum. My contention, which has been challenged on 'mathematical' grounds, is that conservation of angular momentum means the conservation of both direction (vector) and magnitude (amount). This seems to be standard among those sources I have read and consulted. What is not standard, however, is my further contention that the total angular momentum of a freely orbiting body includes that of both orbit and spin, so that for a constant amount of angular momentum the more there is of spin angular momentum the less there has to be of orbital angular momentum. If this is correct, then it neatly solves the Pioneer Anomaly in which NASA's spinning space-probes orbit nearer to the sun than is to be expected in terms of Newtonian 'gravity'. This contention has met with all sorts of theoretical objections, of which I am gravely suspicious, and when I get suspicious of these sorts of arguments, I tend to fall back on pure practical, hands-on experiment.

The apparatus:

Now I happen to have, in my possession, a table-top executive toy which demonstrates quite clearly that the more there is of spin angular momentum the less there has to be of orbital angular momentum. In this toy, the angular momentum is supplied to a pendulum-type of array (a ring) to which are attached, diametrically opposite to each other, two bearings between which is a spindle. In the centre of this spindle is fixed, at right-angles to it, an arm on the opposite ends of which are balanced a pair of porpoise-shaped weights which are free to swing or rotate in a plane fixed at right-angles to that of the pendulum (see picture below).

This means that the pendulum (ring) and spin (porpoise) vectors of motion are permanently at right-angles to each other and therefore sum to zero, throughout. Any change in the amplitude of the swing of the pendulum due to the addition of spin to the porpoises must therefore be due solely to the amount, or magnitude, of the angular momentum, since it cannot be due to any vectorial effect.

The angular momentum is supplied to the pendulum by a standard nine-volt battery in the base of the apparatus, providing magnetic pulses to maintain the angular momentum against losses due to friction. This is via a small electromagnet fixed to the underside of the ring (the pendulum). The amplitude of the pendulum swing (the ring) is measured against a pointer fixed at eye-level in the background (not shown).

The result:

At first, the amplitude of the swing of the pendulum is measured with the transverse beam (the porpoises in the photo) prevented from rotating by tying it to the ring with a fine cotton thread. In this case, the amplitude of the pendulum-swing is seen to remain steady. Next, the swing of the pendulum is measured with the string cut, allowing the porpoises to rotate freely, as they do spasmodically and at random. In this case, the amplitude of the pendulum swing is observed to vary. After observing for a while it becomes clear that whenever the transverse beam with the porpoises rotates, the amplitude of the pendulum swing is reduced and when the beam with the porpoises does not rotate, the pendulum swing is at maximum. This result was witnessed and unanimously concurred by others, independently of one another.

This proves my contention that the amount of spin angular momentum decreases the amount of orbital angular momentum, thereby explaining the anomaly of the spinning Pioneer space-probes. QED.

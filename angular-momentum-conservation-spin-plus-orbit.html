Angular momentum conservation SPIN PLUS ORBIT

N. Vivian Pope

1. Angular momentum, a conserved quantity Any physical measure divided by a time is a rate, a rate of change in that measure. For example, a unit amount of electric charge, called a coulomb, is a collection of $6.29 \times 10^{18}$ static electrons. That amount divided by a time in seconds – i.e., coulombs per second – is the rate of flow of electric current called an ampere (amp). By the same token, a rate times a time is an amount, or quantity. Action is such a quantity, namely, the product of energy and time. This is why the fundamental unit of action, Planck’s constant, h is called the quantum. That quantum of action divided by 2 is known as the quantum of angular momentum which, since action is conserved, is also a conserved quantity. The same applies to angular momentum on the macrolevel, where like action, angular momentum can change only by the addition or subtraction of amounts of the same kind, On its own, its magnitude cannot change, but is conserved, which is what is meant by the Law of the Conservation of Angular Momentum. Angular momentum is also a vector quantity, which means that it has direction of motion as well as magnitude. When a gyroscope is set spinning in space, then barring any outside influences on that gyroscope ,its direction of spin in the plane of its rotation remains the same no matter how far that gyroscope may travel. This is clearly seen with the NASA space-probes, which, throughout their journey through the solar system, retain both the amount and direction of spin imparted to them at launch, It is this conservation of direction, of course which keeps their antennae pointed towards earth. So the Law of Angular Momentum Conservation applies to both the magnitude and the spin-direction (vector orientation) of a body in space. However, like the quantity, or magnitude. of angular momentum, its vectors can also be added and subtracted. Sit in a swivel chair and swing your legs to the left, and your body rotates to the right; swing your legs to the right and your body rotates to the ,left. Swing your legs up and down and nothing happens. This simple demonstration shows how rotation vectors add and subtract without changing the magnitude of the overall angular momentum. With the angular momentum of the chair plus the sitter being zero at the start, the sum of those angular momenta, of the swinging legs and the sitter, respectively, remains zero throughout. This has profound consequences for the orbits of spinning bodies, such as space-probes, planets and galaxies. The ’Pioneer and ‘missing mass’ anomalies. So it is to the amount, or magnitude of angular momentum that the conservation law applies. As applied to vectors, it simply means the conservation of direction of rotation of the various bodies in an angular momentum system. Take, for instance, the case of, say, a NASA space-probe orbiting the sun, According to the conservation law, both the amount and direction of rotation imparted to the probe at launch stays the same throughout its motion thereafter. However, the same conservation law demands that the rotations of the probe and the sun form a single, overall-conserved angular momentum system. This, of course, includes the angular momenta of both orbit and spin. This is where the principle of the composition of vectors comes into play, because the rotation vectors of the two bodies – the sun and the probe, in this case – add or subtract according to their respective orientations. For instance, neglecting the spin of the sun, when the

orbital and spin vectors of the probe are in the same plane and in the same direction – say, clockwise – they add, with the result that the spinning probe orbits the sun faster than if it were not spinning. Conversely, when the spin and orbit vectors of the probe are opposite, the spinning probe orbits more slowly than it otherwise would. And if the probe spins in a direction at right-angles to the plane of the orbit, then, like raising and lowering the legs in the swivel-chair example there is no effect on the speed of the orbit. (Note that in all these instances, unlike the swing of the legs, the spin motion, in whatever direction, is continuous, otherwise the effects if the swivel-chair and the orbiting body are the same.) Now, for a constant (i.e.. conserved) angular momentum $L = mvr$ , any change in the orbital velocity v of a body of mass m must, obviously, change the length. of the radius, r. So when the spin and orbital vectors add, increasing the orbital velocity v in the manner explained above, the orbit radius shrinks, and the spinning probe orbits nearer to the sun; and when the vectors subtract, the velocity decreases and the probe orbits further from the sun than if it were not spinning. This is as explained in their book Light-Speed, Gravitation and Quantum Instantaneity, by Osborne and Pope, Chapter 7, page 133 .[fn]. If what these writers say is true, then when the NASA space-probes spin in a plane at right-angles to that of the orbit – as, in fact, they almost do – then there can be no change in the orbit velocity v, hence no change in the orbit radius r. However, what these writers neglect to consider is that even when the sum of the vectors is at its absolute minimum, the fact that the magnitude of the probe’s spin angular momentum remains the same means that regardless of vector additions or subtractions the orbit radius us decreased. This, of course, is because for the same constant angular momentum, L, the more there is of spin angular momentum the less there has to be of orbital angular momentum. This applies to any orbiting body that is spinning, be it a space-probe, a planet in our solar system or a star in a galaxy – or even galaxies orbiting one another. The effect of the spin is a contraction in the orbit radius in all cases. hence a compaction of bodies in the case of a spiral galaxy. But now there is an anomaly. The ‘gravitational’ mechanics of Newton, which NASA employs for calculating the trajectories of its space-probes takes little or no account of the spins of orbiting bodies. So far as Newton was concerned, the ‘gravitational attraction’ between bodies is on account of their mass alone, so that the spins of bodies are no part of the orbital equation – or, at least, any extent to which the orbital mechanics of Newton includes the effect of spin, as claimed by some apologists for Newton, is so miniscule as to be entirely negligible in comparison with those effects predicted in terms of overall angular momentum conservation. This neglect of spin in orbiting bodies is undoubtedly what has caused those anomalies in the physics which NASA uses to calculate the trajectories of their probes, and which cosmologists employ in calculating the ‘total mass of the universe’. This is what has prompted NASA’s spokesman, Jon Anderson, to say what he did about the need for a ‘New Physics’. Such a ‘New Physics’ would simply include the neglected spin angular momentum together with the orbital angular momentum in the total amount of angular momentum of an orbiting spinning body.

Relativising Relativity

It is more or less common knowledge that Einstein's Special Theory of Relativity is a mathematical theory of space and time, based on experiments of a technical nature concerning the emission and propagation of light. What is not commonly known is that the same theory can be deduced from premises of an altogether different kind, namely, those provided by perception theorists of the sort represented by George Berkeley and Henri Poincaré —and without any conception of light-propagation.

This may be demonstrated in the following way. I shall begin with the dimensions of things as ordinarily applied to phenomena: that is, from within one's own visual sphere, with oneself at the centre and the world stretching radially from that centre in an extrapolated 'onion' of two-dimensional (spherical) surfaces. The number of these dimensions (as in classical or Cartesian volume) are therefore three. To quote Berkeley's modern successor, A.J. Ayer†:-

'It has been argued, notably by Berkeley, that the visual field is originally two-dimensional and that we come to see things in depth only through the association of sight with touch. Against this, psychologists like William James have maintained that depth is as much an intrinsic property of our visual fields as are length and breadth.'

The world of vision is therefore like classical space insofar as it is a world of places and times, but the places and times are now relative. As Ayer puts it‡‡ 'Places are places in a visual field and the times those that furnish the temporal order of experiences. ...In this way the framework is set for the description of visual appearances.'

Nor is this kind of reference-system by any means to be regarded as entirely subjective (solipsistic), for, as Ayer observes, ‡‡‡ 'We can identify a sense-field by reference not only to its own character but also that of its neighbours, and if necessary to that of their neighbours and so on.'

† A.J. Ayer, The Central Questions of Philosophy (London 1973) p.91 ‡‡ ibid. ‡‡‡ ibid p. 93

The spherical (or polar) system of dimensions thus projected is therefore every bit as objective in its inception as the classical, or Cartesian, system.

My point, however, is not so much that of arguing the philosophical merits or demerits of this polar, or observer-centred, frame of reference. It is simply to demonstrate —if as no more than an academic exercise— how this leads logically to relativity. My first premise, then, as opposed to that of conventional relativity, will not be the usual one of how light 'travels in vacuo' but simply that of how objects and events are distributed in relation to one another in the reference-field of some specific observer who forms the unique focus of the whole dimensional system. The space in which events and processes take place is thus the field of ordinary visual observation, where objects, such as the planets of the solar system, are extrapolated by the observer along a spherical radius s, the whole of which evolves in the time (t) of the observer. The dimensions of this system are therefore, altogether, four, namely the three-dimensional sphere described by Ayer, consisting of the radius (depth) and the vertical and horizontal inclinations of that radius (elevation and azimuth) together with the time measured at the radial origin 0.

My second premise is that of objective or public space, which is the logical and mathematical coordination of these private spaces, or perspectives, so far described. In this public space the radial dimension s separating other observers from observer 0 is reciprocated, forming a set of systematic responses or signals (language-system) to the effect that 0 is as much an object in the visual fields of other observers as they are in his. In this way is formed, by systematic intercommunication, an observational community, which is an extrapolated network of basically visual —and of course linguistic— relations. This may now be conceived as of Cartesian, or traditional, rather than of polar construction.

The difference, however, is that extension, in this public space, is not now something which exists a priori but only a postiori, as a measure established by evolved convention, which transforms individual polar perspectives into a decentralised Cartesian grid. Like classical space, this volume is defined by three rectangular dimensions whose units are (currently) metres, and the whole of which evolves simultaneously (i.e., synchronously at all centres) in a dimension commonly measured in seconds.

It can already be seen how radically this fundamentally relativistic approach to physical dimensions differs from the classical, or absolutistic, interpretation. There is, for all practical intents and purposes, the same

kind of Cartesian or cubic space, but now no longer basic to the physical description of things, being now a product —not a metaphysical precondition— of experience. There is thus no question of a continuous self-sufficiently enduring 'empty space'. The only space and time that signify, on this relativistic thesis, are the dimensions extrapolated in positive intercommunication between observers. If, for some reason, all communicants were removed, then it would be nonsense to suppose that what would remain behind would be 'empty space', because with space being no more than an extrapolation made in communication, without such communication there can be no space —nor time, since it is nowhere but in the evolution of relative space that time exists according to the thesis in question.

The stage is set, then, not only for relativity but also for quantum indeterminacy, for there is now no determinate 'universe of space' against which, in its entirety, all activity can be measured. It is the agents who, by their specific interactions, determine the character of space, not vice versa. The term 'agent', moreover, need not necessarily signify a human, or even a sentient, observer. It may be anything that is capable of giving a simple visual response: anything, in fact, which can manifest itself in visual transactions at any level of simplicity or complexity whatsoever (that is, animal, vegetable or mineral).

An agent, therefore, need not necessarily perceive in the sense that a human observer perceives. It is sufficient that it responds or relates in some entirely elementary way in the same terms as those in which human perception is couched. We therefore avoid the idealistic entrapment of Berkeley's dictum esse is percipi, because neither the agents nor the space they generate by their interactions are now required to be conscious. Mind, in this system thus loses the monopoly ascribed to it by traditional idealists like Berkeley and Kant and without becoming the nonentity it was presumed to be by classical physicalists. In the physical category of vision, therefore, we are now not so much concerned with visual signals (qua meaningful communications between users of language) as with actions —that is, responses between agents which may or may not partake in any conscious observation.

In this way, physical interactions and the space —and the time— in which they take place become inseparable. There is no underlying continuum of space, of time or of space-time to be considered. The only thing to be considered is positive interactions between objects which are distinguished by nothing more than the logical type and character of those selfsame interactions. This, as opposed to the metaphysic of a continuous and determinate empty space, is the fundamentally relativistic and indeterministic basis —the

Now the distances s between these centres of action, in the extrapolated cube of objective space, are measured of course in conventional units of metres, and the evolution of the whole relative to each centre in conventional units of seconds. My next (i.e., third) premise concerns a factual discovery made in the context of astronomy by Olaus Roemer in 1676. Roemer, as is well known to students of physics, discovered a constant ratio (now known as 'c') between the distance and time dimensions of astronomical objects. In recording the eclipses of Io, Jupiter's principal satellite, by the body of the parent planet, Roemer discovered a definite correlation between certain alterations in the frequency of the eclipses and changes in the distance between the earth and Jupiter as these planets orbited the sun. The reason for this, Roemer concluded (consistently with the mechanistic ideas of his time) was that it was due to the 'finite velocity of light', which the earth-borne observer anticipates and outruns as the distance between earth and Jupiter decreases and increases, respectively. It was in the wake of this discovery that there followed the familiar tradition of causal-projection theories associated with the names of Michael Faraday, James Clerk Maxwell and Heinrich Hertz. The theory which therefore became standard along this line of conception was the one according to which c is the 'velocity of propagation' of alternate electric and magnetic pulses, expanding away into Newtonian space in the manner of waves in a pond, to be picked up at random (if at all) by any incidental recipient.

Such, then, is the one way of thinking about dimensions and processes involving c, in which c is portrayed as the 'velocity of light'. It is evident, however, that in the thesis we are examining (that is, the polar thesis) c cannot configure in that same mechanistic way. So how, according to that thesis, should these facts relating to distance and time be presented? The simplest answer to that, I suggest, is as follows.

The sheer unembroidered fact discovered by Roemer is that in unmediated observation of distant objects there is a constant numerical ratio between the units of distance and time, such that a second of time has to be allowed for every three hundred million metres (186,000 miles). Thus, whereas the ratio of units of length between the dimensions of public space is unity (i.e., constant k = 1 metre per metre) the ratio between any one of these dimensions and time, in what might be called public kinespace, due to the arbitrary choice of units is c = 3 x 10⁸ metres to the second†. This, traditionally conceived as 'the velocity of electromagnetic propagation in vacuo' is now nothing more nor less than an a priori dimensional pre-

condition of an observational reference-frame, like the equivalence between units in defining the dimensions of area or volume.

Since the dimensions measured in metres and in seconds are now, in effect, no more than dimensions of perspective, then the constant c, giving the ratio of these arbitrary units needs to be thought of as nothing more than a constant of perspective. Indeed, we may well imagine that through a more fortuitous choice of units for length and time (that is, with the length-unit being 3 x 10^8 metres, or the time-unit being 3.3 x 10^-9 of a second, the constant c, like k, would be unity, dispensing with the customary necessity of stipulating c in physical formulae.

However, as things are, the constant does appear, evoking, invariably but unnecessarily, the concept of 'light-velocity'. As a constant of perspective, however, it need not, other than superficially, display those characteristics of causal-mechanical motion. We should not, for instance, expect it to obey the rule of the addition and subtraction of velocities for relatively moving observers, or to involve in any ordinary way the concepts of momentum and inertia. As interpreted in this thesis it simply is not that kind of thing. One is no more compelled to wonder how 'light' behaves when travelling all alone and unobserved in vacuo, between one assignation and another, than puzzle about where rainbows end or where birthdays come from and disappear to.

Nevertheless, by its very definition, c, notwithstanding that it is a constant of perspective, entails a lapse of time in accordance with distance, a kinematical characteristic which it shares, at least superficially, with mechanical motion. In other words, in communal or Cartesian space, made up as it is of observers (agents) interacting with one another, a visual signal made by an observer P, who is a distance s away from observer O, will have a time-value retrospectively assigned (in accordance with Roemer's ratio) as t_p = t_o - (s/c), where t_p is the time signalled by P (a time-centre, say, such as Greenwich) and t_o the time set by O on receiving it. O's direct response to the signal will then be observed by P at the time t'_p = t_o - (s'/c), where s' is the distance of O relative to P at the time of making the response. The time t'_p = t_p at P will thus be an objective interval conventionally interpreted as 'the time taken for the light-signal to make the round trip from P to O and back to P.'

The reciprocity of the perspectival dimension s, relative to observers separated in public space (kinespace) therefore entails that as a characteristic feature of that kinespace, signals of a visual kind will appear to travel. It is evident, however, that there is now no question of mechanical conveyance of the signal between O and P in any pre-existing 'absolute space', by any continuous medium or 'field'. To be conveyed in any such manner the

signal, like all travelling entities, would have to evolve during transit, which in no manner of speaking does photonic energy do. The transfer is immediate and atemporal, forming a kind of space-time triangle P - O - P', whose dimensions, far from being primordial preconditions for the event are coextensive with and inseparable from it. The space and time (or, rather, the space-time) of the signal, in other words, are not something in which the signal travels (which is absurd on so absolutely fundamental a level of analysis) but something which may be measured from the actual and indeterminate occurrence. There is, moreover, no question of non-consummation of such a signal, for this is now not something consigned 'to the void' and thus to random consummation or extinction, as in traditional propagation theory. The notion of a signal 'missing its target' and attenuating away indefinitely therefore becomes absurd. In this thesis, therefore, there can be none of those problems like 'Olbers' paradox' and 'ultraviolet catastrophe' which worried classical propagation theorists, who could see no reason why electromagnetic energy should not simply radiate away automatically in this unrestrained fashion until space was saturated.

The constant c, interpreted in this essentially relativistic way as a constant of perspective, therefore has all those characteristics which are essential to modern experimental science but without any of those mechanistic overtones which have mystified generations of thinkers from Michelson and Morley (with their experiments on light-velocity in moving reference-frames) right up to the present time. Signals do, in the relevant sense, take time to cover distance, but at a rate which is the same for all observers independently of their motions relative to one another, to the source or to any supposed interconnecting medium. This set of propositions, confirmed beyond all question of doubt by experimental science, is completely incomprehensible to anyone contemplating c as a causal-mechanical velocity, yet it is natural enough —indeed, no more than what is to be expected— of a constant of perspective. And it goes without saying that space and time (and all quantities defined in terms of these dimension) are now relative, which they are a priori on the thesis presented. No special 'relativistic' rationale is now required to persuade us of this fact against any logical inclination to think otherwise (as there is, of course, with conventional relativity, evolved as it is on the basis of classical presupposition about light-propagation in absolute and continuous space).

So far, then, there are three main concepts, or 'pillars', on which this thesis rests. These are (1) the particular space consisting of the polar dimensions defined in vision; (2), the coordination, in language, of these fundamental visual spaces to form an objective or public (i.e.,

Cartesian) space, or space-in-general, which has no existence in its own right apart from these particular spaces; and (3), the constant empirical ratio, c, between units of length and time common to these individual spaces and characteristic, therefore, of space-in-general.

Now let us see what happens when we add to these the concept of the velocity of a body relative to the system of reference described. My fourth premise, accordingly, will be the conventional definition of velocity as the displacement, d, of a physical object in a given period of time. There is now, of course, no question of such motion other than in relation to some centre or set of centres of the kind represented by O and P, above. Moreover, all distances d, in this kind of reference-frame are now also times expressed as d/c, in accordance with premise (3). We therefore have the consequence that the distance over which the body travels and the time it takes to cover that distance are dimensions of the same kind — like length and breadth in the definition of an area.

The mathematical relationship between such dimensions, of course, in forbidding that they encroach on one another's domains, is orthogonal. This is to say that they are related, for all quantitative purposes, like the dimensions that define a plane rectangular surface. Thus, if t_x is the time recorded by an observer on a body X in progressing uniformly between two points, x and x', a distance d apart in public or objective space^†,

This very simple result is of profound significance in respect of modern science, in which the formula plays a vitally important —but so far esoteric— role in the affairs of physics. In the present thesis, however, it involves no esotery whatsoever, being based on ordinary commonsense logic, elementary algebra and straight observational information.

Something needs to be said, however, concerning the philosophical significance of this result. It is, of course, true that the result, as a physicist has remarked, could not have been confidently projected in this way unless one already knew about Einstein and the experimental verification of his theory. Only in the knowledge that formula (4) is the acceptable answer, says our critic, is it possible to contend that the result is viable. My answer is that although this is undoubtedly true, and that one does not wish to detract in any way from the greatness of Einstein's theoretical achievement, nevertheless, from a philosophical (as opposed to professionally scientific) point of view it is both interesting and significant, surely, that Einstein's result is obtainable in a different way and from different philosophical premises.

It seems to me, therefore, a singularly unphilosophical attitude to say, as some critics do: 'Well alright, it works, but so what?' Similarly with those who regard Einstein's derivation of the formula as a professional fait accompli, rendering all other derivations merely presumptuous. These seem to miss the whole point and purpose of philosophy. Least of all, however, I confess, can I sympathise with the critic who quoted to me Polanyi as saying 'There must at all times be a predominantly accepted scientific view of the nature of things, in the light of which research is jointly conducted by members of the community of scientists. A strong presumption that anything contradicting this view is invalid must prevail.'

and who said he agreed with this because 'It ensures that some form of stability exists and cuts down the number of fashions you would otherwise get.' I

well imagine that something very much like this defence of the status quo must have been mobilised in the trial of Socrates, or by the Inquisition against people like Bruno and Galileo when they appeared on the charge of heresy.

However, if it be allowed that there is always some philosophical and educational value in re-examining existing ideas, then it will be seen that criticising the conceptual history of present science is not the same as trying to vandalise that institution. It is simply to express the true spirit of scientific philosophy, which is to avail itself wherever possible of the wisdom which hindsight provides. To discover, by means of such hindsight, that there are ways in which relativity theory might have been conceived other than in the manner of Einstein —and with greater conceptual economy— is something which Einstein himself would have anticipated. At least it is difficult to imagine Einstein, imbued as he so evidently was with the philosophical spirit, taking umbrage at this or any other attempt to look at his theory in so radically different and so philosophical a way.

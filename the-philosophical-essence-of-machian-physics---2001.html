The Philosophical Essence of Machian Physics

N. Vivian Pope — 2001

Let us start with a purist scientific assumption of complete ignorance. This supposes that we know nothing about space, time, matter or whatever, other than from pure observation. This, basically, is the philosophical approach to science which is known as radical empiricism, or 'phenomenalism'. It is the approach to physics that was instituted by George Berkeley (1685–1753) and it led, in more modern times, to the sort of physics we nowadays associate with Ernst Mach (1838–1916).

The best way to appreciate this 'phenomenalist', or purely observational way of thinking is, basically, in terms of our astronomical observations of distant bodies, where there is no possibility of any kind of knowledge of these objects other than by means of optical information supplied by our eyes and instruments such as the telescope and spectroscope. Obviously, no question can arise, at this stage, as to how the information is mediated, at what speed, etc. Shorn of those kinds of precepts, everything that is seen is seen immediately, the informational elements (the spectral quanta) as they are associated with the source being indistinguishable from the way they are manifest in direct and/or instrumental observation.

Now this purely observationist approach has some distinctive consequences. For instance, it can be shown, very easily and logically, how the relativism of Einstein follows from this phenomenalistic stance of his philosophy teacher, Mach. Take two elementary observers $A$ and $B$. On the assumption that $A$ knows nothing about $B$ other than through optical communication, the distance of $B$ with respect to $A$ is a measure, by some fixed standard of comparison, of the size and brightness of $B$ in $A$'s telescope, and the time of $B$ is the measure of some number of the periods of $B$ in some standard spectral line, as registered in $A$'s spectroscope.

Now being a complete agnostic — that is, a finitist, as purist science demands — our observer $A$ never presumes to perceive the universe from more than one place at a time. This means that he cannot think, in our traditional God's-eye-view way, of an all-over-simultaneously existing 'universe' of space and time which is absolute, or independent of finite observation. Nevertheless, by means of his instruments, and in communication with other observers, he is able to externalise, or objectify, his perceptions to form a communal grid-space with dimensions of the sort projected by Descartes.

However, without the deistic, God's-eye-view presumption of observational ubiquity, all the various observations and interactions of human and instrumental observers — in short, all communications and interactions whatsoever — are essentially discrete and finite, so that there can be no question of an underlying continuum either of physical or communicational interaction. Particles interact in terms of discrete interaction-elements (i.e., quanta), and scientists describe these interactions in theoretical concepts which they intercommunicate with other scientists in some version or another of human language.

Eventually, $A$ discovers, in the manner of Olaus Rømer, that changes in the distances of bodies thus established by polar extrapolation are accompanied by changes in the spectral periods of those bodies (the so-called 'Doppler effect') in the constant ratio, $c$, of one second for every $3 \times 10^8$ metres.

So now let us suppose that $B$, travelling steadily away from $A$, and equipped with the same sorts of telescopes and spectroscopes, after travelling a distance $s$ in metres, initiates a sharp reversal of motion to bring him back towards $A$. In his spectroscopic observation of $A$, the optical periods of $A$ counted by $B$ from the moment of his leaving $A$ now suddenly change from relatively slowed (redshifted) to relatively quickened (blue-shifted) periods in comparison with his own standard ones. Meanwhile, $A$, being remote from $B$'s changeover action, will have counted more reddened spectroscopic periods of $B$ during the mutual out-and-back motion than $B$ will of $A$ due to the extra time incurred by the distance-time $s/c$ of the event from $A$. And because the speeds of the observers, from that turning-point on, are symmetrical, there will be less blue-shifted periods of $A$ read by $B$ than of $B$ read by $A$. This, in the standard orthodox, Einsteinian way of thinking, produces, on returning together, an irreversible discrepancy in the overall spectral counts of the two observers regarding each other. This is as described by Einstein's well-known relativistic time-dilation formula.

Now as long as we do not suppose that there is some overall God's-eye-view that can be taken of the two discrete time-frames in the same instance, there can be no paradox entailed by this asymmetry between the atomic time-counts of the two discrete observer frames. In other words, the consequence that in the interim between leaving and returning together the two have aged at different rates presents no problem when the two systems are kept consistently discrete.

What is entailed, however, is that among any number of relatively moving and differently time-dilated systems there must always be, even if only in theory, a least-dilated time-frame of them all. This, on a universal scale, corresponds to the Machian time-frame of the 'fixed stars', the uniquely preferred frame with reference to which all true motion and time-dilation are judged. It is also the datum frame of universal angular momentum described in the Keele thesis by Pope and Osborne.

This neo-Machian angular momentum frame includes, of course, our observers $A$ and $B$. Let us suppose that with different masses, $M$ and $m$, respectively (with $M$ greater than $m$), they are in free orbital motion around each other with some constant amount of angular momentum, in the manner described by the Keele thesis. Let us suppose, also, that the barycentre of this particular two-body system is stationary with respect to the sidereal frame. With $A$ having the greater mass $M$, he will have the slowest speed relative to the sidereal frame, and $B$, with his smaller mass $m$, will have the faster. And in consequence of those different speeds, $A$ will have the least-dilated time of the two, relative to the sidereal time-frame, while $B$ will have the greater.

In this case, of free orbital motion, there is no asymmetry between the two views of a reversal event to distinguish which of the observers is time-dilated and which is time-contracted relative to which. The point of reversal of their out-and-back motion (assuming that the orbits are elliptical, as is invariably the case) will be simultaneous for both — because, of course, in an angular momentum nexus the two motions are instantaneously and reciprocally balanced. What, then, distinguishes the ageing-rates of the two observers in this case? It can't be acceleration, i.e., the stopping and reversing of linear momentum as in Einsteinian Special Relativity. The only distinguishing factor in this free-orbital case is simply the difference between what we might regard as the real speeds of the bodies relative to the sidereal frame.

This cannot affect the value of $c$ in experiments of the sort conducted by Michelson and Morley because in this neo-Machian view, $c$ is now not a speed but a dimensional constant for relating measures of distance and time, the same for all observers whether moving or stationary relative to one another.

We therefore have two kinds of time-dilation situations to consider, the one distinguished by accelerated, e.g., simple out-and-back motion of the sort Einstein envisaged in the Galileo-Newton tradition of 'inertial space', and the other by different speeds relative to the sidereal, or 'home' frame. It is to be noted, however, that the instantaneity of correlation of orbital motions entailed by angular momentum conservation is relativistically invariant, that is, the same in all systems of reference including, of course, that of the universal sidereal frame (of both bright and dark matter).

We recall, however, that in the neo-Machian way of thinking, all interrelations between bodies are discrete. That is, there is no single, overall God's-eye-view that can be taken of these various interactional/interrelational events. All we may think of, consistently with our adopted discretist stance, is the interaction or interrelation of just one with the other of a single pair of particles in any particular instance. This, of course, applies only to particles on the most fundamental, microphysical level, where interactions and interrelations are quantised in terms of $h$ and $\hbar$, respectively.

For bodies consisting of large numbers of these particles, this quantum discretum becomes a statistical continuum, the model for which is the candle-flame which, as Heraclitus pointed out, despite the violent atomic changes taking place within it, appears to stand steadily and serenely in still air. In that same way, the angular momentum discretum appears to us on the macroscale as a continuum, that is to say, a Heraclitean as opposed to a Parmenidean continuum, the essential difference being that the former entails fluidity of motion, the other a complete stasis.

For Mach's principle to work, therefore, without entailing a complete cosmic gridlock in which in order to move, every physical object would need, in that same instant, to move all the others, its overall instantaneous connections need to be fundamentally quantised in discrete, intransitive instantaneities. This means that the statements '$A$ is instantly connected with $B$' and '$B$ is instantly connected with $C$' do not entail '$A$ is instantly connected with $C$.' In other words, in gaining some quantum of action from $A$, $B$ cannot in that same instance lose that very same quantum to $C$ (because, of course, gaining and losing are two events not one). So the break in continuity of interaction that there has to be at $B$, between gaining and losing a quantum, is what saves Mach's principle from the logical consequence of an Eleatic stasis. ■

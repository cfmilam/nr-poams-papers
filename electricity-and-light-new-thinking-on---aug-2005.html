Electricity and light. New thinking on - Aug. 2005

N. Vivian Pope — 2005

New Thinking on the Non-velocity Interpretation of c

In an electrical conductor a quantum of electrical energy cannot be fed into the one end until that exact quantum amount is removed from the other. (See the ‘Nev-Viv correspondence, in the POAMS Yahoo discussion group.) The same goes for light, i.e., a quantum cannot leave the source-atom until there is a place for it in some other (resonant) atom at the absorber end. In this regard, electrical energy and light energy are the same. Both kinds of interaction take place in accordance with both the conservation law and the second law of thermodynamics. The difference is that in the electrical case, the source and the sink are specifically linked, with the quantum having nowhere to go other than to the far terminal, whereas in the case of light, the sinks for the source energy according to those laws are distributed far and wide. However, if, in the case of light, the number of possible sinks for the source energy could be reduced to just one, then, logically, the effects observed in the teleblocking of light should be the same as is observed in the electrical example. The difference, then, between the two cases can be modelled in the following way. The electrical circuit is likened to a circulating water system in which the opposite ends of a pipe are plumbed-in to the input and output of a pump. What happens to the flow of the electric current is then analogous to that of the water which, with the pipe already full in the way Nev describes, cannot flow in the circuit until the entrance to the far end of the pump is open. By contrast, in the case of light, the flow is like that of water pumped into a garden sprinkler. If one or two of the holes in the sprinkler are blocked, the flow continues in greater strength though the remaining holes, without any perceptible effect on the output from the source. This is precisely analogous to what happens to light in the two-slit light experiment. There are thus, logically, two kinds of what we have called ‘teleblocking’. In the electrical case the only possible outlet for the source energy is at the specific sink provided by the opposite pole of the battery (or other source of e.m.f.), while in the case of light it consists of blocking all possible sinks for the energy by – if it were possible – placing all possible sinks for the energy at distances where they are asynchronous with the source. The principle of teleblocking is therefore the same in both cases, but is easier to prove with electricity than with light. And since (ex hypothesi) the distance-time flow-rate is c in both cases, then the proof that c is not a ‘speed’ in the one case applies in the same way to the other. Question 1: Why does an electric current not flow from the negative pole of one fully charged battery to the positive pole of another? The answer is supplied by the same water analogy. If a circulating pump forces water into a pipe circuit, then in order for that flow to register in the flow meter, precisely the same amount of water has to be sucked-in at the input end. The answer is that in a battery, or some other source of e.m.f., the source and sink are specifically linked via the common energy producer. The battery cannot deliver energy to the front end of the conductor without losing precisely the same amount of energy at the back end.

An Interview with a Repudiator of ANPA - Spring 1995

An Interview with a Repudiator of ANPA

At various meetings with physicists and at physics conferences I have solicited views about ANPA. The good news is that most of these people have heard about ANPA; the bad news is that they tend to be dismissive about what ANPA is doing.

The reason, so far as I can discover, is that your typical Anti-ANPA physicist (AAP) prefers simply to 'do physics', without thinking too much about it on a philosophical level. To him, this would be as pointless and inhibiting – and annoying – as for an Olympic hurdler, in mid-stride, to be asked why he does what he does and to consider how his legs move in the way they do.

Yet, in a cool hour, your AAP may wax philosophical about what he does, albeit in a naive, 'DIY' kind of way. Also, being an educated man, he has at least heard of philosophers like Berkeley, Hume and Kant and is vaguely aware of the difference between what he conceives as 'Positivism' (usually confused with 'Empiricism') and 'Realism' in attitudes towards physics. This division he more or less accepts as built-in, if not into the very nature of scientific enquiry, then at least into the academic package he swore allegiance to as a Physics student. As to what has transpired in philosophy since those attitudes were delineated and what has since been their fate at the hands of later philosophers, such as Ayer and Wittgenstein, even the most well-appointed AAP knows virtually nothing. Here, for instance, is an edited transcript of a fairly typical repudiating attitude revealed in a recorded discussion with a physicist who has attended some of Pierre Noyes' lectures but who wishes to remain incognito.

N.B. I should like it understood that these views were not solicited in support of, nor are they in any way aligned with, my own critical approach – vide my closing comment on p.16. [NVP]

Where to start? Any Head of Department (of Physics) gets all sorts of crank letters saying 'Einstein is wrong'. I got one yesterday. What these people typically do is pick out one little bit of the theory and talk round it. What they say is, instead of doing it that way we will do it some other way. What they don't realise is that relativity isn't like that, that there are endless hundreds, thousands, millions of tests. If you're going to propose a counter theory, you have to explain everything. You're not allowed to take that little bit out and argue round that.

You see, Science is a funny game. It's quite revolutionary. But when a revolution happens, the revolution has to explain everything that's happened before. In that sense it is revolutionary but it's also very reactionary. It's not good enough to get just one or two things right, you've got to get everything right – or at least have the potential for getting everything right.

This is in a region where we actually think we understand things very well. We make predictions to twelve decimal places. They work. This gives us the real feeling of confidence that we understand everything, where we can see what we have to do. It's not as though we have no predictions or that the experiments have not been done in that particular region, or that we haven't got enough accelerators or that we haven't got precise enough measurements or whatever. There is this area where experiment and theory all agree. Then there is another region where you have a theory that's too hard, we can't actually do the calculations. We think it's right, the quality looks right, it's just that the computations are too expensive. We're not clever enough. Then there are regions where we can make predictions but where the experiments haven't been done. We're always in this situation where there is a core, where we're usually very happy. Then there are the boundaries, either the theory boundaries where we don't know quite how to do the calculations, or the experiment boundaries where at the moment we're in a happy situation in terms of particle physics where everything looks under control, where there are no places where we can calculate and it doesn't agree. You either don't do the experiments because they're too tough or technically too hard or too expensive or you don't actually know how to do them. It's fine thinking-up experiments in your study late at night but to actually do them is another game – to measure the precise times, or precise lengths with not a good enough ruler, so to speak.

Now what these guys do is that they typically try and make a revolutionary step. Somehow, everything that's gone is wrong, or it's not the right way of looking at it. But they don't explain all the past things. They have a new concept-structure, but it doesn't include what's gone before. After all these kinds of things by Mr. Kuhn – you know, his paradigm shifts – what comes over when philosophers discuss these things is that they tend to think there's an old theory which is now wrong and a new theory that is right. A physicist never sees it like that. The old theory is still sitting there inside the new theory. Newton did not get it wrong. Classical big objects are described by Newton's equations. Quantum mechanics explains both atoms and Newton's equations. Newton didn't know about atoms, he had no experimental information. It was a no-go area for him. But quantum mechanics has, hidden inside it, Newton's equations. Similarly with Special Relativity. Of course, it's different; but hiding in there, for big, slow objects, Einstein is Newton. Also, when you're sending an astronaut to the moon you needn't worry about Einstein's corrections. It's all Newton's equations. That will be good enough.

You understand what I'm saying? New theories somehow, almost invariably, since the modern revolution, don't jump over here [gesture]. We have many good theories. They may twist a little bit but really a new theory of the planets almost has to include Newton. It may look very different and may sound very different, But Newton got the right answers. We can send a satellite to Saturn or somewhere and it arrives on time, in place. So, you understand, any new theory had better include that.

Do you see these people, then, as trying to be 'Copernican'?

Yes, trying to jump from theory to theory.

Yes.

You mean the Positivistic sort of empiricism, Logical Positivism?

Yes. They only used experimental data, only talked about instrumental data, and so on. The modern thing is to write it all in terms of quantum field-theories which are very much mathematical objects. They predict the experiments.

Yes, of course, Positivism is a dead duck, now. But I'm still wondering what you think is Pierre's stance in this. Do you see him as being some sort of meta-physician here, or what? Do you see him as stepping outside the normal run of physics? What do you see him as trying to do?

Pierre Noyes? There is this sort of built-in conservatism of physics. It is just conceivable, of course, that eventually you'll get a view, say, of the motions of the planets and quantum mechanics which is just very different from Newton's. But Newton is so accurate. But wherever you end up, there is some limit, you've got to apply Newton. Parts in millions. Sending satellites there, they land on time. The equations work to that accuracy.

What about the rosette motion, like the advance in perihelion of Mercury?

Well, that's a very, very tiny effect predicted by General Relativity. It's about one part in ten-to-the-eight or something. But you'd be happy to get your salary working to an error of something like one part in ten-to-the-eight! Well alright, in some ways Einstein is better. I mean, there are these black holes and things that astronomers make out that they've seen. Newton doesn't get that right either. So Newton has limits. It's for slowish objects and not-very-strong gravitational fields. There are small corrections. There are things that deviate from Newton. That's well understood.

And then you take up the other theory?

Yes, but Newton isn't dead. For most of the time he's perfectly adequate.

But to be fair, I think Pierre Noyes would agree with that. It seems to me that the line you are projecting there is the one Pierre projects.

No, he's trying to be very different.

Okay, so in which way is that?

Well, he's got all these statements about bit-strings and accuracy and all that.

But you, yourself, are a string-man, aren't you, a string-theorist?

But this is different. My string is a physical object that moves in space. In principle, if you're clever enough, you can measure a string of this size. It's very small but in principle you can see it. He is saying that when we measure the position of a particle we cannot measure it accurately within a finite number of decimal places. It's like my computer over there. It can sense sixteen decimal places. I can't put arbitrary numbers in there. I can only put given lengths, so to speak. So all that I can stick in there are sixteen numbers long. If I'm clever I can double it, triple it. But if I don't do anything fancy, the computer works with a certain accuracy. What Pierre argues is that measurements of, say, the position of a particle, work to a certain accuracy. I mean, you can't go to seventy decimal places. There's a limit. His string is not a physical string.

You're saying it's not a physical but a numerological object, purely mathematical?

That's my feeling, yes. The message I get from this thing is that I don't think that it will lead anywhere. That's purely subjective, I admit. But, I mean, you hear the talk. You read the titles of the papers and so on. But I wouldn't want to spend a week really trying to get into this stuff in deep detail. This is a very subjective judgement, of course, but I don't really see it working. That is, I wouldn't go down to the betting shop and put my-money on it!

What is it, then? Do you find it easy to understand? Do you see it as clear and wrong? Or do you see it as garbled or what?

It's clear but it makes me think 'so what'? I mean, in it there is no process that I once couldn't calculate and can now calculate. It doesn't give any deep insight into any calculation that used to be hard and has now been made easy.

As you see it, then, it doesn't help the physicist?

That's right. A theory can advance in many ways. Suddenly things you could not understand are made obvious. A calculation that used to be horribly difficult is now easy. Something you had no idea how to get your hands on, you now see how to do it.

So that, you would say, is the criterion that is required for acceptance of a physicist's theory but that Pierre doesn't supply this, that he seems to be taking an almost mystical view – of, say, the number 1836. In other words, it seems to you a Rationalist, a priori-istic way of going about things rather than in a proper, experimental, way.

Well yes, and it's not the way science operates. There's a bit of theory that gives you an idea. The idea follows from the scientific experiments, but there are, say, fifty of those ideas that may follow. One of them 'smells right'. Most of the books on philosophy don't get that right, They give you a very simplistic view of how physics operates.

Just to put you right regarding my attitude, here: I'm essentially an engineer. I learned my logic as a telephone lineman who, at the end of the day, had to 'come up with the goods'.

It either works or it doesn't!

Yes, I'm very much in that line. I came into philosophy as an adult student and, having graduated in the subject, find myself at odds with philosophers as such.

But of course, everybody has a philosophy, including physicists, no matter how they may be able to articulate it. They may not be able to explain it, but they have one.

Well, I'm glad you said that! And I'm trying to chase that up, you see, both here and at ANPA. I'm here to find out, not to impose anything of my own, or make like some philosophical 'Billy Graham'!

Comment:

My own reaction to this typical interview is as For me to believe it – it's the same as with Einstein – if I'm going to really get involved in working on this it's got to do something dramatic for me.

follows. Obviously, just because the Olympic Hurdler doesn't have to be interested in how his muscles work when doing what he does, it doesn't follow that a physiologist needn't – or shouldn't – be interested either. As I see it, as this physiologist is to the Olympic athlete, so, logically, is the philosopher to the physicist. Their concerns are not and need not be the same. Take Einstein's revolutionary theory. This, as our critic says, enables the physicist to explain experimental results that previously he couldn't explain. The philosopher is not so much concerned with those experimental results as with their conceptual implications. The fact, for instance, that relativity makes the distances and times of things different for all relatively moving observers and that quantum theory splits it all up into discrete and irreducible events alerts the philosopher to the need for some wholesale, fundamental revision in our traditional conceptions of space and time (pace ANPA). All the physicist need do, meanwhile, is simply to make the required relativistic fine adjustments to his equations whilst carrying-on thinking in the old and conservative, more or less Newtonian way. Well, that's alright; he is perfectly free to do that. But why should that make him want to commandeer 'Einstein' for himself? After all, Einstein claimed to be a philosopher rather than a scientist. I blame the Educational system, with its stick-in-the-mud adherence to the bogus, yet now institutionalised, Arts-Science dichotomy, for not allowing our modern physicists to understand what Einstein meant. [NVP]

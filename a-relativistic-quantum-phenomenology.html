A RELATIVISTIC QUANTUM PHENOMENOLOGY

The Keele Approach to Relativistic Quantum Physics: A Brief Description

Though its effect has been mostly delayed, the discovery of quantum indeterminacy was a shock to Western Science. It threw the discipline, for the greater part of this present century, into a state of suspended irresolution between two completely different, indeed antithetical, approaches to our understanding of nature. The one is what might be called the analog approach. The other, in contrast, is the digital approach.

Typical of the analog way of thinking, which is traditional, is to assume that on the most fundamental levels of physics everything takes place in much the same way as it does in our ordinary, mechanical-engineering types of experience in which it seems that bodies exist and move continuously –that is to say, without fundamental breaks or jumps. By contrast, in the digital approach there is no such continuity. In this way of thinking, everything takes place in what are called quantum jumps, like the events that make up the pictures on a video or cinema screen.

In a truly digital account of physics there can be no continuous vacuum or void to contain quantum events in the way that objects on ordinary levels of experience are contained in an ordinary room. Consistency demands that the 'room' and everything it contains are all part of the same digital scenario. Therefore, according to this radically different way of thinking, the only continuity there can be, of space, time, momentum or whatever consists of statistical numbers of these quantum events (discrete packages of those dimensions) in which physical objects only appear to have determinate positions and steady states of motion and rest – like Heraclitus' candle-flame burning steadily in still air while its constituents are in a state of rapid and violent flux. Another example is the stagecoach in a movie, whose apparently continuous motion consists of rapid sequences of motionless frames, or 'stills'.

What keeps our physics in its present suspended state of confusion is the persistence, in what seems to be the newly-emerging quantum-digital approach to nature, of completely incongruous and recalcitrant analog ways of thinking about light and how it travels in a vacuum to link one quantum event and another. The confusion is akin to that of someone watching a Tom and Jerry cartoon feeling compelled to theorise about what the cat and the mouse are doing in the gaps between the frames. So on the one hand we have the self-styled 'Realists' of modern physics insisting on thinking of quantum phenomena in traditional analog terms. On the other hand are the 'Positivists' who eschew all that analog talk of 'hidden variables' and settle for the up-front reality of the quantum data and purely information-statistical methods of dealing with them.

Neither of these esoteric ways of thinking accords with that of ordinary commonsense which has been all but torn apart between these two intellectual factions, as though, in the biblical story of the Judgement of Solomon, each of the women claiming motherhood of the baby had been content to walk off with half a severed child. What makes matters worse are the attempts of other physicists to 'tape together', as it were, these two severed halves, with their mystical talk of 'quantum mechanics' (a contradiction in terms), 'wave-particles' (another contradiction) and so on, the Babelian jargon of which is completely meaningless to any ordinary person.

Also meaningless, strictly speaking, is the current analog notion of light as something travelling invisibly in a vacuum. Although commonsense has become used to it, it was never a truly commonsense conception. The original commonsense meaning of 'light' is that it is simply what you see, the opposite of 'dark', so that you might as sensibly talk about dark travelling as talk of light travelling in a void.

Undoubtedly, then, commonsense could quite comfortably dispense with the analog-type idea of light travelling invisibly in a vacuum and revert to thinking of it in the purely perceptual way, provided it can be shown to be the logical course. No real problem, then, may be anticipated from a commonsense point of view in having to shift from the analog conception of light into a mode more in keeping with the modern quantum-digital developments. Indeed, a highly influential strand of thought, beginning with the seventeenth century philosopher Locke and continued by Berkeley, Hume, Kant and eventually Mach, was aimed at establishing this purely observationalist methodology (called 'phenomenalism') as a basis for science. They failed only because they misconceived the basic informational data as 'ideas' (Locke and Berkeley) or 'sense-data' (Mach), instead of what eventually became the general instrument-data of experimental quantum physics. Otherwise, typical of this phenomenalist methodology is the commonsense understanding of light being something which, regardless of what might be claimed as the 'evidence' for it, cannot sensibly be described as 'travelling' at a speed, constant or otherwise, relative to a vacuum.

What is it, then, since it cannot be commonsense, that keeps physics in its present suspended state of irresolution between the two antithetical approaches? It is that there seems to be as much experimental justification for the one theoretical approach as for the other, whereas the way the two theories are currently formulated makes this logically impossible. For instance, according to the 'Realist', Einstein, all physical events are localised in space and connected only by forms of interaction that are limited by the finite speed of light which, mysteriously from any commonsense point of view, is the same for all observers regardless of how they may move or remain at rest with respect to one another. For the 'Positivist', Bohr, on the other hand, in quantum physics there have to be events that are 'non-local', that is, which occur together simultaneously regardless of the distance between them –such as the balancing-moments of systems of planets and galaxies and/or the spin-orientations of elementary particles. So the problem, as usually posed, is that of how the fundamental connections between physical events can be both time-delayed, as according to Einstein and instantaneous, as according to Bohr?

At Keele university, UK, in a rare alliance between Arts and Science (to wit, Philosophy and Mathematics), this problem has been addressed in a completely novel and radical way. This is on the basis of a short correspondence that took place in 1954 between a young Welsh telephone lineman and Albert Einstein. The proposed solution is logically to refute the customary belief that it is contradictory to think of fundamental physical interaction as both limited and unlimited. For instance, as these researchers point out, in the sort of action we see in a movie the motion of an object from A to B consists of a finite-speed sequence of cinematic frames, or 'stills', in which A, B and the travelling object coexist, simultaneously, at every stage in the motion. This, they say, can be used as a logical model for reconciling Bohr's instantaneous action-at-a-distance with Einstein's delayed action, combining the two as parts of the same conceptual package. The constant c, which Einstein traditionally interpreted as the 'finite speed of light' can then be thought of quite simply as a constant relating two sets of circumstantially chosen, traditional units. One of these is the set of units arbitrarily apportioned to the dimension of instantaneous separation between objects in the quantum 'stills', as it were (e.g., metres), and the other is the set no less arbitrarily used to measure the time-sequences of those instantaneous separations (e.g., seconds). These co-exclusive, yet juxtaposed dimensions of all-distance-and-no-time and all-time-and-no-distance are taken to be, respectively, the natural extremes of motion and rest. The constant c is

then as necessary as one that would need to be used in surveying if, by some strange convention, lengths north-south were measured in inches and lengths east-west in metres. In such cases the constant can be eliminated by using the same units in the two dimensions, all metres, say, in the surveying example and all seconds in physics. In the latter case, if metres are insisted upon, then time may be suitably measured in units of metre/c = 3.3 nanoseconds. (This, we understand, is now the custom at the Standards Institute in Paris.)

These arguments, then, reveal that progress in scientific thought has been chronically inhibited by two fallacies. One is to assume that it is logically obligatory to interpret the evidences of what we call the 'speed of light' in that mechanistic or analog sort of way. The other is to assume that in a finite-rate process of energetic interaction between distance-separated bodies, those bodies cannot also, throughout that same interaction, remain instantaneously connected. Recognising these assumptions as fallacies, say the Keele researchers, creates a whole new philosophical 'ball-game'. In the first place it means that in its most parsimonious interpretation, instead of the constant c being a 'velocity' it becomes a ratio of units between an instantaneous extension or 'action-at-a-distance' measured in metres and a time measured in seconds. This logically removes the apparent contradiction between these two assumed 'distinct' kinds of interaction and, in so doing, settles the conflict of interest between the two factions of experimenters, the Bohr group (the Positivists) and the Einstein group (Realists).

Is this, then, just another of those tiresome, iconoclastic 'kicks' against Einstein and Relativity? By no means, say the Keele researchers. In fact, this new way of thinking supports Einstein. They demonstrate, for the mathematicians, that the two co-exclusive (orthogonal) components of a body's motion, that is, the all-distance-and-no-time component and the all-time-and-no-distance component (the latter being the time registered by the travelling body, the so-called proper time) produce between them, by plain Pythagoras, a space-time resultant which is the same as that given by Einstein for the relative, or 'dilated', time of the motion. The distance divided by that dilated time is then what might be called the 'Einsteinian' velocity' of a body, whose unreachable upper limit is c, as opposed to the former, the 'Newtonian' velocity of that same motion, whose upper unreachable limit is infinity.

What is it, then, that is measured in those experiments that are held to have established the 'speed of light'? In the Keele interpretation, every quantum of distant interaction is a quantum of both pure length and pure time in some constant ratio of units which is what Römer actually measured in his original observations of the distance-time changes in the eclipses of Jupiter's principal satellite. It is also what was measured by Bradley in his studies of stellar aberration and by Fizeau and Michelson in their experiments with toothed wheels and revolving mirrors. What these experiments measure, the Keele researchers arue, is the movie-type speed of the process, consisting of statistical numbers of these instantaneous quantum connections between bodies in which energy is redistributed from one, called the 'emitter', to another, called the 'absorber', all in accordance with the statistical thermodynamical law called the law of entropy. In that process, a mirror, say, is activated by the emitter which, in turn, activates some other absorber near the source. The time of that two-step process, as measured in the toothed-wheel and mirror experiments, is that of the total there-and-back distance divided by the time (the relative time) recorded by the experimenter between what he regards as the 'emission' and 'return' of the signal, which in traditional analog fashion has been called the 'speed of light'.

In the digital approach it is these statistical counts of intensity, parallax, etc., by the processional end-user (the observer or observing instrument), that the distances, directions and so on of physical bodies –that is to say, space and its contents– consist, not in any reified self-extended 'void' of analog conception. This, say the new quantum phenomenologists (or normal realists as they prefer to describe themselves) does not mean that the world is some 'Berkeleyan illusion' in which the quantum data just appear out of the blue, as if supplied (as Berkeley would see it) by the hand or mind of God. The source of these data is much the same sub-world of atoms and so on as that postulated in the analog (i.e., Realist) approach. That is to say, the source of the quantum data out of which observational space is 'video-constructed' is a world of particulate masses instantaneously (i.e., non-locally) balanced within an overall angular momentum nexus. This is conserved, in such a way that a quantum change in that angular momentum at one place has to be accompanied by a compensatory change in the angular momentum somewhere else, in the same way that when a new coin is minted, in order to conserve the value of the currency as a whole, another coin of the same value has to be withdrawn somewhere else –and vice versa. In this digital approach, therefore, there are no 'fields' and no 'charges'. Everything is angular momentum of one kind or another (orbital, spin or whatever). And since angular momentum is quantised in units of bar-h (Planck's constant divided by 2 pi) the material sub-world of the discretist is a quantum discretum not a continuum; and therein lies the essential difference between the two approaches to physics, what we have called the analog and the digital.

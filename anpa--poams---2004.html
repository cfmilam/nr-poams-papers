ANPA & POAMS - 2004

N. Vivian Pope — 2004

ANPA What is ANPA? ANPA is the Alternative Natural Philosophy Association. Alternative to what? An alternative to standard orthodox modern physics. In what way? Explanation: Most of modern physics is conceived in what might be called the classical analog style of thinking.

What does this mean? According to the dictionary, analog (or analogue) refers to ‘a physical object or quantity, such as a pointer on a dial or a voltage, used to measure or represent another quantity.’ This is as opposed to digital, described as ‘displaying information as numbers rather than by a pointer moving over a dial’ This same distinction between ‘analog’ and ‘digital’ can be applied to two fundamentally different approaches to physics – or ‘natural philosophy’ as it was once called.

Classical physics is what might be described as an analog approach, in which the explanations of physical phenomena are couched in terms of conceptions of mechanical processes of essentially the same kind as those we observe on levels of ordinary sensation and perception.

In that way of thinking, the ultimate elements of things are microphysical bits of matter moving about in essentially the same space that things move about in on ordinary macrophysical levels – all in ways which it is the purpose of physicists meticulously to observe and measure.

According to that analog agenda, what physics does, essentially, is to describe, record, measure and mathematically predict the behaviour of things like ‘waves’, ‘particles’, ‘wave-media’ – such as fields, ethers and so on – all conceived by analogy with the phenomena to which those words refer in ordinary, everyday sense-experience.

What, then, by contrast, is a ‘digital’ approach? It is an approach to physics which seeks to explain all physical phenomena, not in the usual terms of microphysical objects and processes of the sorts that are familiar to us on ordinary mechanical levels of experience, but in terms of mathematical systems of elements, which elements, in themselves, have none of the usual physical features of motion, duration etc., being the ultimately irreducible bits out of which all those things and their features are constructed.

However, there are two of these ‘digital’ approaches which need logically to be distinguished. The aim of the one is to produce explanations in terms of elements such as pure numbers (e.g., literally digital matrices of purely abstract and intrinsically featureless ones and noughts), whereas the aim of the other is to explain things in terms of systems of actual sensory/instrumental information (what the philosopher, Ernst Mach named ‘sense-data’). In the first of these approaches, the ‘geometry’ of things that we see in the world is an automatic construct out of relations between pure abstract

numbers (or points), in the manner conceived originally by the Pythagoreans. Let this be distinguished as the Numerical Approach. In other approach, geometry is essentially a natural and a priori feature of phenomena, the ultimate elements of which are the endresult of a top-down process of analysis of these phenomena, terminating, empirically, in further-irreducible quantum measures such as h and $h/2\pi$ (Let this be distinguished as the Sense-Datum Approach.)

Both of these two competing ‘digital’ approaches to modern physics have been presented at ANPA. So far, however, the ‘initial focus’ of discussion (according to the Association’s ‘Statement of Purpose’ regularly published in the ANPA Newsletter) has been on the Numerical, or ‘pure numbers’ theory of Fredrick Parker Rhodes. 1 However, this has now been challenged by another, less popular but equally ‘digital’, wellworked-out alternative approach based on the philosophical tradition of Berkeley, Hume and Mach.

This approach is that of ‘phenomenalism’, as modified and updated by the POAMS group 2 along the divergent (anti-idealist) lines established in the 1920s by the commonsense philosopher, G.E. Moore (see following). The proper way forward for the Association, therefore, consistent with its stated aims, should be to give a full and balanced consideration to both these competing alternatives, with a view to deciding which, if either, could provide what Peter Marcer anticipates as ‘the BIG ONE – a theory of Everything’ to which, he suggests, ‘it is therefore worthy of devoting the Group's resources to for the next few years.’ Like Marcer, most of the ANPA members would say that these resources should be provided exclusively for the purpose of exploring the Numerical Alternative.

But of course, it would be a complete travesty for the Group to decide ahead of time, and purely by fiat, which of the two competing alternatives should be exclusively resourced. In a completely fair competition – and if the aim of the Association really is to unearth the truth – then the Group’s resources should, ideally, be shared more or less equally towards exploring both of those logical Alternatives.

However, there is a snag. What militates against this ideal balance of consideration is the intrinsic unpopularity of the one alternative as opposed to the other. This has nothing to do with logical reason but only, basically, with emotion. So far as the Numerical alternative is concerned, a theory that postulates pure numbers as the basis of physical reality poses no threat to the conceptual status quo.

It can be regarded, with equanimity, as being of interest only to Mathematicians, having little or no consequence for our ordinary everyday lives. The same cannot be said, however, of the Sense-Datum Alternative. Why is this? It is because of the logical suggestion that the ultimate elements of physical phenomena are not items of abstract and remote Mathematics, but are items of the phenomena themselves.

This can be frightening. Why? Because it demands a complete revolution in our ordinary commonsense ways of thinking about light. Light is usually conceived in the traditional, analog way, as something travelling in space between the objects of our perceptions and our perceptions of those objects.

This, of course, places physical reality beyond our perceptions, which are accordingly regarded as subjective ‘images’, hence never more than just ‘pale shades’ of the ‘real things’.

But, of course, for centuries, most people have been happy with that. It was a bifurcation of reality that we have got comfortable with, having its conceptual origin in the theoretical mind-matter dualism of Descartes. According to that way of thinking, the physical world is one reality and our perception of it another, and so far as subsequent science and philosophy were concerned, it was always a case of ‘never the twain shall meet’.

Putting the elements of reality into the phenomena breaks down that traditional dualism and puts the elements of the object and of the observation of it together, as part of one and the same physical reality. What is so frightening about this – for some people, at any rate – is that it seems to take physical reality out of the realm of the absolutely objective and put it into the realm of the relative and subjective – that is, as it were, ‘inside our own heads’.

The logical obverse of this is, of course, as Wittgenstein described it, 3 that instead of taking it to mean that we incarcerate the world inside our subjective ‘bottle’, we remove that confining (Cartesian) ‘bottle’ and release ourselves out into the one and only real world, making us, in a sense, as big as everything we may see and comprehend.

The intellectual challenge this presents is, as ever, that of coming to grips with what, since Einstein, has come to be known as Relativity. For some, ‘Relativity’ is synonymous with ‘Subjectivity’, hence is to be dismissed as ‘solipsism’. (Solipsism is the idea, following from Berkeley’s idealistic dictum, ‘To be is to be perceived’, that we can be certain of nothing but our own perceptions (ideas) and that everything else is therefore a dream.)

However, this negative, or down-side interpretation of phenomenalism had already been logically disposed of by G.E. Moore in his famous 1903 essay ‘Refutation of Idealism’.4 This leaves us, by default, with the positive implication of Einsteinian relativism, which was described by G.N.

Lewis in 1926. 5 This, for anyone who really understands Relativity, is that in its own, proper time – that is, intrinsically – a quantum element of light-interaction between one atom and another is absolutely instantaneous. By that same token, the distance over which that interaction takes place, being the product of the constant c and that zero proper-time, is absolutely zero.

In other words, each quantum element of the light by which we perceive an object is, in one and the same instance, as much an element of the object as of our observation of it. The Cartesian barrier between the two (that is, Wittgenstein’s ‘bottle’) is shattered and, at the quantum level of perception, the traditional ‘two worlds’ of ‘Mind’ and ‘Matter’ become one.

The full philosophical explanation of this, developed over more than forty years, is available in the many publications listed on the POAMS website (see below). However, for present purposes, it is sufficient to draw attention to the fact that what is explored at ANPA is not just one ‘digital’ Alternative Natural Philosophy, but two.

Where else is this dual, Digital Alternative to orthodox physics being hosted? Nowhere else, it seems. Does this mean, then, that ANPA is unique? At this time of writing, it would certainly appear so. But what of the imbalance between these two equally ‘digital’ approaches presented at the Association? How should we deal with the emotional disincentive

systematically to explore the one logical alternative, as opposed to the other? Naturally, the approach which receives most attention at ANPA is that on which the Association was initially based, namely, the ‘CH’ (Combinatorial Hierarchies) of Frederick ParkerRhodes. After all, this was the reason that ANPA was established. But the fact of this ‘historical’ precedent cannot be taken as a logical reason for excluding the other digital thesis, to which much less attention (or credence) has been given. The requirements of this second approach, which are to discard the altogether unobservable and metaphysical ‘velocity of light in vacuo’ and re-start with phenomena in the one-andonly real world of everyday experience. are undoubtedly unsettling. This is not only for orthodox theoreticians but also for society generally, demanding, as it does, a retrospective and radical rethink of the empirical data that have led to the standard ‘velocity of light’ interpretation that is now customarily regarded as ‘commonsense’. Nevertheless, the proponents of this second approach feel that a fairer balance in the presentation of the two approaches could lead to a greater understanding and appreciation of the second, residual part of the digital Alternative Natural Philosophy that the Association was designed to explore. Viv Pope, Nov. 11th, 2004.

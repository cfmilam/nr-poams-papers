"When we come into the world we are obviously not confronted with just a chaos of point-like or pixel-like sensations – far less an awareness of atoms, sub-atomic particles, pure numbers or whatever – and then presented with the task of having to build out of these our ordinary everyday experience. Experience is essentially holistic. It consists, right from the start, of whole organised systems of sensation, or impressions. Nor is it ever completely what William James called 'a great buzzing and booming confusion'. All animals are born with organised systems of sensation. Anyone who has seen a calf being born, for instance, perceives that the animal, when it emerges, is by no means confused about its behavioural intention of carrying out the highly coordinated task of gathering its strength and standing up.

"Where all science and philosophy start, then, is in similarly self-organised, albeit higher-order systems of sensation, with the task of making as much logical sense of them as we can, with a maximum of sufficiency and consistency and a minimum of redundant language, or jargon. Science never, in fact, begins with 'sense-data' (or 'sense-atoms') or with elementary ideas of logic, mathematics or whatever, with which to set about rationally constructing or reconstructing the world on those (assumed) absolute bases. That was a peculiar ideal of science conceived by certain physics-philosophers of the nineteen-twenties, who very soon had to declare the ideal abortive. There is no way of dissociating relata from relations and then, starting with the relata, reconstructing the relations out of them. To imagine that there is such a way, is obviously absurd. [Imagine, for instance, cutting out the characters of a newspaper story with scissors and then putting them under a microscope to reveal the 'structural' or 'combinatorial' principles which made them form the story!]

"The impressions we are born with, and even those we acquire with the best of education and social conditioning, are therefore always finite and can never be guaranteed immunity from the likelihood of having to be revised. Our first impressions, for instance, that the whale is a fish, that the earth is flat, that the sun, planets and stars revolve around us, that time is the same everywhere in the universe, that matter is immutable ... and so on, have all had to be revised – indeed, radically in these cases. So our impressions are inherently and inexorably unreliable.

In having no recourse but to depend on impressions we are poised, so to speak, "between the devil and the deep blue sea".

"Rationalists, dissatisfied with this inherent uncertainty and frailty of our human sense-apparatuses have sought, from time immemorial, to short-circuit them with systems of pure reason (i.e., pure logic and/or mathematics). To these Rational systems they ascribe a Platonic or Pythagorean validity transcending all the failings of human sensation and cognition. So instead of starting where everyone else starts, in ordinary perception and language, the Rationalist presumes to start in some fancied God's-eye-view of an absolute 'reality' which, like that of the atomist Democritus, is supposed to underlie our perceptions or, like that of the Idealist, Plato, wholly transcend them. The fallacy of Rationalism, however, is that all its projections are inescapably human and therefore made always from within the very same realm of frailty and uncertainty they seek to escape. What makes Rationalism such a "con", therefore, is that whilst being human it purports to be superhuman – in effect, God-given and infallible.

"As a basis for science, then, Rationalism is eminently unworkable. Recognising this has led to the "idiosyncratic theory" to which Bastin and Kilmister refer. This is the theory that my colleague, Dr. Osborne, and I know as Normal Realism. Its "idiosyncrasy" is that it eschews dogmatic metaphysics and rests, as all truly empirical science must rest, in the continued gathering of observational and experimental information and its articulation by the most logically efficient use of an evolving common language. [A critic says that this statement seems to contradict the previous one (third paragraph back) that 'There is no way of dissociating relata from relations and then, starting with the relata, reconstructing the relations out of them.' In Normal Realism, however, such abortive dissociations are expressly avoided, so there is no contradiction.] Now it scarcely needs to be said that rejecting Rationalism is not the same as rejecting reason. Indeed, reason, in the shape of logic and mathematics, forms the very basis of Normal Realism. But the 'idiosyncrasy' Bastin and Kilmister see in this Pope-Osborne approach is that where it starts, in the matter of reason, is in the ordinary everyday world of commonsense

'Of course, if by "commonsense", here, we meant some oracular, all-time description of the world established by literary scholars and paragons, then our approach would definitely be idiosyncratic. To assume, however, that this is what is meant by "commonsense" would be flagrantly to misrepresent Normal Realism. What we mean by the word is nothing more nor less than the sort of developing common consensus that is sought on issues discussed by people of normal, unimpaired intelligence when presented with the fullest knowledge of the facts and the soundest of ordinary logical arguments. [] Our aim, then, with the very best use of ordinary logic and mathematics, is simply to do what Socrates did with the circumstantial precepts of his time: that is, to process-out the contradictions in that intellectual raw-material of impressions and opinions towards a purified state of true knowledge – that is to say, the most refined, most sufficient and efficient, most coherent and consistent set of ideas that, by common consensus, can be obtained. Truth and reality are seen, in this way, not as cosmic verities that we can access directly at any time by methods of 'pure unadulterated reason' (as though that were ever possible) but simply as what remains in current common language when as many errors and contradictions as can be found are processed-out – that is, by means of Socratic-type arguments of the sort I am using in this paper. 'So if this neo-Socratic approach, called Normal Realism, is to be judged "idiosyncratic", then so must every argument of ordinary commonsense and empirical science; and if, as some carpers have said, we (that is, Osborne and I) seem to be "building the world out of language" then that, surely, is what every person in recorded history has done, from the cavemen who represented the world with their grunts and depictions on cave walls to modern scientists who talk about quarks and quasars. So all that the "idiosyncratic theory" turns out to be, in short, is simply taking the language as we find it – the language of physics in this case – and analysing it for logical and mathematical consistency and efficiency. Calling this "linguistic analysis" unfortunately sparks off, in the minds of those who remember it, the sort of philosophy that was promulgated in the '60s, which somehow managed to make a complete "pig's ear" out of the whole issue. Those slavish and unthinking followers of Wittgenstein, among whom I sojourned at Swansea, made it seem they were saying that all physical objects, from sticks and stones to atoms and galaxies are no more than "bits of ordinary language", which was as glaring a misuse of ordinary language as might be conceived.

'The founders, then, of modern 'Linguistic' philosophy could never, really, have been saying anything quite so absurd. What they were doing [] was emphasising – or, perhaps over-emphasising – the undeniable fact that it is language which organises our thinking and perceiving, so that without language, cognition as we understand it is impossible. Language, one might say, forms the template, or "stencil", through which, via our senses and instrument-systems, nature "air-brushes" its images onto our minds. And if that stencil becomes clogged-up or congested, then so, inevitably, do our ideas. In effect, all that our dreaded "Linguistic" philosophers were saying, and which is surely incontestable, is that because language organises and directs our thinking, to misuse language is to create obscurity. And that, basically, is what the "idiosyncratic theory" of Pope and Osborne is all about; that is, employing the Socratic method to clean-up our language-templates – in physics in particular – so as to clear the subject of its baked-on and obscuring historical accretions. "natural philosophy", as compared with the non-naturalness of some metaphysical "God's-eye-view" approaches to physics. And in the light of this question of "naturalness" or otherwise, I would now like to discuss the question of numbers in relation to the Combinatorial Hierarchy.

'To a Normal Realist it comes as no surprise that however many bits you reduce a real thing to, the numbers of those bits always add up to the original whole. For instance, if you start with a metre rule, then no matter how many millimetres, nanometres, micrometres or whatever you divide it into, putting them together will always give you the original metre. But if you assume that your metre rule is infinitely divisible, then that is literally the same as supposing that its ultimate divisions are infinitesimals, the sum of which is always zero, so with that assumption your metre rule vanishes. Logically, then, you have to assume that at some stage in any physical analysis you will reach a level of what might be called "terminals" of reduction. Otherwise you lose everything. Infinitely reduced, the whole firmament disappears into its own fundament, like the Oozlam Bird in the infamous adage.

'With that proviso, then, that the process of reduction cannot be infinite, it nevertheless makes good sense for the Normal Realist to contemplate reducing things right down to whatever might prove to be their ultimate microphysical parts (terminal reducibles) and then determine how these parts join together to make the original whole. This makes sense because, of course, things that reduce to the same elements can be reconstituted and reconstructed, in an engineering-like way and, if necessary, repaired, or even transformed to create other things – some that perhaps might never before have existed. One may even sensibly think of these reconstructions occurring naturally, without human intervention, by random mutation and evolution. So, I repeat, it makes very good sense – one might suitably say good commonsense – to analyse things right down, as far as possible, to their ultimately irreducible microphysical parts and then determine the geometrical, mechanical, chemical, biological and other physical interrelations between those parts in making up the whole. All this, of course, involves mathematics. One can no more imagine the various bits and pieces of things reduced in this way being devoid

'What does not make good sense, however, from the Normal Realist point of view is to project ideal levels of reduction where the world we started with, as our essential given, conceptually evaporates, leaving us nothing to contemplate but a chaos of completely self-sufficient and dissociated simples. And the idea of such dissociated simples waiting around in a primordial void in a primordial time – or, worse, a primordial pre-time – waiting to form relations with one another, by chance, by determinate mechanisms, "combinatorial" principles or whatever, to form the world as we know it, is a nonsense in neon lights.

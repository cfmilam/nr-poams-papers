ANPA & POAMS - Two systems of Combinatorics 2002

N. Vivian Pope — 2002

ANPA 2002 ANALYSIS

ANPA and POAMS (Two systems of Combinatorics) Viv Pope

1. Introduction An interesting discussion I had at the ANPA banquet at Jesus College, Cambridge, with Mike Horner, Lou Kauffmann and Doug Matzke, has prompted me to write this piece. It is a response to something on which we all four eventually agreed.

In vino veritas, I had remarked how it seemed to me that no-one at ANPA seemed to know how to handle philosophical questions. The answer I received from my companions at the table surprised me. They entirely agreed with my diagnosis. It was not that anyone regarded my questions as nonsensical.

Rather, it was that to answer them was sometimes so difficult that they would take weeks to consider. So what I had seen as evasion in the answers my questions elicited was, I was told, the best sort of respectable noise that speakers could come up with as an alternative to lapsing into sheer embarrassed silence.

I explained that what my questions were designed to express was what is called ‘Socratic irony’. They were, typically the probing sorts of enquiries designed to reveal faults in illconsidered arguments and statements. This, I assured my companions, was not aimed at disconcerting and embarrassing the speakers, or simply to be horrid towards them, as I explained to Nicky Graves-Gregory.

It was in order, kindly and respectfully, to disabuse them of fallacies in their reasoning which they would not, knowingly wish to entertain. This classical philosophical approach is surely not to be despised in an association with ‘Philosophy’ central to its title.

The discussion then turned, under Mike Horner’s organisational direction, to the serious consideration that some future meeting should be arranged in which I – and perhaps others – as representative of POAMS, should be allowed sufficient time for this ‘lateral’ group of combinatoricists to present its case.

Unanimously, it was decided that a mere twenty minutes or so was hopelessly insufficient to inform the members of ANPA what the POAMS movement was all about. In other words, the agreed suggestion was that my irritating role as a philosophical ‘gadfly’ to ANPA should be reversed and that instead of me doing the questioning, an interrogatory session might be organised in which I (or whoever) should subject ourselves, as a presenters of POAMS, to a kind of ‘inquisition’ by ANPA members as to what POAMS is all about and why Pierre Noyes, hearing my talk at PIRT, in 1990, enlisted me as a member of ANPA.

The following, then, is something in the way of an encapsulation of POAMS, explaining its relevance to ANPA in the way that Pierre originally intuited. In my view, the two approaches to discreteness in physics should be systematically combined. Let’s see what the rank-and-file ANPA members think, eh!

2. Combinatorics There are two kinds of curiosity about numbers. They both start with physical objects. They both see that these objects form numerical and geometrical arrangements, and they both see that individual objects may be split into parts which are similarly arranged. The essential difference between the two kinds of curiosity begins when the one wonders how far the things we start with in the world may be divided up and counted in that way, while the other abstracts the number systems that we have evolved in counting and dividing things, and studies their arrangements as pure forms, regardless of whatever material content these forms might originally have had.

For these Mathematical Purists, the measures that engineers, physicists and surveyors start with and take for granted, such as mass, length and time are intrinsically puzzling. Like their precursors, the Pythagoreans, their aim is to ascertain what kinds of forms, or ‘sets’, purely abstract and dimensionless points or ciphers may make in themselves.

Two points make a line, three a triangle … and so on until, hopefully, every possible arrangement and arrangement of arrangements is defined. This, it is assumed, is the abstract cosmical principle on which the universe is designed. If we work at it long enough and diligently enough, the Purists say, we will see how the world is constructed and how it functions without having to do the sorts of experiments and theoretical guesswork that the others in the opposite, materialistic camp have to do in their practical dealings with things.

Like the Greek Rationalists, Plato, Parmenides and Pythagoras, the Purists believe none of that preoccupation with materiality would be necessary if everyone did what the Pythagoreans did – that is, set themselves apart from the workaday world and, in cloistered seclusion, religiously study the Pure Forms, or ‘Universals’, as they came to be known.

These Rationalists, the originators of Pure Mathematics, were referred to, by Plato, as the ‘Gods’ among his contemporaries. By complete contrast to these ‘Gods’ were those Plato called the ‘Giants’. These were the Empiricists, the sorts of scientists of the day who tended to eschew abstraction and rely, instead, on observation and experiment.

In Plato’s words, they ‘have to examine every stick and stone in order to be assured of what it is and what it does’. These ‘Giants’ of nascent science were led by Plato’s friend and pupil, Aristotle. There is a famous statue showing the two thinkers engaged in an argument where, while Plato is pointing heavenward, Aristotle points downwards towards the ground.

This signified the essential difference between the two philosophers, that while for Aristotle the Forms existed nowhere but in things (in Latin, Universalia in rebus), for Plato they existed prior to and independently of things (Universalia ante res).

Now this also indicates the main difference between ANPA1 and POAMS.2 It is that whereas in the twenty-four year evolution of ANPA the premiums placed on the ‘natural selection’ of members have been on those of the Rationalist persuasion described above, POAMS, in its fourteen-years representation at the Association has always stressed arguments of the opposite, Empiricist kind.

In other words, whereas ANPA, in the manner of Plato’s ‘Gods’, fosters the purist study of ‘combinatorics’ (mathematical systems for their own sake), POAMS – whose representatives at ANPA have scarcely ever exceeded more than just one – places its opposite emphasis on the more materialistic preoccupation of Plato’s ‘Giants’.

In past ANPA meetings, this infiltration of Empiricism into an Association dedicated to developing systems of Purist Mathematics has, predictably, created tension. But why? The two kinds of curiosity about numbers are by no means incommensurate. They both have the same starting point in the numerical systems that are manifest in the world around us.

However, while they are not necessarily incommensurate, neither are they necessarily always commensurate. This is because, in many instances of analysis, what we end up with, in the way of patterns of addition, division and so on, depends very much on what we start with.

For example, one of the first Empiricists to notice the discreteness of numbers in nature was Johann Balmer. In 1885 he announced his discovery of a basic formula which, by methods of sheer trial and error he had found to fit the distributions of discrete lines in atomic spectra.

Thus, in complete contrast to the way in which John Dalton, in 1803, had shown that atoms exist and that in chemistry they combine in simple numerical ratios by weight, in 1885 Balmer showed, in effect, that these atoms are not the simple numerical entities that Dalton had envisaged after the fashion of Democritus but that they have internal constitutions characterised by the series of lines they display on a spectrograph.

How could those spectral series and sets of series have ever been anticipated in terms of the simple number-systems, of points, lines, tetrahedrons etc, of pure combinatorics?3 Without that empirical input from the likes of Balmer, we might never have been able to construct the knowledge of the internal compositions of atoms that is now so essential for modern technology, industry and society.

And what about motion? The Greek Rationalists were notoriously unable to deal with this phenomenon in their purely Mathematical terms. Indeed, so stubbornly did motion resist their attempts to explain it that they decided it was unreal – a mere apparition, or ‘convention’ in the language of the time.

Even now, the Rationalists of modern microphysics find it virtually impossible to explain motion at the lowest microphysical level. The reason for this, of course, is that the most essential measure-component of motion, namely, time, is not extrapolable from simple uninterpreted and dimensionless dots in the way that was envisaged by the Pythagoreans.

Rationalists have tried unsuccessfully to extend the formless dots of Pythagorean ratiocination with equally dimensionless and formless events. However, unlike a mathematical point, an ‘event’ is not easily described. Indeed, it has found no place whatsoever in Pure Mathematics – other than by being jammed in arbitrarily, by prior presumption from ordinary experience.

In fact, the concept of time can come from nowhere but ordinary temporal experience, where it is one of the indissociable dimensional components of ordinary existence, duration and motion. By indissociable I mean that in motion, which is surely the most natural and basic of all phenomena, there is no single one of those dimensions or measure-categories, of mass, length and time, that it makes sense to speak of without implicitly involving all of the others.

Nature presents us with these dimensions, on every occasion, as a completely integrated holistic package in a way which it has not been possible to construct out of sheer numbers and arrangements thereof. Now from a purely pragmatical point of view, there are, obviously, advantages to be gained from both kinds of curiosity concerning the occurrences of numbers in nature, particularly when these approaches are employed together in the way that is perfectly suitable in, say, a college of Technology.

However, in the context of the ‘Natural Philosophy’ signified by its title, ANPA is not concerned in that way with pragmatics. It behoves us, therefore, in the sorts of discussions which are scheduled at ANPA, to consider very carefully how best, at the logical and philosophical level, to approach the science of numbers.

The first thing to note, in this philosophical context, is that the two approaches to numbers, namely, the Rationalist and the Empiricist do not logically exclude each other. Nevertheless, they may become alienated from each other by partisan entrenchment.

The fact is that if nature is the sort of algorithm that the Pythagoreans imagined, then with all the computer power that is now available to us, that algorithm should, by now, surely have been solved – or, at least, there should have been enough success in that direction to capture the public interest to an extent that is at least

The fact that in its twenty-four years of purist preoccupation with number systems, ANPA has achieved nothing like that measure of success surely suggests that the Alternative Natural Philosophy Association needs all the help it can get from a serious infusion of other Alternatives than just the one that is signified by its title.

Now here is a question: Is our modern science good or bad? How can we possibly tell unless we have something to compare it with? For all we know, all its ‘electrons’, ‘protons’, ‘photons’ ‘quarks’ and so on could be chimeras, as unfamiliar to nature as they are familiar to modern physics.

Plainly, then, it is a fallacy to seek Mathematical systems to fit a physical science which may well be, in some unsuspected way, fundamentally flawed. It is no more than a collection of human ideas, after all! Ideally, then, Rationalism and Empiricism should combine forces in any serious attempt to crack nature’s code; otherwise, in ‘going it alone’, combinatorics, might end up as little more than a Mathematical equivalent of the game of Trivial Pursuit.

For instance, not knowing what Römer eventually discovered, it is extremely unlikely that the Pythagoreans, even if they had lived till now, would have been able to anticipate what Einstein discovered about time-dilation or to project what we now know about atoms without what Balmer’s formula intimated to us about the spectral series.

Yet the irony is that if only Pythagoras could have known what was discovered empirically by Olaus Römer many centuries later, namely, that observational distances and times are related in the constant ratio of units c, then he would surely have seen that his three-dimensional geometrical formalism was implicitly fourdimensional.

In that case, relativistic time-dilation, as we now know it since Einstein, might have been, among the Greeks, a commonplace, extra-dimensional extension of Pythagoras’ theorem. Also, as a further consequence of the same combinatorics, Balmer’s formula for the discrete frequencies of atomic spectra, plus the orbit-levels of the atom described by Niels Bohr, could, in principle, have been deduced directly from this Pythagorean time-derivative, bypassing all our historical preoccupations with the ‘electrodynamical’ theories of experimentalists such Faraday, Maxwell and Lorentz – and, indeed, Einstein. Such a statement cannot, obviously, go unchallenged, so here is its justification.

3. Time-dilation, as a combinatoric deduction from Pythagoras, direct Just how easily Pythagoras might have anticipated Einstein may be demonstrated as follows. If time is taken (pace Römer) as a fourth dimension, related to the three spatial dimensions in the same orthogonal way that those dimensions are related to each other, then if, in the time t ticked by a clock, that clock moves a distance s, the time of that motion relative to the observer will be the geometrical resultant of the two orthogonal components t and s/c, according to Pythagoras’ own formula $$t_R = \sqrt{(s/c)^2 + t^2} \quad \text{(1)}$$

where $t_R$ is the relative, or resultant time and c is Römer’s constant converting measures in metres into measures in seconds. (Note that c, here, is a dimensional conversion factor, not the Einsteinian ‘velocity of light in vacuo’.4) That this formula is the same as Einstein’s can be seen if, for s in the equation we substitute its equivalent expression $vt_R$, where v is the velocity of the moving clock relative to the observer. This produces $$t_R = \sqrt{(vt_R/c)^2 + t^2} \quad \text{(2)}$$

which, by transposition and simplification, becomes the Einsteinian time-dilation formula: $t_R = t/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ \quad \text{(3)}

Now no-one of the ANPA-type persuasion should be appalled by this seemingly Draconian collapsing of our scientific history. This, surely, is what ‘combinatorics’ is about, namely, the mathematical rationalisation of nature in a purely formal way, enabling us to identify and avoid conceptual blind alleys down which the experimentalists (Plato’s materialistic ‘Giants’) may inadvertently have been – and maybe still are – led. We should therefore scarcely be surprised if, in the sort of research that is conducted at ANPA, some of the most venerated theoretical excursions in our scientific history prove to be redundant. Indeed, in a purist, combinatoric review of things, it would be surprising, to say the least, if at least some of our most cherished historical notions of physics did not turn out, in this way, to have been no more than intellectual caprices.

4. The atomic spectrum by combinatorial deduction from time-dilation, direct It lends point to what was said earlier, that this Rationalistic derivation of the Einstein formula would scarcely have been possible without Römer’s empirical input in discovering the distance-time constant c. Nor is it at all likely that the rationalising of the discrete frequencies in atomic spectra would have been accomplished without Balmer’s completely trial-and-error discovery of that spectral series. However, with his empirical formula as something to aim for, a combinatorial deduction of those spectral series from the Pythagorean time-derivative, equation (3), is a simple matter, as may now be demonstrated. For instance, in that formula, viz.: $$t_R = \frac{t}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} \quad \text{(3)}$$

let t be, ex hypothesi, some natural whole time-period, of value unknown in conventional units. This makes tR some integer multiple n of that time-period, so that (3) becomes $nt = t/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ \quad \text{(4)}

Compare H. Bondi, who writes ‘Any attempt to measure the velocity of light is … not an attempt to measure the velocity of light but an attempt at ascertaining the length of the standard metre in Paris in terms of time-units.’ (Bondi, H., Assumption and Myth in Physical Theory, CUP, 1965, p. 28.

Expressing this equation in terms of $v^2$ then gives: $v^2 = c^2(1 - 1/n^2)$ \quad \text{(5)}

Now v2 is, dimensionally, e/m, where e is the potential energy of some unknown mass m. Substituting accordingly produces: e = $mc^2$[1 - (1/n^2)] \quad \text{(6)}

Now these potential energies all have the same central zero of reference. And since the implicit t in this equation is the period of some single cycle of energy, that reference-zero is a point around which the cycles are circumscribed in the manner of the Bohr atom – indeed, with exactly the same distribution of main series and intermediate sub-series: e = $mc^2$[(1/n_1^2) - (1/n_2^2)] (7), where $n_1$ and $n_2$ are, respectively, the fixed and running terms. Dividing (8) throughout by Planck’s constant h turns that equation into a formula for frequency, , viz.: limit [(1/n_1^2) - (1/n_2^2)] (8). And here, for comparison is the empirical formula, taken from a physics textbook, 5 for the full Balmer series for the basic hydrogen spectrum, as subsequently developed by Lyman, Paschen, Bracket and Pfund:

R[(1/n2 – (1/m$^2$)] \quad \text{(9)}

In this textbook formula, m is a whole number larger than n, is the frequency in conventional wave-number units, and R is the empirically determined Rydberg constant, the limit of the equation in those same conventional units, Now it can scarcely be doubted that apart from the difference in nomenclature (being expressed in ‘wave-number’ units of metre -1 instead of hertz, and with n and m in place of our $n_1$ and $n_2$) this empirical formula and our combinatorially deduced formula (7) are the same. In that case, with this empirical input supplied by the measured value of the Rydberg constant ($R_H$ = $1.0967757 \times 10^{7}$ m-1 for simple hydrogen) we can deduce the value of m in equation (6). It is the maximum massincrement of the cyclic configuration, for n in that equation approaching infinity (i.e., m = $hcR_H/c^2$ = 2. $424153451 \times 10^{-35}$ kg). This raises the question as to what it is that increases its mass in that way with the absorption of spectral energy. The answer has to be a mass of some kind – let’s call it m – which, in this simplest case of hydrogen, is in an automatically paired angular momentum relation with another mass, M, forming the nuclear countermass of the cyclic configuration. And since the sum of the two masses, plus the incremental mass m, cannot exceed the mass of the hydrogen atom determined empirically by Dalton, the counterbalancing mass M cannot be infinite. It must therefore wobble around the centre of motion in its angular momentum relation with m. Since the value

However, there is a more straightforward way in which the values of m and M can be ascertained.

For instance, we now know the value of t in equation (3). It is (for hydrogen) $1/cR_H$, which is $3.041315514 \times 10^{-16}$ sec. We also now know that at this fundamental level, all values $t_R$ in (3) are integer multiples n of this basic unit.

Multiplying the energy $mc^2$ in (6) and (7) by these units, we therefore have $nmc^2/cR_H$, which gives us integer multiples, n of Planck’s quantum of action, h. Dividing this action by 2 then gives the same integer multiples of a basic quantum of angular momentum of the masses m and M about each other.

The picture we derive is therefore that of an accumulation, relative to the observer, of angular momentum in integer multiples of a fundamental quantum $h/2$, and as shedding and absorbing relativistic mass-energy in the discrete fractions of the coefficient $mc^2$ indicated by equations (6) and (7).

What else can we glean from this combinatorial process? One thing we know, from pure ratiocination, is that for multiples n approaching infinity, the angular momentum of m and M is such that the two particles are virtually separated and in that state can be measured empirically.

This has, of course, been done, and the values are: m = $9.1093897 \times 10^{-31}$ kg and M = $1.6726231 \times 10^{-27}$ kg. We also now know the value of $mc^2$, in equations (6) and (7), which is hc$R_H$ = $2.178720468 \times 10^{-18}$ J. If this energy E is equivalent to the kinetic energy, $K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ of m in its orbit around the central mass M (that is, $K/c^2$ is the incremental mass m), then the orbital velocity, v, of m is v = $\sqrt{2hcR_H/m}$ = $2.187112455 \times 10^{6}$ m s$^{-1}$.

The length of the orbit of m is therefore = $vt = v/cR_H$ = $6.65169904 \times 10^{-10}$ m, and dividing this by 2 gives the radius of m’s orbit as $r_m$ = $1.058650782 \times 10^{-10}$ m. This is just twice the official Bohr radius.

Conclusion This configuration of masses and mass-energies, which might be described as an observational quantum accumulator, has been derived by pure ratiocination by extending the theorem of Pythagoras to include time, and therefore motion, as one of its orthogonal dimensions. The model thus produced turns out to be almost identical to that of the hydrogen atom which Bohr constructed on the basis of traditional fieldelectrodynamics. It will be noted, however, that none of those traditional concepts and associated measures have been employed here. There has been no talk, for instance, of ‘electrons’, ‘protons’, ‘charges’, ‘field forces’, or whatever. Nothing is involved but plain masses and the observational motions thereof. Like the straightforward deduction of relativistic time-dilation from the Pythagorean formula, bypassing Einstein’s famous Second Postulate regarding the ‘speed of light in vacuo’, the basic formula for the series of discrete frequencies in atomic spectra has now been deduced directly from the Pythagorean time-dilation formula, with the barest minimum of empirical input. Of course, those who wish simply to defeat the argument will say that the Bohr model is now ‘old hat’, that it has been superseded by the later, probabilistic, wave-functional model of Schrödinger, et al. which incorporates the indeterminacy principle of Heisenberg. Also, these defeatists will point out that even in the basic hydrogen spectrum, there are fine structures that cannot be accounted for in terms of Bohr’s very simple, circulinear, electrodynamical model. Nevertheless, that model still stands as the definitive basis on which modern atomic theory has been built. Nor is there any logical reason to suppose that all the scientific developments that have

accrued on the basis of Bohr’s atomic model could not have been made more simply and efficaciously on the basis of the more conceptually economical, combinatorial model of the Pythagorean quantum accumulator that has now been described. (Indeed, many such developments have already been proposed by the POAMS group in their conferences and publications.)

To make redundant great chunks of our circumstantial scientific history, by replacing it with clear-cut combinatorial logic, is the common aim of both ANPA and POAMS. In such an enterprise, what would be the good of everyone trying to do this and then being frightened if someone succeeds?

To the extent, then, that POAMS has succeeded in compressing the history of physics in this combinatorial way, it should surely be of vital concern to both parties to form and maintain, in opposition to the inevitable conservationist back-pressure, an alliance in pursuit of their common aim of rationalising and condensing our scientific history – or, at least, economising, so far as possible, on the inevitable conceptual excesses and caprices of pure, unadulterated. empiricism (what the late, lamented POAMS supporter, Alan Smart, called ‘poke and hope experimentalism’).

Another concern, surely, for the health and survival of ANPA, should be to avoid sticking to just one stereotypical approach to combinatorics and to consider, very seriously, the possible validity of lateral alternatives to its original ParkerRhodes version of the ‘CH’ (Combinatorial Hierarchies).

All the keys, it is said, hang not at one man’s girdle’. And, certainly, more than just one locksmith will be required to unlock nature’s numerical secrets. Now none of this message should be confused with mere iconoclasm. Partisan loyalty and tradition may well be considered sacrosanct is some areas of human endeavour.

Even in areas of didactic science, veneration of ‘The Greats’ undoubtedly plays its part in stimulating students and maintaining standards of academic excellence for them to follow. However, as soon as it can be clearly demonstrated that there are truths of nature that are best understood when stripped of their traditional adornments, then that is surely the time for those adornments to be removed from serious science and respectfully consigned to the cabinets of scientific history.

If the aim of combinatorics at ANPA is of this careful and conscientious, a-historical sort, then members will surely appreciate that what POAMS has achieved, as described above,6 has gone a long way in that direction, and that far from being hostile to ANPA, it may well be one of its closest friends. ■

WARNING T.H. Huxley said ‘All truths begin as heresies’. What you are about to hear is not for the faint-hearted. Indeed, a professor from Bristol has remarked that it should be preceded with a ‘Government health-warning’.

Now why this warning? Well, as you know, most of us readily assume that all is well with our understanding of the world, and it is disconcerting to find that, as sometimes happens, the very best and most authoritative ideas we receive from the trusted experts may need to be overhauled. What is more disconcerting – dangerous, even – is to find for oneself that what the experts say is fundamentally flawed or that there is some logical interpretation of things that is far simpler and more conceptually efficient than that which is taken as standard. What should we do in such cases? Should we keep that discovery safely ‘under our hats’ or should we speak out and be damned? In this talk we take this latter, altogether heretical course. Now that heresy all started, would you believe, at the Swansea Astronomical Society in its association with Ted Featherstone. This was in the 1950s. My father, Donald Pope was an amateur astronomer. Don was a state-of-the art telephone Engineer, an expert in the maintenance and repair of the Strowger electromechanical switching systems that preceded the advent of modern computers. As well as being an amateur astronomer, he was also a philosopher, an early prophet of modern space-exploration, a predictor of the Internet and, all-in-all, an extremely far-sighted, liberal-minded free thinker. At this time he was a close friend and associate of Ted Featherstone, who was an early leading light of the Society. Now I have to confess that I was an Eleven-Plus failure (twice); a rebel and general ‘bad-lad’ of the district, an inveterate non-scholar and non-academic, having wandered around the countryside and rummaging in scrap-yards for interesting bits and bobs when everyone else was at school. This made me a great disappointment to my father, Don, especially when he discovered my truancy. After leaving school (officially) my job, following in his footsteps, became that of a telephone lineman for what was then the GPO. My first spare-time contribution to astronomy was, as I think I already said, to mix concrete for the base of the Society’s telescope. Sadly, my father died, two months before the opening of the Observatory * * * * and just days before his fiftieth* birthday.) * (Fig. 6)

My first view through the Society’s new Newtonian nine-inch tracking telescope at Tycoch astounded me. I could scarcely believe it. This led me to a lifelong interest in Astronomy, Physics, and Philosophy of Science. It was at an Astronomical Society lecture, presided over by Ted Featherstone in the Royal Institution (the Museum), Swansea in 1952, that I heard for the first time about Einstein and Relativity. This lecture left me profoundly disturbed. I had automatically assumed, as just ‘plain commonsense’, that time is the same everywhere, all over the universe, and that everything endures at the same rate. To be told, in effect, that my ‘commonsense assumption’ was wrong seemed to me quite absurd. But Einstein’s Theory of Relativity was big news at the time. So I studied it in the books my father bought for me from ‘Ralph’s’ famous bookshop near Swansea High Street Station. From these it appeared that relative motion between observers, even if these observers were identical twins, made them age at different rates. However, in trying to understand this I failed abysmally (as had everyone else, it seemed, including even my sointelligent father). I bent his ear about it continually and – in exasperation, no doubt – he jocularly advised me to ‘write to Einstein about it’. And in my incredible innocence, I did just that. In my letter I asked Einstein how it was possible that twin observers, after travelling relatively to each other could, paradoxically, each end up older than the other. To my father’s absolute astonishment, Einstein replied. Here is the letter I received. (Fig. 7)

The letter from Einstein

Now do you understand this letter? No? Well, at the time, neither did I. Nor did anyone else that I knew. Nevertheless, everyone thought it was wonderful, so it was put on display at one of the Swansea Society’s Open Days at the Royal Institution, where it was pounced upon by the local press. (It has since appeared in other newspapers elsewhere, by the way, and on TV, where, again, it was applauded but never understood.) Here is how it was first featured locally, in the South Wales Evening Post:- Fig. 8.

You might like to know that the original Einstein letter is now held by the Archives Department, County Hall, Swansea, where it is stored as a treasured item. On request, it can be read, but, they tell me, only under strict supervision. The way this came about was when my sister-in law, Claire Edwards read in the paper that a similar letter written by Einstein had been valued at some thousands of pounds. She urged me to contact the County Archivist, who, seeing the letter, was appalled that it had been kicking around in the atmospherically uncontrolled conditions of my attic. So it was duly spirited away to where it now resides in the County Archives. Now this was all very well, but at the time, as I say, I didn’t understand Einstein’s reply. This worried me. But then I was reminded of one of my grandmother’s old philosophical sayings: ‘All the keys hang not at one man’s girdle.’ I therefore decided that if what Einstein had discovered was a natural truth, then that truth should be discoverable by others in other ways. I was no mathematician so I went about it in the following way: On sick leave after a climbing accident in work I was thinking hard about this. Messing

Distance in units of 186,000 miles about with a ruler and dividers, I made an exciting discovery. (Fig. 9)

Time in units of seconds Dashing in from the garden where I had been scribbling, I mystified my wife by shouting: ‘Mary, I’ve just discovered the length of a second!’ This ‘Eureka!’ discovery was very simple. I had tried all sorts of different units in scaling this sort of diagram. What I discovered, by simple trial and error, was that if the distance travelled by the body, represented in the vertical dimension of a page, was measured in units of 186,000 miles and the time it takes, measured in the horizontal dimension in units of seconds, then plotting the motion as a gradient against those two dimensions gives a measure in seconds which is precisely equivalent to the relatively stretched, or ‘dilated’ time of Einstein’s Relativity. Yes, it really was that simple! Measuring the lengths of various motion gradients meticulously with a ruler and comparing them with the lengths along the base-line (all measured in seconds) gave exactly the same time-dilations as were given by Einstein’s formula.

I was naturally delighted. I had circumvented all the abstruse mathematics of Einstein’s theory with nothing more than an ordinary ruler and dividers! But then something gradually dawned on me. It was that in this diagram, there was no way of depicting the ‘speed of light’, which is a central feature of Einsteinian Relativity.

In Einstein’s Theory it is axiomatic that: The velocity of light in vacuo is a constant, c, which is the same for all observers, whether relatively stationary or moving. This, I had been told, is the well-known Second Postulate of Einstein’s Theory of Relativity.

But nowhere in my diagram was there any ‘velocity c’ portrayed. Any velocity whatsoever could be portrayed in my diagram except that of the alleged ‘velocity of light’. This was disturbing. Was it, I wondered, a failure of my discovery. Yet everything else fitted so nicely and logically.

So I decided to forget about ‘light-velocity’ for the while and carry on regardless with my philosophical investigations into the relativity of space and time. Then after one of the Society’s meetings, I mentioned this concern to Ted Featherstone. He told me not to worry about it.

He said that if there were any problems regarding c as a ‘velocity’, then it could be thought of instead as ‘no more than a constant’. All equations involving c, he said, would be the same in either case. This struck me like a ‘bolt from the blue’. My simple model of Relativity had made a radical departure.

It made c no longer ‘the velocity of light’ but simply a geometrical scale constant, of just the sort that Featherstone had suggested. Some years later, in a contact with the eminent Cambridge astrophysicist and cosmologist, Prof. Sir Herman Bondi, I learned, to my great satisfaction, that my interpretation of c as no more than a dimensional scale constant matched exactly his nonvelocity interpretation of c as a distance-time conversion factor.

Added to the letter from Einstein, the boost that this gave to my confidence was immeasurable. (My wife said she wondered how I managed to get my head in through the door!)

Some further experimenting with my ruler and dividers produced the following diagrams depicting a whole series of increasing velocities. You must understand that there were no mathematics involved in this at all. The notoriously abstruse Theory of Einstein, it seemed, had been circumvented by plain pictorial commonsense. Such mathematical symbols as this diagram contains were added a long time afterwards (Fig. 10)

Now this left-hand diagram is just like the previous diagram except that it portrays many different velocities instead of just the one. The horizontal dimension tP is the time of the motion registered by the travelling body, in seconds (called the ‘proper’ time), and the vertical dimension s/c is the distance travelled by that body in units of 186,000 miles (300,000 kilometres in modern ‘money’) Now, as can be seen, this left-hand diagram puts no limit on the velocity at which a body may travel, which may be anywhere from zero to infinity – the vertical dimension of the diagram. So, what about the diagram shown on the right? This is what you get when you stretch a dividers from the origin O in the first diagram along each of the arrowed velocitygradients in turn and then project those lengths from the distance-dimension parallel to the horizontal time-dimension tR. In this new diagram, whereas the distances travelled are the same as in the first diagram, the dimension tR signifies the time of the motion relative to the observer of the motion, not relative to the moving object as in the first diagram. Now there are two things to note here. One is that any description of motion involves not just one time but two times, one being the time of the motion registered by the travelling object and the other the time of that same motion as measured by the observer. The second thing to note is that in the right-hand diagram there is now an upper limit placed on the velocity at which a body may travel. This limit, in terms of the units employed, is 186,000 miles per second, equivalent to the alleged ‘speed of light’. This is, of course, one of the famous consequences of Einstein’s Theory that, as usually expressed, ‘nothing can travel faster than the speed of light. It seemed, then, that my naïve, commonsense, ruler-and-dividers representation of motion had produced the same results that Einstein had discovered. This vastly boosted my confidence, not only in myself but also in the power of ordinary commonsense in opposition to Specialised Mathematics. A later development of this discovery in consultation with some mathematicians would reveal that these two diagrams are, in effect, the front and side elevations of a rectangular conic surface. (Fig. 11)

The ‘Cone Model’

This is because relative motion cannot be properly represented on a two-dimensional or flat surface since, as we have seen in Figure 10 (Just click, here, on next frame, Fig. 12 to repeat this), there are three dimensions to be represented in relative motion. One is the distance s travelled by the object; the second is the time tP seen to be registered by the moving body itself. The third, tR, is the time of the motion relative to the observer of the motion. (Demonstrate this with the actual model.) Now it cannot be over-stressed that this three-dimensional, conic representation of motion was discovered, not mathematically but intuitively with, basically, nothing more than an ordinary ruler and dividers. It was subsequently revealed that this rectangular conic surface, had I known it, is actually a well-known mathematical projection of Pythagoras’ theorem – although I have known of no-one else, so far, who has made the connection with Relativity Theory. However, that was for later. The fact was that in this altogether naïve, hands-on way, I had plainly derived an exact facsimile of Einstein’s famous Special Theory of Relativity. This was confirmed in a visit to the Physics Department at Swansea University in 1954. There, my simple geometrical model was given its first truly mathematical expression by Professor (then Doctor) P.M. Davidson, known affectionately by his students as ‘The Noble Savage’. (Describe). He demonstrated to me how those lengths I had measured with a ruler could be far better expressed in terms of Pythagoras’ theorem, and that by a simple and natural substitution of terms, that Pythagorean formula could be transposed to give Einstein’s famous and definitive time-dilation formula. It seemed that I had stumbled on a new way of obtaining Einstein’s mathematical results, but, strangely, from premises entirely different from Einstein’s. So my dear Grandmother had been right in saying that ‘All the keys hang not at one man’s girdle’. The same truth that Einstein had discovered was there to be discovered by others in other ways, and commonsense ways at that! This, I had now ascertained for myself. The significant difference was that in my naïve, commonsense discovery I had by-passed the whole tortuous tradition of electrodynamics and mathematics, a scholarly expertise in which is generally considered to be a necessary prelude to understanding Relativity. This was obviously why ‘Einstein’s Theory of Relativity’ was commonly regarded as so highly intellectual and abstruse.

In my innocence, I had circumvented all that esotery by going straight back to ancient Pythagoras to derive the same results on a commonsense ahistorical basis. This ‘lateral thinking’ alternative to Einstein confounded everyone. Attempts to publish it – even to get it looked at – fell on stony ground.

For thirty years it got nowhere – except into the ‘bad books’ of just about everyone it was mentioned to who could not see my questioning of Einstein as anything more than some kind of megalomania. (This made me realise the extent to which society reacts, in so many subtle and insidious ways against those it sees as ‘stepping out of line’.)

However, in the meantime, after making a nuisance of myself by asking awkward questions in some Workers Education Association evening classes conducted by two Physics lecturers from Swansea university, they advised me, as they put it, to ‘come in from the cold’.

They persuaded my employers to grant me leave from my telephone lineman’s job to attend the WEA college, at Harlech, North Wales. There I matriculated and went on to the University of Wales, Bangor, where I graduated in Philosophy of Science. Seeking to return to my beloved job as telephone lineman, I was forced to leave for being ‘overqualified’.

So I became a lecturer in Adult Education at a Midlands college and tutor for the newly founded Open University. Eventually, my continuing researches into Relativity brought me into contact with Dr. Anthony Osborne, of the Mathematics Department of Keele University, whose doctoral thesis had been on the subject of Special and General Relativity.

He bravely offered to collaborate on the mathematical development of my new Pythagorean Relativism. This led, in 1987, to the first Pope-Osborne publication, entitled ‘A New Approach to Special Relativity’. This was in the International Journal of Mathematical Education in Science and Technology, which is so mathematically specialised that it is scarcely if ever read by physicists, none of whom had realised the connection of my cone model with Relativity, anyway.

They didn’t know what to with my discovery, any more than a cow with a musket. My continuing collaboration with Osborne has resulted in numerous further publications, This Philosophy-Mathematics collaboration has become known as POAMS, the acronym for the Pope-Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis (for reasons which will soon become clear). Another long-time colleague in this POAMS enterprise is Alan Winfield, professor of Electronic Engineering at the University of West of England, which has recently fronted conferences, seminars and publications of the group. The group has also, recently, been joined by my dear friend, Penny Gower who acts as agent for arranging seminars and talks such as this, and disseminates information and papers to an intelligent, non-academic, philosophical spin-off group called the NRIG (Normal Realist Interest Group). Anyway, to return to the subject in hand, armed with the confidence gained from Einstein’s encouraging response to my question and Bondi’s subsequent support of my conclusions, I decided to examine other historically accepted theories for possibly overlooked logical faults and paradoxes. One of these, I discovered, concerns Newton’s Laws of Motion. Newton’s First law states that ‘All free moving bodies travel in straight lines’. However, it was plain to me that nothing ever travels in that way. Even balls on billiard tables and pucks on ice travel in curves if they are seen to travel far enough. So, I wondered, whatever possessed Newton to state something that is so obviously untrue? Here, for instance, is a conspicuous example of how eminently un-straight free motion is. Now a balance is, by definition, instantaneous and reciprocal. But a typical spiral galaxy is about 100 thousand light-years in diameter, while their distances apart are in the order of millions of light-years. So how can there be this balance between stars in a galaxy and between galaxies if, as Einstein’s Relativity says, nothing can travel faster than the ‘speed of light’? That same problem occurs, not only on the macrophysical level. It also occurs on the microphysical level, where, for the same reason connected with the alleged ‘speed of light’, Einstein’s Theory is fundamentally at odds with Quantum Physics, which requires that actions at a distance may be instantaneous. The infamous clash between Einstein and Niels Bohr over this is known as the ‘EPR Paradox’ (from a well-known paper written by Einstein in collaboration with Podolski and Rosen). However, the fact is that this immediate contact between distant objects and our observations of them undeniably exists on the quantum level. The name for that instantaneous contact is instantaneous action-at-a-distance, or ‘IAAAD’, which exists in quantum physics but which Einsteinian Relativity declared to be impossible. Both in astronomy and microphysics, then, Einstein’s postulate that light travels at a ‘velocity c in vacuo’ proves to be no more than an embarrassment and a prime source of conceptual confusion – which is doubtless why ‘Relativity’ remains such a music-hall-joke for intellectual egg-headedness. What the simple Pythagorean derivation of relativity has shown is that this embarrassment is not logically necessary, that c can be validly interpreted as a pure constant of observational distance-time projection, as Ted Featherstone suggested to me all those years ago. This purely pragmatic reinterpretation of c as no more than a constant disposes, at a stroke, of all problems as to how atoms, planets, stars, galaxies and so on balance one another, as they plainly do, and as to how atoms can interact at great distances in instantaneous quantum jumps. The vexing ‘EPR paradox’ simply vanishes, as the cone model shows. Note that the cone model, which we have already seen, shows that there is no logical contradiction whatsoever between instantaneity and time-delay in distant interaction. As you can see, it combines both the so-called ‘light-speed’ limit c and quantum instantaneity. (Demonstrate this with the model again.). So I saw that the EPR paradox was no longer an issue, that it was no more than an academic ‘storm in a teacup’. This led me to my next innocent inspiration – and this is where the ‘heresy’ becomes compounded. As I saw it, while Newton’s first law of motion states that everything left to itself travels in straight lines, his ‘Law of Universal Gravitation’ explains why nothing travels in that way. The two laws negate each other. The one states that something should be so and the other law explains why it never is. The whole thing was absurd. As I saw it, everything, from atoms and parts of atoms to stars and galaxies, if left free to do so, seeks to orbit everything else. So, I thought, why not replace Newton’s straight-line law, which is so obviously false, with a new law stating what is so obviously true, namely, that everything, if left free to do so, seeks to orbit everything else in automatically paired and balanced angular momentum relations. Now one of the many implications of the conservation of angular momentum is a steady state cosmology, because there is no way in which conserved angular momentum can be reconciled with the customary ‘Big Bang’ theory of cosmological creation. Suffice it to say that in a system of overall-conserved angular momentum it is impossible to imagine any overall increase or decrease, or any ‘beginning’ or ‘end’, so that speculations along those lines are pointless.

This means that the Cosmological Redshift phenomenon observed by Hubble and Slipher has to be interpreted other than as a Doppler shift due to galactic recession, with all that that entails in terms of ‘expansion’, ‘creation’ and so on – I told you, didn’t I, that this is a heresy!

In the Pope-Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis (POAMS), the Redshift is explained as the effect, not of galactic recession but of time-dilation due to the statistical accumulation of mass with observational distance. Now it needs to be stressed that the essential difference between this Pythagorean strand of relativity and Einstein’s is that in Einstein’s Theory of Relativity (both Special and General) the ‘finite speed of light’, c, places a limit on the speed at which bodies may interact at a distance. By contrast, in the Pythagorean derivation there is no such limit, as the cone model shows. In other words, in our Pythagorean relativity which, in all other respects, is the same as Einstein’s, all interactions in terms of any balancing influence are, by default, instantaneous. A paper on this, entitled ‘Instantaneous Relativistic Action at a Distance’, by myself and Osborne, was published in 1992 in Physics Essays. So, what is the upshot of all this? Well, for starters, we achieve something that has never been achieved before. That is, we reconcile Relativistic time-delay with quantum instantaneity, thus solving the notorious ‘EPR paradox’. This is by simply leaving out all the redundant talk about the ‘speed of light’ in explaining Relativity. All that .does is to confuse the issue by feeding in ideas that are superfluous to logical requirements, thereby creating unnecessary paradoxes. The whole business of relativistic space, time motion and quantum instantaneity can then be understood far better without that light-velocity concept. Indeed, without that concept, the Theory of Relativity and its relation to Quantum Theory can be deduced, as you have seen, much more easily by plain commonsense methods starting, not with a degree in mathematics but with nothing more than an ordinary ruler and dividers. Next, by amending Newton’s first law of motion and his law of universal gravitation we go straight into General Relativity, replacing ‘gravitational attraction’ between bodies with reciprocally balanced angular momentum. This links bodies together instantly with one another at a distance, in direct opposition to Einstein’s assertion that no physical influence can travel faster than the ‘speed of light’. This latter consequence of the New Pythagorean Approach to Relativity therefore supports the contention of a growing number of scientists that instantaneous action-at-a-distance (IAAAD) is a true fact of nature. (It is interesting to note, by the way, that in the Nova book there were more arguments in favour of IAAAD than there were against.)

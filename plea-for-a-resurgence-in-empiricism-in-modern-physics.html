Plea for a Resurgence in Empiricism in Modern Physics

N. Vivian Pope

Plea for a Resurgence of Empiricism in Modern Physics Viv Pope

Sit with me in my garden on this sunny Spring morning and look around. See those trees. Are they as real as they appear to be, or are they only theoretical? Those flowers and the bees buzzing among them, are they real or just theoretical? That fountain; that mower with which, all too often, I have to mow the grass; that aeroplane, with its white con-trail slicing the blue. Are these things real or just purely theoretical? What a daft question. Of course they are real. And if that is not reality, then what is? Well, according to Einstein’s famous Theory of Relativity, those things are not real. They are all no more than images of a reality that lies beyond them, forever inaccessible to direct observation. This is on account of what, in the Theory of Relativity, is called ‘Einstein separation’. In that Theory it is claimed, in what is famously called Einstein’s Second Postulate, that the light by which we see things travels in space at a constant velocity, c, which is the same for you, me and everyone, regardless of whether we are moving or stationary with respect to one another. This separates physical reality from our perceptions of it by 3.3 nanoseconds of time for every metre of distance, creating, in effect, two ‘realities’ the one we directly perceive and the other we perceive only indirectly, as ‘after-images’, by courtesy of ‘light’ acting as mediator between physical objects and our perceptions of them, and since we never directly perceive those physical objects, this makes their existence, in the last analysis, purely suppositional, or ‘theoretical’. Scientists who subscribe to this theoretical view of reality as something essentially remote from perception describe themselves as ‘Realists’, implying, of course, that the world of our immediate perception is unreal. Let these be called Theoretical Realists. In contrast, those for whom the first call in realism is perception have been called Normal Realists (see ‘Normal Realism’ on Google Search). Now there are one or two things to think about here concerning Einstein’s postulate that light is something travelling at a constant speed in a vacuum. In the first place, in space (the void or vacuum) what is there against which to measure the ‘velocity’ of anything, constant or otherwise? In the second place, for anything to be truly a velocity, that velocity has to obey the rule of the composition of velocities, according to which a velocity is systematically different with respect to relatively moving observers. But c is the same for all observers in all different states of relative motion and rest. So how can c possibly be a velocity? Thirdly, what is certain is that c has the same dimensions as a velocity, that is, distance divided by time. But the fact that all velocities are distances divided by time does not logically entail that all distances divided by time are velocities, any more than ‘All prostitutes are hardworking girls’ implies ‘All hard-working girls are prostitutes’. Moreover, I can divide any length whatsoever by any time whatsoever without that being a ‘velocity’. For instance, the length of my desk divided by its age doesn’t give it a velocity. Nor does the diameter of my wall-clock divided by the time it ticks give that a velocity. In any case, during the 1950s, it was demonstrated, independently, by both H. Bondi and N.V. Pope, and, later corroborated by others such as A.D. Osborne and A.F.T Winfield (see website www.poams.org) that all the important consequences of the Theory of Relativity, both Special and General, can be deduced, far more simply and efficiently, without Einstein’s Second Postulate, taking c as no more than a

constant of dimensional projection (a distance-time ‘conversion factor’, as Bondi calls it). In effect, then, there is no logical necessity whatever to think of light as travelling in the way Einstein envisaged. It can’t be seen travelling. For instance, we do not see it leave objects and travel towards us. Nor, as the medieval scientists well knew, can even the brightest sunray be seen shining through opposite holes in an empty barrel unless there is some dust or smoke inside the barrel – in which case, it is the motes of dust or smoke that we see, not the light passing between the holes. The same applies to even the strongest searchlight and laser beams, which are visible only in misty or smoky atmospheres, being completely invisible in a vacuum. So any notion that light travels in a vacuum is completely theoretical. What is not theoretical is that light is the optical information on which, directly, all our knowledge of the world is based. From that information, transacted between objects, we observationally project all the dimensions, properties, qualities, dispositions and behaviour of all the various things in nature. So, okay, let’s get back to the garden and take another good look around. On this clearest and brightest of Spring days, do we see any of the things that the Theoretical Realists talk about? Do we see light travelling in space? Of course we don’t. We see light, and in contrast we see dark, but in neither case does it make sense to ascribe to them a ‘velocity’ Do we see ‘electromagnetic waves’? Again, no. Look, there’s the Moon, shimmering palely in the blue sky (as it sometimes does in broad daylight). Do we see the ‘gravitational force’ that, acting in the vacuum, is supposed to keep that body orbiting around us? No way! Is the Moon theoretical? Decidedly not. Is that in vacuo ‘gravitational force’ theoretical? Decidedly, yes. Do we see the ‘atoms’ that Theoretical Realists regard as the underlying sources of the light that we see? No, what we see, in any instance, is the light, from which the Theoretical Realists extrapolate the existence of those entities. Do we see the ‘electrons’, ‘protons’, ‘quarks’ and so on that, for the Theoretical Realist, are the constituents of those ‘atoms’? Not as such, certainly. These entities are nothing like the trees, rocks, flowers, bees and so on that we directly perceive on this bright morning. Not even with our very best, most powerful microscopes, can we ever perceive these things as they are ‘in themselves’. What we see are, at best, the supposed ‘effects’ of these theoretical entities upon our sense-organs and instruments. But, someone will say, aren’t these atoms, electrons and things directly visible when they leave tracks in cloud chambers, tanks of liquid hydrogen and so on in atom-smashing laboratories such as CERN? No. Again, what we actually see are the tracks, not the things supposedly making them. When we see tracks such as those made by aircraft, like that con-trail up there, we know that it is made by something that can, ultimately, be seen, like that ‘plane when it is coming in to land or standing at the terminal. In the case of those ‘fundamental particles’, there is no such directly perceptible object. What you see is what you get – and all you’ll ever get. In other words, the ‘plane is a real object; the ‘fundamental particle’ is and remains, purely and inexorably theoretical. But something must be making those tracks, and what is that ‘something’? The answer is that it is something theoretical, what else! Then what is the difference between that and a real object? The difference is that whatever we may think of as the theoretical object can never be as assuredly real as, say, a tree, a rock or the Moon. We can never be sure that it exists in the way we think of it. There is simply no way in which we can compare our ideas of that entity with whatever that entity might be

‘in itself’. All we can ever do is make guesses at it, based on our actual, finite observations of what we might think of as its effects on the things that we actually observe. All priority, therefore, in the conduct of physical science goes to observation, logically our first and foremost (a priori) contact with the world, with theories being never more than secondary (a posteriori). The difference, in other words, is that we can quite justifiably be dogmatic about real things such as trees, rocks, etc. As J.L Austin says, ‘if that is not a pig (a tree, church or barn) then I don’t know what is’. But the same cannot be said of, say, an ‘electron’, a ‘photon’ or a ‘quark’. All these sorts of identifications are held on lease, pending the results of ongoing researches which might reveal the necessity for radical revision – as, for instance, in the case of theoretical substances such as ‘caloric’ and ‘phlogiston’. A prime fallacy, therefore, is to reverse this order of priority in the matter of knowledge by assuming that we have first-hand knowledge of these theoretical entities and then setting out to ‘explain’ our perceptions in those terms. The fallacy is as follows. Take the sequence of propositions: i) All fundamental particles (P) are known by their quantifiable effects (Q). [The implication is then P ‫ כ‬Q (P implies Q)]. ii) We know the quantifiable effects (Q), therefore, iii) We know fundamental particles (Q ‫ כ‬P). A truth-table analysis of this argument reveals it to be fallacious (see

Appendix). For those not acquainted with logical analysis, a commonplace paraphrase of this fallacious argument would be: Peter knows Quentin (P ‫ כ‬Q), and I know Quentin (Q), therefore I know Peter (P). The fallacy of this (as if it needed stressing) is perfectly obvious. No less obvious is to assume, as all too many theoreticians do, a God’s-eye view of what they envisage as a theoretical underworld of colourless, odourless and generally inscrutable sub-entities and then, pretending to be ‘God’, reconstruct themselves and all they know out of these ‘fundamental’ bits and pieces. All this is completely un-empirical. Empiricism works the other way around. It bases its science first and foremost on phenomena. Its only use for theories is in seeking the correct, most logically efficient and natural interpretations of what is actually observed. The one and only reality is then the most logically coherent and consistent – most conceptually efficacious – set of classifications, divisions and categorisations of all the various phenomena. From this it follows that for the Empiricist, all stress falls on the correct logical use of language as the main tool of science. As people like Wittgenstein have revealed, misuses of language create chimeras, mythical entities, conceptual monsters in what has nowadays become more pseudo science than science. (See H. Bondi, Assumption and Myth in Physical Theory , CUP 1964.) The more these chimeras appear in the common language as names of real objects alongside those of things like pigs, ducks, churches and so on, the more our prospects of ever achieving a commonsense understanding of nature are exponentially and catastrophically reduced. And the more that the emphasis is placed, in science, on the production of theories for their own sake – as is definitely the vogue in Physics and cosmology, nowadays, the further apart science and commonsense become, to the detriment of both. The task of keeping science and commonsense together on the same rational track has been undertaken by a group of science-philosophers called the POAMS group (website www.poams.org. These have demonstrated, as stated above, that by the simple removal of the logically redundant ‘Einstein separation’, implied by the Second Postulate, Relativity ceases to be a Theory and becomes more a statement of

phenomenological fact. In other words, the ‘two realities’, of theory and observation, merge into one, in which the so-far irreconcilable categories of phenomena that current Relativity and Quantum Theory deal with separately, become one and the same in a wholesome and continually evolving commonsense knowledge of natural reality. This re-creates the logically joined-up way of thinking that was once called Natural Philosophy prior to the disjointed ‘pick and mix’ set of ideas that has become modern Physics. Incredible? No, not really. Just read the website. Then go sit in your garden in the bright sunshine and think deeply and sensibly about it.

Welsh Phenomenalism

N. Vivian Pope — 1980

Welsh phenomenalism, currently called Normal Realism, is, arguably, the latest, most up-to-date development in the long line of science-philosophies famously associated with the names of John Locke (1632–1704), George Berkeley (1685–1753), David Hume (1711–1776), Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Ernst Mach (1838–1916) and Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951). Each stage in this development, strangely named ‘English Empiricism’, is marked by some quite radical change in the original conception. For Locke and Berkeley, the true elements of physical reality were ‘ideas’. For Hume, they were ‘habits’ or ‘conventions’. For Kant, they were ‘phenomena’. For Mach, they were ‘sense data’. For Wittgenstein, they were, at first, ‘atomic propositions’ about sensation and then, in his second phase, ‘language-games’, or customary uses of ‘ordinary language’ applied to sensory experience.

During the first half of the 20th Century, a spin-off from this ‘English Empiricism’ burgeoned on the Continent in what was called phénoménologie. Based on the philosophical views of Edmund Husserl in Germany during the mid-1890s, this spread widely in Europe and in Australia in the form of ‘existentialism’, as expressed in the famous works of Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980). This Continental branch of phenomenalism was largely psychologistic, concerned with matters of the mind and consciousness generally, as opposed to English Empiricism which, especially with Mach, was more concerned with Physics — or Natural Philosophy as it was then called.

In the early 1960s, the influence of Wittgenstein became paramount in the Philosophy Department at the University of South Wales, Swansea. This was through the influence of the Welshman, Rush Rhees, Wittgenstein’s friend and philosophical ‘right-hand man’. These meetings were attended by another Welshman, Viv Pope, who was then a critic, if not a repudiator of this ‘Linguistic’ movement. Having moved to the University of North Wales, Bangor, as a mature student of Philosophy under Professor Clem Mundle, Pope found himself, surprisingly, at odds with some anti-Wittgenstein, or ‘Wittgenstein-bashing’ members of that Department. In this tussle, after studying the works of G. E. Moore, Gilbert Ryle, J. L. Austin and A. J. Ayer, he began to discern the significance of this ‘observationist’ philosophy for modern relativistic physics. Relativity was all about ‘observers’ and so was phenomenalism. Perhaps the two could be merged into a single, new approach to Natural Philosophy — a ‘New Physics’, in effect, combining the two so-far separate disciplines of Science and Philosophy.

In his attempts to pursue this united course, Pope came into contact with Dr. Anthony Osborne of the Mathematics Department of Keele University, after which he was invited by the Head of that Department, Professor Graham Wilkes, to conduct his researches with Osborne under the aegis of the Department as a doctorate. However, Pope now had his foot in two academically separate camps, one in the Arts and the other in Science. With the Education System unprepared for this overstepping of the academic boundaries, the Philosophy Department at Keele refused to cooperate in this merging of Philosophy with Mathematical Physics. So, although Pope’s doctoral work with Osborne continued, his ‘doctorate’, from then on, was left simply ‘hanging’. Was he, by default, ‘Doctor’ Pope or just plain ‘Mister’? Among those who were bothered about this, Pope was not included.

Pope had now decided to look at the world, not through the long-dead eyes of the ‘Greats’, such as Newton, Rutherford, Faraday, Maxwell, Weber, Ampère, et al, but through new, educationally unclouded modern eyes. These eyes could not directly see light travelling towards them from distant objects. Nor could they directly see light travelling from one object to another in empty space — in vacuo as Einstein had taught. Nor could they directly see ‘electrostatic’ and magnetic ‘fields’ or ‘ethers’, with ‘waves’ travelling through them. What they saw they saw directly, that is, objects themselves, such as the moon, stars and planets.

In force-free motion, these planets and satellites, quite obviously and without exception, did not travel in straight lines, in the way Newton’s ‘First Law of Motion’ had directed. They always travelled in curves, in the manner of a thrown stone, and in closed orbits in the case of the planets and their satellites. Seeing no in vacuo ‘force’ responsible for these curves, he decided that he would look for some other explanation of that orbital motion. This, he discovered, was in what he learned was angular momentum. Angular momentum was curved for no other reason than that it was what it was, curved by definition. Moreover, he found that angular momentum was an automatically paired relation, also that this paired relation was a conserved quantity, or amount. The greater the amount of angular momentum a body possessed relative to another body, or centre of balance, the larger was the radius of that orbit, that is to say, the distance between the two bodies.

Logically, he determined that to travel in Newton’s straight line, a body would have to have infinite angular momentum. And since infinite angular momentum was impossible, all finite angular momentum had to be curved, or orbital. This dispensed with ‘gravity’ at a stroke. The reason why bodies were pressed to the earth was simply that they had insufficient angular momentum to orbit clear of the planet, so that the earth’s surface prevented them from orbiting where they otherwise should, way down below our feet. The force that these bodies registered on a weighing scale was not some invisible in vacuo ‘force’; it was the real, visible and tangible, directly measurable force set up by the earth’s surface against a body’s natural tendency to follow its unrestrained orbit. So, like ‘light travelling in vacuo’, the notion of ‘gravity’ as an in vacuo ‘force’ was, for Pope, consigned to the scrap-heap of history.

The more he explored it, the more he discovered the extent to which angular momentum, in and of itself, could explain, not only the motions of astronomical bodies but also of the particles of those bodies, such as ‘electrons’ around ‘protons’, ‘electrons’ around ‘electrons’ and nucleons around nucleons, etc. This, for him, solved the notorious ‘Unified Field’ problem by getting rid of all these invisible in vacuo ‘forces’ altogether — i.e., uniformly.

In his association with Osborne, at the Keele University Maths Department, the further logical ramifications of this new way of thinking were explored in what came to be known as the Pope-Osborne Angular Momentum Synthesis (POAMS). For instance, Pope had discovered that dispensing with the customary interpretation of the constant $c$ as ‘the velocity of light in vacuo’ created a breathtakingly simplified version of Einstein’s Theory of Relativity. This completely bypassed all the historical rigmarole of the invisible ‘luminiferous ether’, ‘electromagnetic fields’ and ‘waves’ in vacuo, plus all the tortuous puzzles about ‘ether drift’, Michelson, Morley et al., with which the standard educational route to Relativity had been cluttered.

Moreover, since he discovered that angular momentum analyses-out to fundamentally irreducible measures, or quanta, $h/2\pi$ (where $h$ is Planck’s constant of action, the product of energy and time), he realised that the Relativity he had rediscovered was not only massively simplified, it was also quantised, which solved the chronic antagonism between the Relativists and the Quantum theorists over the so-called ‘EPR’ (Einstein, Podolski, Rosen) paradox presented by the seeming contradiction between Einstein’s ‘speed-limit’ $c$ and the instantaneity of distant interaction required by Quantum Theory. In other words, Pope’s vastly simplified Relativity theory was also, automatically, without any theoretical contrivance, a ‘quantum theory’. It was, in fact, automatically, a Quantum Relativity.

This, of course, was just too much for academic Philosophy and Physics to swallow. It was also too much for the Science-led public to swallow. In trying to promulgate this Synthesis, Pope became something of a pariah. Nevertheless, work on the Synthesis continued apace, under the aegis of the Maths Department at Keele. The Synthesis was presented at international conferences, some at Swansea University, published in Conference Proceedings and other journals and, eventually, in dedicated books, edited by Pope and Osborne, in collaboration with Professor Alan Winfield of the University of West of England (UWE) Bristol.

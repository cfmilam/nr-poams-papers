The Locality and Non-Locality of Special Relativity

N. Vivian Pope — 1997
c/o the Department of Mathematics, Keele University, UK

In the 1920s, theoretical physics was split down the middle. This was with the realisation that the time-separation, or 'locality' of physical happenings, demanded by Einstein's special theory of relativity (STR), is contradicted by quantum theory, which demands the instantaneous correlation of certain distance-separated events (called 'non-locality'). This contradiction became known as the 'EPR' paradox after the 1935 paper by Einstein, Podolski and Rosen, which challenged Bohr's quantum theory on the grounds that it implied some kind of nonsensical 'spooky superluminal action-at-a-distance'. However, in the 1970s, on a principle enunciated by Bell, the existence of Bohr's non-local correlations was experimentally confirmed. The problem, therefore, for many physicists, is that far from resolving the dispute between these antitheses, experiment continues to confirm both. This suggests that the chronic 'EPR' issue is more a case for radical revision of our current theories than a need for more and more experimental information.

According to Einstein's special theory of relativity (STR), physical objects cannot interact in less time than it takes for light to travel between them at the finite and constant speed $c$. This is what physicists call 'locality'. According to STR, all time has to be local. There can be no intrinsic distance-extended present moment, or non-local space-time frame in which bodies co-exist and act together.

This poses a problem, because in systems of angular momentum (in astronomical orbits, for example) bodies balance one another; and balance is a non-local or holistic relation in which masses on opposite ends of a moment-arm act together.[1] Moreover, there are plain enough evidences emerging of spin-orientations of particles that are non-locally linked, or 'entangled', so that any alteration in the one angular momentum orientation instantaneously affects the other.[2] Special Relativity flies in the face of these well-confirmed examples of angular momentum conservation, which is the root of the notorious paradox known as 'EPR'.[3]

However, every student of relativity knows that while, in its relative-time aspect light has the finite speed $c$, in its proper-time aspect its transition is instantaneous. In this sense, STR implies both locality and non-locality. So why are the evidences of locality and non-locality regarded as contradictory when there is a well-established theory, namely, STR, that entails both? This seems not to have been even considered by the disputants at the Solvay conferences.[4] How did they miss it? What made them think of 'local' and 'non-local' as antithetical when plainly they are not?

The trouble is that a whole superstructure of theory had already been built around the notion that because in observation and experiment $c$ has the dimensions of a velocity it necessarily is a velocity — which is like saying that because all Chinese are human, all humans are Chinese. So although the 'velocity' precept (to which even Einstein succumbed) is a fallacy, to revise it at this stage in history would be about as acceptable as to propose rewriting the Constitution of the United States. Perhaps, then, the real problem modern physics faces is not so much logical as sociological, or even psychological. The logical solution is simple. It is to revise the over-interpreted and paradoxical notion of $c$ being the speed of something travelling (waves, particles, wave-particles or whatever). This would be to adopt a more economical interpretation of $c$ as simply what Herman Bondi, in 1965, called a dimensional 'conversion-factor'.[5] This satisfies all the essential conditions of observation and experiment without involving those logically unnecessary 'something-travelling' implications that have led to the ad-hoc and paradoxical, not to say nonsensical, 'wave-particle' ('photon') interpretation which some see as quite inappropriate and misleading.[6]

When that 'velocity' interpretation is laid aside, what remains is Bondi's conception of $c$ as a pure constant relating conventional units (i.e., metres and seconds) in two co-exclusive, or orthogonal dimensions, one being that of the instantaneous connection required by quantum theory and the other being that of time-delayed sequences of these in a movie-like action, like that generated by sequences of 'stills' in the running of a cinematographic film.

In this way, instead of having to think of infinite and finite-time interaction as superimposed or collinear, which is impossible to visualise, we may, without logical contradiction — or, indeed, without intuitive difficulty — think of them as adjacently alternating components of a composite process. One of these components is that of all-distance-and-no-time (i.e., an instantaneous or non-local extension, $s$, measured in metres) and the other is that of all-time-and-no-distance (i.e., a purely time-sequential process, $t$, measured in seconds). The composite of those two orthogonal components is then a resultant $t_R$, according to the Pythagorean formula:

$$t_R^2 = (s/c)^2 + t^2 \tag{1}$$

in which $c$ is the conversion of the distance measure $s$, in metres, into time measure $s/c$, in seconds. As Bondi has shown, in a simplified formulation of STR, this is the same formula that produces the standard time-dilation of Einstein's special relativity (as can be seen when $s$ in (1) is replaced by $vt_R$, where $v$ is a 'speed of light' in the conventional parlance).[7]

In this simple and intuitive cinematic model of interaction as presented above, there is nothing contradictory about an event being both instantaneous (non-local) and time-delayed (local). This is the situation in everyday cinematography, where the presentations of the objects in the individual photographic frames (stills) are instantaneous (non-local) whereas the sequential action connecting those instantaneous frames is time-delayed (local), the resultant being what we call the 'movie'. Thus, the Einsteinian time $t_R$ is the 'movie-time', the proper-time $t$ is the time of individual frames (stills) and the distance $s$ is the extension of the objects in the instantaneous frame. In the case of optical interaction (light) the proper-time $t$ is zero, so that formula (1) becomes:

$$t_R = s/c \tag{2}$$

which is simply the time conventionally attributed to 'the transit of light over distance $s$'.

It is scarcely coincidence that this interpretation of the 'two times' of special relativity — i.e., the local time $t_R$ and the non-local proper-time $t$ — exactly matches the empirically confirmed descriptions of the local and non-local aspects of quantum interaction: the proper-time-instantaneous connection being the quantum interaction as such, and the local, time-delayed connection being the observational time-sequencing of those quantum events in the manner above described.

Whether this simple, commonsense-logical thesis is now taken seriously by the current establishment of physics or is rejected by it out of hand is, as far as POAMS[8] is concerned, a matter of complete indifference, because with or without the endorsement of that establishment, the logic of what has been said is now available for all and sundry to assess in their own free and rational capacity, regardless of 'expert' opinion. ■

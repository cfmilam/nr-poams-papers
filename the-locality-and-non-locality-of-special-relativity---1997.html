The locality and non-locality of special relativity Viv Pope c/o the Department of Mathematics, Keele University, UK

In the lg2}s,theoretical physics was split down the middle. This was with the realisation that the time-separation, or 'locality' of physical happenings, demanded by Einstein's special theory of relativity (STR), is contradicted by quantum theory, which demands the instantaneous correlation of certain distance-separated events (called 'non-locality'). This contradiction became known as the 'EPR' paradox after the 1935 paper by Einstein, Podolski and Rosen, which challenged Bohr's quantum theory on the grounds that it implied some kind of nonsensical 'spooky superluminal action-at-a-distance'. However, in the 1970s, on a principle enunciated by Bell, the existence of Bohr's non-local correlations was experimentally confirmed. The problem, therefore, for many physicists, is that far from resolving the dispute between these antitheses, experiment continues to confirm both. This suggests that the chronic EPR' issue is more a case for radical revision of our current theories than a need for more and more experimental information. According to Einstein's special theory of relativity (STR) physical objects cannot interact in less time than it takes for light to travel between them at the finite and constant speed c. This is what physicists call 'locality'. According to STR, all time has to be local. There can be no intrinsic distance-extended present moment, or non-local space-time frame in which bodies co-exist and act together.

This poses a problem, because in systems of angular momentum (in astronomical orbits, for example) bodies balance one another; and balance is a non-local or holistic relation in which masses on opposite ends of a moment-arm act together.' Moreover, there are plain enough evidences emerging of spin-orientations of particles that are non-locally linked, or 'entangled', so that any alteration in the one angular momentum orientation instantaneously affects the other.2 Special Relativity flies in the face of these well-confirmed examples of angular momentum conservation, which is the root of the notorious paradox known as EPR'.3

However, every student of relativity knows that while, in its relative-time aspect light has the finite speed c, in its proper-time aspect its transition is instantaneous. In this sense, STR implies both locality and non-locality. So why are the evidences of locality and non-locality regarded as contradictory when there is a well-established theory, namely, STR, that entails both? This seems not to have been even considered by the disputants at the Solvay conferences.a How did they miss rr? What made them think of 'local' and 'non-local' as antithetical when plainly they are not?

The trouble is that a whole superstructure of theory had already been built around the notion that because in observation and experiment c has the dimensions of a velocity it necessarily ls a velocity - which is like saying that because all Chinese are human, all humans are Chinese. So although the 'velocity' precept (to which even Einstein succumbed) is a fallacy, to revise it at this stage in history would be about as acceptable as to propose rewriting the Constitution of the United States. Perhaps, then, the real problem modern physics faces is not so much logical as or even psychological. The logical solution is simple. 11 is to revise the sociological over-interpreted and paradoxical notion of c being the speed of something travelling (waves, particles, wave-particles or whatever). This would be to adopt a more economical interpretation of c as simply what Herman Bondi, in 1965, called a dimensional 'conversion-factor'.5 This satisfies all the essential conditions of observation and experiment without involving those logically nnnecessary 'something-travelling' implications that have led to the ad-hoc and paradoxical, not to say nonsensical, 'wave-particle' ('photon') interpretation which some see as quite inappropriate and misleading.6'' When that 'velocity' interpretation is laid aside, what remains is Bondi's conception of c as a pure constant relating conventional units (i.e., metres and seconds) in two co-exclusive, or orthogonal dimensions, one being that of the instantaneous connection required by quantum theory and the other being that of time-delayed sequences of these in a movie-like action, like that generated by sequences of 'stills' in the running of a

But what does it matter what we call these commonly recognised longitudinal and transverse components of distant interaction so long as in essence they are the same in the differentaccounts of them? ln ourpapers of 1992 and 1995, we (Pope andOsborne) point out that in all the various forms of interaction, called 'gravitational', 'inertial', 'electrostatic', 'magnetic' 'nuclear' or whatever, the common factor, throughout - the essence, in effect - is angular momentum, which we regard as sufficiently described by a single angular momentum equation.la The different strengths of the various 'forces' are thus explained by different involutions and convolutions of angular momentum, such as spin or some other kind of purely mechanical oscillation, all measured uniformly in joules - i.e., without recourse to the traditional 'coulombs', 'farads', 'henrys' ... and so on, of circumstantially established theory. In these terms, the instantaneous component of distant interaction depicted by the horizontal axis of Figure 1 is the moment-arm of an angular momentum nexus between masses or angular momentum subsystems of one kind or another, all within an overall-conserved angular momentum system, and the finite-speed (vertical) component is that of the (ultimately quantised) soquences of these angular momentum 'stills'at the measured, movie-like'speed c'. This, we claim, unifies all the various'forces'of orbital acceleration by getting rid of them altogether.

In sum, then, if the reasoning in this paper is correct, it seems that by the simple

expedient of switching from thinking of the constant c as a 'velocity of motion'to thinking of it in Bondi's way as a dimensional constanr and nothing more, we produce a theoretical facsimile of the special theory of relativity which does everything that the extant theory does but avoids its

notoriously paradoxical 'EPR' implications. That is to say, whereas in STR, the finite velocity of light and instantaneous quantum action-at-a-distance are generally supposed to be antithetical, this simple logrcal adjustment in our customary interpretation of c makes them part and parcel of the same thesis. Moreover, with no 'fields' for the mediation of proper-time-instantaneous action, in this conceptually pared-down rendering of STR, Einstein's 'unified field' programme is completed by default.

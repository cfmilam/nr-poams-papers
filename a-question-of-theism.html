A Question of Theism

(The continuing Blue Anchor dialogue)

by

Viv Pope

In a lesson on Liberal Studies at a Midlands Technical College, I happened to pose the question 'How many atheists are there in this class?' All the students raised their hands. 'Do you know what atheism means?' I asked them. 'Yes,' said one, 'It means we don't believe in God.' Another added the corrective, 'It means we believe there is no God.' My next question, put to them on the spur of the moment, turned out to be one of the most inspired I ever asked. 'Describe to me, then,' I said, 'the God you believe doesn't exist.'

The descriptions those students provided, on bits of paper which I should have kept but which are now unfortunately lost, were so various and bizarre that, as I said to them in my summing-up: 'If what you guys have described there is God, then we should all be atheists, because no-one but a lunatic could ever believe in a God like that!

I can't remember how the lesson went after that, but it certainly got me thinking. How can anyone possibly believe or disbelieve in some 'X', be it 'God' or whatever, if they haven't a clue what that 'X' means? The question of the precise meaning of 'God', for me at any rate, has therefore to come first in any enquiry into theology because, unlike those students, I cannot say whether I believe or don't believe in the existence of something of which I have no clear idea.

So I take a look in the dictionaries I have at my disposal to see what, in common language 'God' means. The Oxford School Dictionary tells me that it means 'The Supreme Being, Creator.' The Pocket Oxford Dictionary, says that God is 'The Supreme being, Creator and Ruler of the universe.' The Britannica World-Language Dictionary gives 'The One Supreme Being, self-existent and eternal; the infinite creator, sustainer and ruler of the universe: conceived as omniscient, good and almighty.' I now look up the word 'omniscient'. It means 'all-knowing'. So, fair enough! Now I have some clear meaning of what 'God' means in my native language. It means, so far as I can see,

The One Supreme, self-existent, eternal, infinite and infinitely good, all-knowing almighty creator, sustainer and ruler of the universe

Do I believe, then, that the entity so described does or does not exist? But how can I answer that without first asking: What evidence do I or may I have of the existence of such an entity?

Some people would answer that the evidence I seek is in the Bible – or the Koran or in some similar authoritative tome. But I am approaching this question essentially as a scientist, and with my scientist's hat on I have to dismiss that sort of historical evidence, no matter how venerable, as no more than anecdotal. As I see it, if that evidence of the existence of God was available to the ancients who wrote those works and if it was valid for them, then it should be no less available and no less valid for me, here and now, otherwise I have every right to be suspicious of it.

So what I have to look for, in my life, not in the reports of others – far less in some bygone age – is some sort of direct, personal evidence of that 'infinite being' the dictionaries describe. Well, that scuppers me for a start, because how can my finite intellect possibly have any conception of what it means to be 'infinite', 'supreme', 'self-existent', 'all-knowing' ... and so on and so on? So I cannot see how I can ever have any true knowledge, or indeed any faith in what is to me totally inconceivable and of which I can never have any direct experience.

But now someone tells me that as he understands it, the word 'God' means 'The Creator of the universe who reveals himself to men in his works.' Chasing this up, we find, under 'theism', in the Britannica Dictionary:

theism. Belief in a personal God as creator and supreme ruler of the universe, who transcends his creation but works in and through it in revealing himself to men.

– and women, I suppose, to keep up with the times. Anyway, with reservations concerning the personification this entity (on which the Britannica statement seems ambiguous),¹ that now makes more sense to me because it allows us to have at least some indirect experience of God. So now I can amend my understanding of the meaning of the word 'God' to read

The One Supreme, self-existent, eternal, infinite and infinitely good, all-knowing almighty creator, sustainer and ruler of the universe who transcends his creation but works in and through it in revealing himself (personally/as a person) to human individuals.

This 'creation' through which God reveals himself is, of course, what science calls nature. But now one has to take account of a crucial change that has occurred in the meaning of that term. In all departments of physics-based classical science, 'nature' meant the purely mechanical world of atoms acting absolutely determinately according to Newton's purely mechanical laws. In that case, God could be never be conceived as anything more than a cosmic Machine Designer, who made the world, set it on automatic and left it to its own mechanical devices to produce all we see around us including, somehow, those peculiar bits of pure mechanism we call ourselves. The trouble with that is that if we are no more than collections of atoms driven by blind mechanical forces, then we can have no responsibility whatever for our thoughts and actions or their consequences. And where there is no responsibility there can, of course, be no morality. So the only kind of belief in 'God' which is consistent with classical science is what the Britannica dictionary identifies as deism, which is defined in the following three ways:

deism. 1 The belief in the existence of a personal God, based solely on the testimony of reason and rejecting any supernatural revelation; natural religion. 2 The belief that God created the world and set it in motion, subject to natural laws, but takes no interest in it. 3 The belief in a first cause which is not intrinsically perfect or complete, and therefore not a proper object of worship.

In other words, this 'natural religion' which classical science contemplates consists of a belief in a personal 'God of nature' (1), where 'nature' is conceived in (2) the old classical-science, 'Machine-Designer' way. Whether or not one believes in a first cause which is not perfect or complete (3) is incidental.

For instance, does 'personal' mean that God is a person or that the conception of God is a personal one? The latter interpretation seems unlikely since it would suggest mere subjectivity of the concept.

However, with the shift I have mentioned from classical to modern physics that mechanistic, or deistic, conception of the nature of things has changed. The reason, specifically, is as follows.

The phenomenon of physical persistence and motion can be interpreted in two radically different ways. The one way is to regard it as something substantial and the other way is to regard it as, as it were, purely 'cinematographic'. That first, the substantial way is the mechanistic way of classical physics. According to this view, nature is not something we directly perceive. At best, our perceptions are never more than just accidental glimpses of the real goings-on, assumed to take place in an underlying continuum or substratum of cosmical space and time. The other, the 'cinematographic' – or, rather, information- processor – interpretation is that of relativistic quantum-physics which takes these 'observational glimpses' as the primary reality and regards all physical permanence and continuity as an illusion created by close-ordered 'cinematographic' sequences of pure observational events, events which have no permanence or motion in themselves but which simply occur. These occurrences are the quanta of quantum physics, where the quantum is Max Planck's fundamental, irreducible unit of action, h, the relation between any one quantum and any other being completely indeterminate in the manner described by Werner Heisenerg. In this case, instead of thinking of matter, space and time as forming an underlying continuum we think of those dimensions instead as relativistic 'video-projected' out of probabilistic, or information-statistical distributions of pure quantum-events in the way that the space of a TV action-drama is projected by the viewer out of informational patterns of dot-occurrences on a video-screen and which collapses into featureless flatness when the tuning goes wrong and those distributions of dots become random.

What worries classically-minded physicists about this quantum-relativistic, or information-based development in modern physics is, of course, that it seems to require us to turn our backs on what has so long been conceived as 'substantial reality'. That is to say, physics appears to lose its traditional standing as the objective study of 'permanent physical substance' and appears to become, instead, a subjective study of 'mere impressions' – demoting physics from its traditional position as the central science to become no more than a department of psychology. In order to avoid that seeming catastrophe, rather than contemplate this breach in historical continuity of their subject, many modern physicists strenuously seek to maintain some sort of compromise-position aimed at adapting all the new evidences of quantum relativity to the old and venerated mechanistic precepts, without which expedient many regard those new developments as 'inconceivable'. However, that is a mistake, since psychology has to do exclusively with the sorts of information-processors which we human observers represent, whereas the 'observers' of relativistic quantum-physics are not necessarily human beings but swappers of quantum information at any level, complex or rudimentary, direct or indirect, perceptual or instrumental, physical or psychological, in a many-dimensional communicational network of information the modern conception of which answers to what the Greeks referred to as logos – as opposed to the physis of their substantialist contemporaries.

So there is no danger of relativistic quantum-informational physics turning its back on physical reality nor, therefore, of losing its status as the central, most fundamental and most objective science. All that our efforts to hang on to the old precepts can achieve is to confuse the issue and prolong what should otherwise be a clean and natural transition from the old absolutistic, 'God's-eye-view' way of thinking to this new quantum-informational way. This, for me, puts an entirely different complexion on the issue. For no longer can we justifiably think of nature as the great Machine of the deists, in which our actions, like those of everything else, are the result of blind mechanical forces for which we have no moral nor any other kind of responsibility. In a relativistic, quantum-informational world, all that changes. In quantum physics, for instance, there can be no predetermined future. Nor, according to the relativistic arm of that same physics, is there any 'cosmical' extension of Greenwich Mean Time (GMT)' in which 'the universe' can be accounted for as a whole or by which it evolves as a whole – that is, everywhere in unison – or which can be accounted for as a whole, because all the various bits and bobs of which nature consists endure indeterminately and at different rates. The situation we now have to contemplate, therefore, is nothing like that envisaged by classical physics. In that classical view, everything takes place automatically and determinately. In the new view nothing takes place in that determinate mechanical way but only probabilistically according to laws, not of mechanics but of information-statistics.

The big difference, of course, from the point of view of the present discussion, is that this restores to commonsense the recognition of personal responsibility for our actions and therefore reinstates, at least in social if not religious contexts, the principle of morality with regard to human behaviour. No longer can we excuse ourselves by claiming that life, consciousness, volition and moral responsibility are illusions generated by underlying mechanisms of material particles. Even matter and space are now, in modern science, as much relativistic projections from observational information – as much 'in the eye of the beholder', in effect – as qualities like colour, beauty, goodness, justice, morality and so on. All these things, without exception are now seen as existing relatively to the observer in the same quantum-informational terms.

So at least we can eliminate deism, as Britannica defines it on counts 2 and 3 which are, just to remind us: 2 the belief that God created the world and set it in motion, subject to natural laws but takes no interest in it and, 3, the belief in a first cause which is not intrinsically perfect or complete, and therefore not a proper object of worship. (These two, incidentally, constitute the classical element of the 'Big Bang' view of modern cosmogony which flies in the face of both relativistic and quantum physics by assuming an absolutely determinate, cosmical GMT.) The remaining meaning of deism on count 1, namely, 'natural religion, the belief in the existence of a personal God based solely on the testimony of reason and rejecting any supernatural revelation' no longer conflicts with the theistic 'belief in a personal God as creator and supreme ruler of the universe, who transcends his creation but works in and through it in revealing himself to human individuals.' This is because 'the testimony of reason', applied to the workings of nature (no longer regarded as mechanical but as rational, nevertheless), may quite sensibly be seen as God's way of revealing himself – that is, in rational communication. This does no logically exclude the possibility of the 'supernatural revelation' that definition 1 mentions, except that such revelation (which scientists tacitly recognise in the form of rational 'inspiration', or the 'Eureka experience which so often anticipates logical and scientific reason) need no longer be regarded as 'supernatural'. It happens; none but the meanest-minded scientist would ever deny that!

All that our efforts to hang on to the old precepts can achieve is to confuse the issue and prolong what should otherwise be a clean and natural transition from the old argued that Jesus or Mahommet are to religion as Newton or Einstein are to physics. Indeed, it might be plausibly argued that all human ingenuity is that same 'divine inspiration' to some extent or other. In that case, modern science can have no legitimate quarrel with anyone who chooses to believe in a God who, through that agency of reason 'reveals himself to men.' Nor, it seems to me, can any truly religious person justifiably quarrel with that mention of reason, because since it no longer implies classical science's purely mechanistic mode of explanation it no longer implies deism, .

I conclude, then, that my purist approach to science-philosophy can, without any inconsistency whatsoever, be extended to include religion. As an empiricist, my method of approach to knowledge generally is based on the premise that although my logical reason can never apprise me of absolute truth it can, nevertheless, definitely apprise me, through the logical and mathematical law of contradiction, of what is false and, indeed, absolutely false. So, in a similar manner, in contexts of morality I can extend that to say, 'Although I have no idea what 'Right' is, I sure as hell know what is wrong! And the same goes for justice. I have no idea what absolute Justice is, but I sure as hell know what is unjust. That is true, even though these moral convictions may sometimes be more instinctual than rational. In some cases (and I can't be alone in this), my instinctive reaction against injustice, or evil is as imperative as my reaction against being suffocated. These things are as unquestionable to me, and to many others, surely, as life itself. (As someone has said, there is much beyond albeit never contrary to reason.)

However, in the same way that I cannot see how anyone can preconceive the ultimate end-aim of life or love or scientific curiosity, neither can I see how anyone can preconceive the ultimate object of moral and/or religious faith. For me, therefore, there has to be some form of religion, just as there has to be some form of science; and that religion has to be as open-ended in its ideas of God as a scientist's faith has to be in the order of nature. That is to say, in neither case, I would say, is the goal of understanding to be preconceived and approached dogmatically. It is something to be discovered and evaluated as we go along in the ordinary piecemeal, often risky, experimental and sometimes inspirational way we live our ordinary everyday lives – and/or pursue our inquiries in scientific laboratories and seminars. Some people, of course, will not want to settle for this long drawn-out piecemeal approach, which a normal life's span can never completely fulfill and the completion of which cannot be guaranteed even in the fullness of human history. This leads them to seek some quicker route to understanding by short-circuiting that gradual, rational procedure and relying on pure dogmatic faith in a 'salvation' (a personal fulfillment or 'survival' of the self) outside the ordinary historic process; that is to say, in realms other than those of ordinary space and time. And there are, as everyone knows, religious denominations that cater for that desire for psychological 'side-stepping' history. Western science, of course, ostensibly recognises no such 'other realms' and therefore rejects en bloc the denominational dogmas associated with them.

Yet there are things, even in science, that survive in realms outside that of ordinary physical process. Instances of these are geometrical relations like the ratio of the circumference to the radius of a circle, which we call pi, or the relation between the sides of a right-angled triangle, which we call 'Pythagoras's theorem'. These survive without any physical or historical attrition, and are 'there' whether or not there are any actual circles or triangles to which they may be applied. Nor does that 'survival' depend on whether or not they are retained in human memory.

The same may be said of all the various interrelations of numbers which we call 'mathematics'. The harmonic scales of music have a similar mathematical character – to the extent, indeed, that for the early, Pythagorean mathematicians, music and mathematics were indistinguishable. And if the integrity of a mathematical theorem or a tune can survive in that way, 'outside' the normal physical process, without there being any actual instances of them, then why not a logical truism, a piece of literature or even that intergrity of a human character we traditionally call a 'soul'? Is a soul any more the 'product' of our physiological processes, or is a Shakespearean sonet the 'product' of information-statistics than the 'laws of nature' are the 'product' of those processes they determine? Who is to say?

What is clear is that these sorts of questions are of the unanswerable 'Chicken-and-egg' type, which there is no point in asking. However, what is no less clear, and more to the point, is that the existence of this 'other realm' of mathematical, musical and other truths and relations, which the Greeks called logos cannot be eliminated from any true scientific enquiry without that science logically collapsing into nihilism. But neither can that logos be isolated from physics to become a 'realm apart', in way Plato and the Canon Law of mediæval Christianity imagined. As I see it, those two 'realms', the one of things and the other or the relations between things, have to be and remain fully integrated in the way Plato's friend and critic, Aristotle, taught. And for that reason, the traditional division that has developed between Western Science and Western Religion has to be entirely artificial. In my view, as soon as that is understood, Religion may cease to have to rely on dogma and join forces with Science in studying what both have traditionally conceived as 'God's handiwork.' In the meantime, I now reject all present partisan divisions or denominations of professed religion – including atheism – as now no more than vestiges of byegone intellectual tussles from which the present developments in empirical science and philosophy have now removed all but purely historical significance.
